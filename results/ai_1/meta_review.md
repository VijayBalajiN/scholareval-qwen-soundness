## Core formalization: We will formalize deception within the framework of partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs), modeling interactions between a speaker agent and a listener agent.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed speaker-listener communication POMDP is the explicit MDP treatment of deception as sequential decision-making to influence an adversary's beliefs and actions under behavioral constraints in [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5), which validates the core premise that deception can be formalized as policy selection in a sequential environment rather than as statement-level truth classification. The next strongest support is empirical and can be read as a newer extension of that older MDP framing: [(Aitchison et al., 2022-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/40da24aa836382577eee06de64652ff15d0012e4) shows that a Bayesian belief-manipulation module with an intrinsic deception bonus can change multi-agent game outcomes, with red players using alpha = 0.5 improving end-of-training score from -8.71 +/- 0.040 to -7.21 +/- 0.58 (95% CI) across four-agent groups, 16 games every ten epochs, and 128 games total, demonstrating that belief manipulation can be optimized as a reward signal in sequential decision making, although the setting is egocentric RGB and status-light game play rather than natural-language speaker-listener communication and it does not compute regret relative to a prior-only listener. A weaker but useful support is [(Zhang, and Zhu, 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6fa3757ffb04345109518f18e5cfb1231a873d89), which operationalizes deception as a POMDP observation kernel with p_k = 0.8 in Tiger and RockSample, using linear alpha-vector approximation and POMCP, and thereby supports measuring downstream decision damage from belief distortion, but it manipulates observations directly instead of modeling a speaker's message channel or prior/posterior regret. The weakest support is [(Tung et al., 2020-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ef3218533a8d1a6117683b75a7efe5c29c144888), whose headings indicate a POMDP-based dialog-management system with multimodal deception detection, user simulation, and human interaction, which is directionally relevant to Experiment 2, but the excerpt lacks state/action/reward definitions, metrics, and quantitative results, so it cannot substantively validate the proposed regret formalization.

### Contradictions

No related work directly refutes the POMDP speaker-listener formalization, but the strongest tension is empirical: the newer [(Aitchison et al., 2022-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/40da24aa836382577eee06de64652ff15d0012e4) result tempers the older theoretical framing in [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5) by showing that the deception incentive produced only a small score improvement, from -8.71 +/- 0.040 to -7.21 +/- 0.58 (95% CI), that the effect appeared only at the end of training, and that blue opponents learned a winning strategy more slowly but eventually converged to the same outcome; this suggests that a regret-based deception metric must account for opponent adaptation, equilibrium convergence, and possibly small effect sizes, rather than assuming that a single listener's prior-to-posterior regret will robustly identify deception. A second tension is scope: [(Zhang, and Zhu, 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6fa3757ffb04345109518f18e5cfb1231a873d89) models deception as a deceptive observation kernel that directly transforms observations o to o' in Tiger and RockSample, with p_k = 0.8 and distance-dependent Check-i correctness, which contradicts a narrow interpretation of deception as only speaker-generated messages and implies that the proposed communication POMDP may miss observation-level or environment-level belief manipulation unless such interventions are represented as speaker actions or baselines. A weaker tension is that [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5) frames deception through optimal control and behavioral constraints rather than through a regret comparison to a prior-only listener, so the proposed regret metric may need to incorporate constraints and policy optimality to avoid labeling constrained or truthful-but-incomplete behavior as deceptive. The weakest limitation is [(Tung et al., 2020-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ef3218533a8d1a6117683b75a7efe5c29c144888), which provides no quantitative outcomes, no POMDP definitions, and no human-rating correlation, so it neither supports nor contradicts the regret-based metric and leaves open whether POMDP dialog management can be evaluated against human deception intuitions.

### Suggested Action

Most importantly, the method should first validate the regret formalization on small POMDP benchmarks before human studies: implement the speaker-listener communication POMDP on Tiger and RockSample-style tasks inspired by [(Zhang, and Zhu, 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6fa3757ffb04345109518f18e5cfb1231a873d89), define prior-only listener policies, compute task regret, belief regret, and combined regret, and compare against truthful, no-communication, and deceptive-kernel baselines with p_k = 0.8. Second, incorporate the reward-based belief-manipulation evidence from [(Aitchison et al., 2022-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/40da24aa836382577eee06de64652ff15d0012e4) by training or evaluating speaker policies with intrinsic deception bonuses, using multiple random seeds, reporting 95% CIs and p-values, and measuring opponent adaptation over training epochs to ensure the metric captures stable rather than transient effects. Third, add constraint-aware policy evaluation following [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5), so that omissions, truthful-but-incomplete statements, and behaviorally constrained policies are distinguished from deceptive policies by regret relative to the listener's prior and by belief-accuracy rewards. Fourth, for the dialogue experiments, specify the POMDP state, action, observation, and reward structure and use a user simulator plus human evaluation as in [(Tung et al., 2020-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ef3218533a8d1a6117683b75a7efe5c29c144888), but with explicit metrics, sample sizes, power analysis, and correlation tests between human Likert ratings and regret components. Finally, report implementation details for LLM conversion, scenario generation, feature correlations, and baseline LLM comparisons (GPT-4, LLaMA, Google Bard) so that the human-study correlations are reproducible and not confounded by language generation artifacts.

## Regret-based deception metric: Our core innovation is measuring deception through regret: comparing the listener's expected reward after interacting with the speaker versus what they would have received acting on their prior beliefs alone.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed regret-based deception metric comes from [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5), which formalizes deception in finite MDPs as an agent policy that maximizes an adversary's utility loss or regret relative to the adversary's optimal expected reward under correct or prior beliefs, while imposing constraints on the agent's own behavior. This is the closest prior work to the proposed method because it already defines deception by the effect of one agent's actions on another agent's expected reward rather than by the literal truth of individual statements, and it explicitly connects deception to prior or correct beliefs, which maps directly to the research plan's prior-only listener baseline. The next strongest support is [(Alon et al., 2024-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a3b6fdb088db514a8c69e97156f48d4b4b48bd26), which uses an aleph-IPOMDP receiver in an Interactive Ultimatum Game to monitor sender behavior and protect receiver reward; its reward-monitoring component Z2, comparisons to the sucker payoff of 0.1, and random-sender expected reward of 0.5 show that interactive reward degradation is a meaningful signal of harmful sender behavior, even though the paper does not compute regret against a prior-only policy. [(Zhang, and Zhu, 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6fa3757ffb04345109518f18e5cfb1231a873d89) supports the POMDP framing by modeling deception as observation manipulation in Tiger and RockSample, using belief changes and downstream decision damage such as prolonged wandering, which aligns with the plan's belief-based and outcome-based deception components. [(Liu et al., 2021-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66b2314d65a87af50588d16579c3610e485a109a) supports the sequential observer-belief approach and the feasibility of human evaluation in path-planning deception, using P4 three-goal and five-goal maps, Berkeley Pacman, a naive intention-recognition observer, and 69 Amazon Mechanical Turk participants rating 480 map-path stimuli, although its metrics are goal probability, simulation, and path cost rather than regret. [(Chirra et al., 2024-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1ea08ff192dd2d9236f4f7c87826a717d5f3159e) provides weaker but relevant support for reward-based deception in MDPs by using Max Misinformation, an anti-reward linear program and primal-dual descent, constrained by a true-reward threshold, and evaluating an IRL observer's recovered reward in Cyber Security, Frozen Lake, Four Rooms, and random MDPs; this supports the idea that deception can be defined by the effect on another agent's reward inference, but it is less directly tied to listener regret. [(Tung et al., 2020-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ef3218533a8d1a6117683b75a7efe5c29c144888) is the weakest support because the excerpt only indicates a dialog system with multimodal deception detection and POMDP-based policy management, without algorithmic or empirical details.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction to the proposed method's assumption that a simple regret comparison will cleanly identify deception is [(Alon et al., 2024-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a3b6fdb088db514a8c69e97156f48d4b4b48bd26), which shows that reward-based deception signals in an IPOMDP are highly parameter-sensitive and can produce false positives: against a random sender, even lower delta and omega values reduced receiver reward from an expected 0.5 to about 0.4, while high values could nullify the interaction; against a high-threshold DoM(1) sender with psi=0.5, the mechanism often caused fast termination and receiver reward below the 0.1 sucker payoff, and only delta=0.1 and omega=0.1 produced receiver reward above that baseline. This implies that post-interaction reward can be degraded by monitoring, termination, or interaction costs even when the sender is not deceptively harmful, so the plan's regret metric must explicitly model communication and monitoring costs and report false-positive rates rather than treating any negative regret as deception. The next strongest contradiction concerns the plan's reliance on human ratings: [(Liu et al., 2021-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66b2314d65a87af50588d16579c3610e485a109a) found that human participants were less accurate than the naive algorithmic observer, accuracy at the 25% checkpoint was close to random, and because participants knew deception was possible, honest behavior was sometimes judged deceptive early, while deceptive models were judged deceptive through the 75% checkpoint; this warns that the proposed 50-participant, 10-scenario user studies may yield noisy correlations unless the design controls for suspicion, includes honest and no-communication baselines, and reports confidence intervals. [(Chirra et al., 2024-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1ea08ff192dd2d9236f4f7c87826a717d5f3159e) contradicts the sufficiency of listener regret by showing that deception can be defined and evaluated through an observer's recovered reward function, using Max Misinformation, MCE-IRL, IQ-learn, IRL random, and IRL max, with metrics such as Pearson correlation, EPIC distance, and expected reward of the recovered optimal policy; this suggests that a regret metric focused only on the listener's immediate expected reward may miss deception that manipulates the observer's inferred reward or privacy without producing a simple prior-only regret gap. [(Zhang, and Zhu, 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6fa3757ffb04345109518f18e5cfb1231a873d89) provides a softer contradiction by evaluating deceptive observation kernels through belief discrepancy and potential decision damage in Tiger and RockSample rather than regret relative to a prior-only baseline, indicating that belief manipulation and downstream damage may need separate metrics, especially when the listener's action policy does not convert belief error into reward loss. [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5) does not contradict the core idea but limits its empirical support because it is theoretical and reports no quantitative regret values, baselines, or statistical significance, so the proposed method cannot claim that regret-based deception has already been validated in interactive settings. [(Tung et al., 2020-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ef3218533a8d1a6117683b75a7efe5c29c144888) offers no substantive contradiction because the excerpt lacks method, data, and results.

### Suggested Action

Most important, the method should operationalize the prior-only baseline as a well-specified counterfactual POMDP value: define the listener's prior belief, the no-communication or prior-only policy, the same action constraints, communication cost, and reward functions, including task reward, belief-accuracy reward, and combined reward, then compute expected reward using value iteration, POMCP, or another solver with reported horizon, discount, and convergence criteria; this directly addresses the confound identified in [(Alon et al., 2024-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a3b6fdb088db514a8c69e97156f48d4b4b48bd26), where reward degradation can arise from monitoring parameters delta and omega, false positives, and termination rather than deception. Second, redesign the human evaluation to reduce the suspicion and noise effects documented in [(Liu et al., 2021-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66b2314d65a87af50588d16579c3610e485a109a): include honest, random, no-communication, and deceptive speaker baselines; avoid telling participants that deception is possible unless the research question is explicit; collect confidence ratings, multiple ratings per stimulus, and manipulation checks; use mixed-effects or Bayesian models with participant and scenario random effects, report correlation coefficients with confidence intervals and p-values, and pre-register the analysis. Third, broaden the evaluation beyond a single regret score by adding belief regret, task regret, combined regret, false-positive rate, path or interaction cost, and observer-inference metrics inspired by [(Chirra et al., 2024-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1ea08ff192dd2d9236f4f7c87826a717d5f3159e), such as IRL reward-recovery error, and by [(Zhang, and Zhu, 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6fa3757ffb04345109518f18e5cfb1231a873d89), such as belief discrepancy in Tiger and RockSample; this will show whether regret subsumes existing notions or merely correlates with them. Fourth, validate the formalism in small POMDP and MDP testbeds before LLM dialogues, using domains with known optimal policies and constrained speaker policies as in [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5) and [(Liu et al., 2021-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66b2314d65a87af50588d16579c3610e485a109a), so that the regret metric can be checked against ground-truth optimal listener rewards. Fifth, specify implementation and reproducibility details missing from the related work: LLM versions, prompts, seeds, number of generated dialogues, hardware, hyperparameters, and statistical power for the 50-, 30-, and 30-participant studies, and compare against LLM judges such as GPT-4, LLaMA, and Google Bard with the same correlation and significance tests. Finally, incorporate speaker constraints and omission baselines to avoid trivial deception, as emphasized by [(Ornik, and Topcu, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a250728df2774905652e62325bcb259be5c787c5), and test the metric on technically true but misleading, omission, and prior-updating cases highlighted in the research plan.

## Conversational scenario design: We will design three realistic scenarios where deception commonly occurs: house bargaining (seller-buyer), nutrition consultation (nutritionist-patient), and workplace small talk (colleagues).

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed conversational scenario design comes from [(Chou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9e8c54f4bd1ad434f3ebde280e242a18c14a53c3), which uses the DDDM Mandarin conversational deception corpus with about 27.2 hours of audio, 96 unique speakers, 283 question-level samples, 7,126 utterances converted to 2,764 question-answering turns, and human judgments from both interlocutors. This supports the plan to build realistic conversational scenarios with human judgments, because it shows that conversational deception can be studied through turn-level dialogue features, dyad-independent 10-fold cross-validation, macro F1, PyTorch implementations, and zero-padded sequences to a maximum timestamp of 40, and because interrogator judgments improved BLSTM-DNN macro F1 over models without those judgments, especially when interrogators distrusted deceivers. A weaker but still relevant support is [(Kuzio, 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f214aebf2dc4aa3fc3c6128949a26b07ec5e6ad0), which used 100 volunteers, 41 male and 59 female, average age 25.0 years, in a 2 x 2 x 2 online chat deception experiment and collected 787 honesty judgments on a seven-point scale with an undecided midpoint. This supports the proposed user-study component in which participants rate deceptiveness after seeing speaker features, listener priors, revealed features, and listener goals, because it demonstrates that humans can provide graded deception judgments in conversational exchanges and that an undecided option can capture uncertainty.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Kuzio, 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f214aebf2dc4aa3fc3c6128949a26b07ec5e6ad0) found only marginal evidence that human deception judgments in conversational chat exceed chance: overall accuracy was 0.542 across 637 confident judgments, with Z = 1.923 and p reported as less than .0554 but not below the conventional .05 threshold, and power was only about .68 for a small effect. This challenges the proposed assumption that 50 participants rating 10 scenarios per situation will produce stable, statistically reliable correlations with regret-based metrics, especially if the scenarios are novel and the ratings are forced onto a 1-5 Likert scale without an explicit uncertainty option. A weaker but important contradiction is that [(Chou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9e8c54f4bd1ad434f3ebde280e242a18c14a53c3) does not validate the proposed POMDP, regret, LLM-generated, or binary-feature scenario design. It is a supervised detection study on Mandarin interrogator-deceiver data with 283 question-level samples, conditions of 283, 183, and 100 samples, dyad-independent 10-fold cross-validation, macro F1, human performance of 54.7 percent macro F1, and classifiers such as SVM, random forest, logistic regression, DNN, LSTM-DNN, and BLSTM-DNN with attention, but it does not model listener priors, actions, utilities, task regret, belief regret, or combined regret, and it does not use house bargaining, nutrition consultation, or workplace small talk scenarios.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to strengthen the human-rating protocol by adding an explicit undecided or uncertainty option and by conducting a power analysis before the 50-participant study, because [(Kuzio, 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f214aebf2dc4aa3fc3c6128949a26b07ec5e6ad0) showed that conversational deception judgments can be near chance and underpowered, with overall accuracy 0.542, Z = 1.923, p less than .0554, and power about .68. The proposed 1-5 Likert scale should therefore be supplemented with a confidence or undecided response, and the study should pre-register effect-size targets, sample size, and correlation thresholds. The next most important action is to incorporate interlocutor judgment and trust/distrust structure into the three scenarios, inspired by [(Chou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9e8c54f4bd1ad434f3ebde280e242a18c14a53c3), where interrogator judgments improved BLSTM-DNN macro F1 and were especially contributive when interrogators distrusted deceivers. The house bargaining, nutrition consultation, and workplace small talk scenarios should therefore include explicit listener prior beliefs, revealed features, listener goals, and trust/distrust conditions, and evaluation should use dyad-independent or participant-independent cross-validation to avoid leakage. A less urgent but useful action is to use DDDM or similar real conversational deception corpora as an external validation set or baseline, while keeping the proposed English scenarios and LLM-generated dialogues as the primary testbed, and to report macro F1, correlation coefficients, p-values, and effect sizes for both detection and human-alignment analyses.

## Scenario generation and dialogue conversion: We will programmatically generate conversation scenarios and use an LLM to convert symbolic POMDP actions into natural language dialogue.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed scenario-generation and dialogue-conversion method comes from [(Hagendorff, 2023-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/aa5993501f4d0036d310e0292dea20e2c60b6c8f), which demonstrates that LLM-assisted scenario generation can produce deception-relevant stimuli at scale while preserving a controlled underlying task structure. In that work, researchers manually designed eight raw binary tasks with placeholders for agents, objects, and places, then used GPT-4 to generate 120 semantically varied variants for each raw task, yielding 960 base scenarios and 1,920 option-permuted task instances after permutation. This is directly relevant to the proposed Experiment 1, where three realistic situations with three binary features must be expanded into enough conversation scenarios to support 50 participants receiving 10 random scenarios per situation, for 1,500 total interactions. The related work shows that a template-based, LLM-expanded generation pipeline can plausibly produce the volume of semantically varied but structurally consistent items needed for such a user study. A weaker but still relevant form of support is the related work’s attention to experimental control: temperature was set to 0 or 0.0001, option order was permuted to reduce positional or recency heuristics, and generated items were manually checked with low-quality items replaced. These practices support the feasibility of making LLM-generated deception scenarios reproducible and less susceptible to surface-form artifacts, which is important if the proposed plan relies on an LLM to convert symbolic POMDP actions into natural language dialogue. However, this support is limited because the related work generated single-turn binary-choice tasks rather than multi-turn conversational scenarios, did not use POMDPs, and did not convert symbolic decision-theoretic actions into dialogue.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction, or at least the strongest tension, is that [(Hagendorff, 2023-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/aa5993501f4d0036d310e0292dea20e2c60b6c8f) does not provide evidence that an LLM can faithfully convert symbolic POMDP actions into natural language dialogue while preserving the decision-theoretic variables required by the proposed method. The related work generated 1,920 static task instances from eight raw binary tasks and administered them to 10 LLMs, including GPT-family models, BLOOM, and FLAN-T5, but it did not model sequential belief updates, speaker-listener communication, listener utilities, or regret-based deception. This contradicts the implicit assumption that LLM-generated deception scenarios from static binary tasks can be directly extrapolated to conversational POMDP settings where the meaning of a message depends on hidden state, revealed features, listener priors, and downstream actions. A second contradiction is that the supplied excerpt reports no quantitative experimental outcomes: no accuracy rates, deception rates, baseline comparisons, correlation values, or statistical significance results are available. Therefore, the related work cannot support the proposed plan’s central evaluation claim that human 1-5 Likert deception ratings will correlate with task regret, belief regret, or combined regret. A weaker contradiction is that the related work used GPT-4 both to generate scenarios and to classify deceptive or non-deceptive responses, with manual double-checking by hypothesis-blind research assistants. This suggests that LLM-assisted generation and evaluation may introduce model-specific biases, atypical responses, or classification errors, which means the proposed plan should not assume that LLM dialogue conversion alone will produce valid deception stimuli without independent validation against the intended POMDP structure.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to add an explicit validation layer for the LLM dialogue-conversion step: after converting each symbolic POMDP action sequence into natural language dialogue, the system should back-translate or otherwise verify that the dialogue preserves the intended speaker actions, revealed features, hidden true features, listener prior beliefs, and listener-relevant features. Items where the dialogue introduces unintended information, omits required features, or changes the decision-theoretic structure should be discarded or regenerated, because the proposed regret metrics must be computed on the intended POMDP actions rather than on LLM paraphrase artifacts. This is especially critical for Experiment 1, where 1,500 participant ratings depend on participants correctly interpreting which features were revealed and which features the listener cares about. Next, the proposed method should adopt the template-and-variant strategy demonstrated in [(Hagendorff, 2023-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/aa5993501f4d0036d310e0292dea20e2c60b6c8f): define raw conversation templates with placeholders for agents, objects, places, and binary features; generate multiple surface variants per template using a strong LLM; manually check low-quality outputs; and permute utterance or option order to reduce positional and recency effects. Finally, the plan should implement reproducibility controls analogous to the related work, including fixed low temperature such as 0 or 0.0001, explicit system prompts, logging of prompts, model versions, and generation parameters, and a pilot study with a subset of the 50 participants to verify that the generated dialogues are interpretable and that the intended POMDP features are recoverable before full data collection.

## Interactive dialogue action selection: The system will use an LLM to convert model actions into natural language, randomly selecting actions that either maximize or minimize deceptive regret.

### Support

The strongest support comes from [(Gasic et al., 2010-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/04213b2da00939e9fb72efcd8b304c18b20b6f4a), which extends the earlier POMDP dialogue-management line of [(Zhang et al., 2001-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ba659c62e93021a54500fd20089fe4c0d2060fb) by replacing handcrafted value iteration and 2TBN observation models with GP-Sarsa over continuous belief states, epsilon-greedy exploration, and uncertainty-based active learning, and by providing quantitative simulated-user evaluation with 0.3 user error probability, 10-turn toy dialogues, +10/-100 terminal rewards, epsilon 0.1, 1000 random seeds, evaluation every 20 training dialogues on 1000 test dialogues, and a grid distance of 0.01 for the Monte Carlo Control baseline; its finding that the scaled norm kernel reached near-optimal performance in about 400 dialogues and that active learning accelerated learning supports the proposed Experiment 2 idea of using a POMDP-like dialogue manager and comparing action-selection policies under controlled simulated interactions. Weaker but still relevant support is [(Zhang et al., 2001-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ba659c62e93021a54500fd20089fe4c0d2060fb), which establishes POMDP dialogue management with belief states, value iteration, piecewise linear convex value functions, MDP approximation, grid-based linear function updates, 25 observations, and 11 handcrafted 2TBNs for observation probabilities, demonstrating that symbolic dialogue actions can be selected from abstracted speech, keyboard, and mouse signals; however, its outcomes are qualitative and it does not provide numerical accuracy, baseline comparison, statistical significance, or human deception ratings, so it supports the POMDP action-selection architecture more than the specific deceptive-regret or LLM surface-realization components.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that neither related work provides direct evidence that randomly selecting actions that maximize or minimize deceptive regret will produce human-perceivable deception or align with human ratings. As the newer work, [(Gasic et al., 2010-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/04213b2da00939e9fb72efcd8b304c18b20b6f4a) evaluates GP-Sarsa and active learning against grid-based Monte Carlo Control using task-success rewards, not deceptive regret, and although it shows faster learning in the first 400 dialogues, it also reports that after many iterations under incremental noise training GP-Sarsa and the grid baseline converged to the same performance, which contradicts the assumption that a POMDP action-selection rule alone will produce a stable, distinguishable max/min deceptive-regret behavior unless the reward explicitly encodes deception. A second contradiction is that the proposed method relies on an LLM to convert model actions into natural language, but neither paper uses an LLM; [(Zhang et al., 2001-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ba659c62e93021a54500fd20089fe4c0d2060fb) uses handcrafted Bayesian networks and 2TBNs to map low-level signals to 25 abstract observations, and [(Gasic et al., 2010-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/04213b2da00939e9fb72efcd8b304c18b20b6f4a) operates at the dialogue-act or belief-state level without natural-language generation, so there is no evidence that LLM surface realization will preserve the intended max/min regret condition or that linguistic style will not confound deception ratings. A weaker but important contradiction is that the evidence base is limited in scale and evaluation: Zhang et al. report only qualitative signal-level outcomes in four situations and no participant size, hardware, hyperparameters, or metrics, while Gasic et al. use simulated users rather than the 30 human participants planned in Experiment 2, and their metrics are task reward and learning speed rather than deception ratings, correlation coefficients, or p-values.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to make the action-selection procedure explicit and controlled rather than merely random: for each candidate dialogue action, compute the proposed deceptive regret using the communication POMDP, then assign the system to a maximize-regret or minimize-regret condition by selecting the argmax or argmin action, with randomization only for participant assignment, initial belief states, or tie-breaking, so that the experimental contrast directly tests the regret objective. Second, adopt the evaluation protocol and learning algorithms from [(Gasic et al., 2010-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/04213b2da00939e9fb72efcd8b304c18b20b6f4a), using GP-Sarsa or value iteration over belief states, simulated users with specified error probabilities, turn limits, terminal and intermediate rewards, multiple random seeds, periodic evaluation on held-out test dialogues, and a grid-based Monte Carlo Control or handcrafted policy baseline, while replacing task-success reward with a deceptive-regret reward or loss and reporting learning curves, regret values, and statistical significance. Third, extend the observation and belief model from the limited 25-observation, 11-2TBN setup in [(Zhang et al., 2001-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ba659c62e93021a54500fd20089fe4c0d2060fb) to the housing scenario's eight correlated features, using learned or validated observation probabilities rather than only handcrafted TBNs, and log the symbolic action, belief state, revealed features, and regret value before LLM conversion so that the LLM can be treated as a surface-realization layer rather than an uncontrolled source of deception. Finally, add a pilot evaluation with simulated users and a small human subset to verify that max-regret and min-regret conditions produce distinguishable dialogue behavior and that human ratings correlate with regret, before scaling to the planned 30-participant study.

## Negotiation user study: A user study with 30 participants will provide human deception ratings for correlation analysis.

### Support

The strongest support comes from [(Mell et al., 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/32d2236cc0394c2379828cb4de2a7998b8b197f0), which directly implements a negotiation user study with human deception ratings: 190 US Mechanical Turk participants completed a 10-minute multi-issue bargaining task on IAGO with four automated agents crossed on tough/fair deeds and nice/nasty words, using 20 items with private point values and a BATNA, and completed the ANTI deception scale before and after negotiation; a 2 x 2 x 2 mixed ANOVA found a significant toughness-by-time interaction, F(1, 186) = 5.81, p = .02, with endorsement increasing after tough agents from pre M = 4.10, SE = 0.12 to post M = 4.32, SE = 0.15, p = .16, and decreasing after fair agents from pre M = 4.30, SE = 0.15 to post M = 3.99, SE = 0.17, p = .03, while agent toughness also affected negotiation points, tough M = 36.56, SE = 0.31 versus fair M = 32.04, SE = 0.36, F(1, 186) = 90.67, p < .001. This supports the proposed 30-participant negotiation user study as a feasible way to collect human deception ratings in an interactive bargaining setting, although it does not validate a regret-based metric. As an iterative extension, [(Lin et al., 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/77d956cdab4508d569ae5741549b78e715fd0749) supports the broader plan of correlating human ratings with automated metrics by showing that human true/false and informative/not informative ratings can be approximated by a fine-tuned 6.7B GPT-3 judge with 90-96 percent validation accuracy on truthfulness, 90 percent accuracy on UnifiedQA answers, 89.5 percent accuracy on a held-out human baseline, and 86.3 percent accuracy for informativeness, using greedy decoding with temperature set to zero; this is relevant to the plan's LLM baseline comparisons but is limited to static general-knowledge truthfulness rather than negotiation deception. Next, [(Cai et al., 2024-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8c291f3f31325720a05dc76aa4930fab50d5d127) supports controlled collection of human deception judgments at scale: 193 native Chinese speakers completed a 24-question interview with nine deceptive and fifteen truthful questions, up to 15 minutes of preparation, interviewer judgment scales, subject lie confidence scales, and studio recording with a GoPro Hero9 at 1920x1080 and 60 fps, producing 1,737 deceptive and 2,895 truthful responses, 1,808 minutes of deceptive video, and 4,401 minutes of emotional video, with repeated five-time training/validation splits using 19 training and 5 validation answers; this demonstrates that human deception ratings can be operationalized in a controlled experiment, though not in LLM-generated negotiation or regret analysis. The weakest support is [(Appling, and Briscoe, 2016-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7f97d11f708de9aef7e3dde3c12e5aa329f9e58e), an earlier controlled deception experiment with 75 Georgia Tech students using the FaceFriend mock social network for a 12-minute subarctic survival conversation with a confederate, where participants self-identified deceptive statements and assigned strategy categories; it supports the general idea of collecting human deception judgments in interactive decision-making, but it reports no quantitative outcomes, counts, frequencies, correlations, baselines, or statistical significance, so it provides only weak methodological support for the proposed correlation analysis.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that none of the related work directly validates the proposed regret-based deception metric, and the closest negotiation evidence, [(Mell et al., 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/32d2236cc0394c2379828cb4de2a7998b8b197f0), shows that human deception endorsement can shift because of agent toughness rather than actual lying or regret; tough agents earned more points than fair agents, M = 36.56, SE = 0.31 versus M = 32.04, SE = 0.36, F(1, 186) = 90.67, p < .001, and users earned fewer points against tough agents, M = 24.73, SE = 0.48 versus M = 29.98, SE = 0.56, F(1, 186) = 50.25, p < .001, while agents did not lie or withhold information; this warns that the proposed 30-participant ratings may capture perceived competitiveness, strategic pressure, or agent style rather than task regret, belief regret, or combined regret. It also contrasts with the proposed sample size: Mell used 190 participants after attention-check filtering and group-level ANOVA, whereas 30 participants is likely underpowered for item-level correlations across 30 conversations, multiple regret metrics, and LLM baselines. A second contradiction comes from [(Cai et al., 2024-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8c291f3f31325720a05dc76aa4930fab50d5d127), which shows that human deception judgments are confounded by individual differences and expressive context: deception success was associated with low extraversion, high agreeableness, low conscientiousness, and heightened emotional expressivity, while high/low neuroticism and openness were not significantly different; the ratings were interviewer judgments of live interview responses and subject lie confidence, not participant ratings of LLM-generated negotiation dialogues, and no correlation with a formal deception metric was reported, so the proposed method must control for personality, emotion, and task context. Third, [(Lin et al., 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/77d956cdab4508d569ae5741549b78e715fd0749) limits transferability: its human ratings concern static truthfulness and informativeness of generated answers, not deception in sequential decision-making, and its GPT-judge accuracies of 90-96 percent, 90 percent, 89.5 percent, and 86.3 percent do not establish that LLM judges or 30 human ratings can predict negotiation regret; it provides no deception-specific or negotiation-specific correlation coefficients. The weakest contradiction is [(Appling, and Briscoe, 2016-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7f97d11f708de9aef7e3dde3c12e5aa329f9e58e), because the provided method lacks reported quantitative outcomes, strategy frequencies, baseline comparisons, or statistical significance, and its reliance on self-identified deceptive statements may introduce recall or self-report bias; therefore it cannot support the proposed statistical correlation design or the use of Likert-scale deception ratings aligned with a formal regret metric.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to make the 30-participant negotiation user study statistically adequate and explicitly tied to the regret formalism: if the study uses 30 LLM-generated Deal or No Deal conversations, recruit more than 30 participants or ensure each conversation receives enough independent ratings, run a pilot to estimate rating variance, and pre-register a mixed-effects or Bayesian hierarchical model with participant and conversation random effects, multiple-comparison correction, effect sizes, confidence intervals, and p-values for correlations with task regret, belief regret, and combined regret. Second, use a 1-5 Likert deception rating consistent with Experiment 1, add manipulation checks, attention checks, demographic and personality measures, and record the true item values, Agent 1 knowledge, Agent 2 prior beliefs, posterior beliefs, speaker actions, BATNA, and final allocation for every conversation so that human ratings can be tested against the specific regret components rather than generic agent toughness, as warned by [(Mell et al., 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/32d2236cc0394c2379828cb4de2a7998b8b197f0). Third, validate the LLM-generated dialogues and chain-of-thought belief extraction by having annotators verify that extracted priors, posteriors, and actions match the dialogue, report inter-annotator agreement, and fix model versions, decoding settings, temperature, max tokens, and prompt templates to improve reproducibility. Fourth, include the planned LLM baselines, GPT-4, LLaMA, and Google Bard, and if feasible train or prompt a GPT-judge inspired by [(Lin et al., 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/77d956cdab4508d569ae5741549b78e715fd0749) to predict human deception ratings, reporting accuracy and correlation against the human ratings. Fifth, report implementation details that are missing in the weaker related work, including number of conversations rated per participant, rating order, compensation, platform, time limits, and statistical tests, to avoid the underreported analysis seen in [(Appling, and Briscoe, 2016-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7f97d11f708de9aef7e3dde3c12e5aa329f9e58e) and the group-level ANOVA limitation in [(Mell et al., 2020-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/32d2236cc0394c2379828cb4de2a7998b8b197f0), while also controlling for personality and emotion confounds highlighted by [(Cai et al., 2024-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8c291f3f31325720a05dc76aa4930fab50d5d127).

## Baseline comparison: We will also compare our approach against baseline evaluations from state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-4, LLaMA, Google Bard) to demonstrate that our formalism captures deception better than existing approaches.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed baseline comparison comes from newer [(Hazra, and Majumder, 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2a3f459e09c1e0faff4628d79b43470a129be565), which extends earlier LLM deception evaluations by adding a 150-session, 86,746-word, 1,546-utterance deception-detection benchmark with 450 unique contestants, 56 recurring judges, 12-15 question-answer pairs per session, Whisper transcripts with 8.81% word error rate versus 7.61% human transcription, anonymization, and contestant identity swapping. It directly evaluates GPT-4, GPT-3.5, GPT-3, Llama-2, and Bard under zero-shot, two-shot, chain-of-thought, and bottleneck prompting, and reports accuracy, accuracy@2, human pairwise preference, and Fleiss kappa (0.68-0.91). This supports the plan's choice of GPT-4, LLaMA/Llama-2, and Bard as LLM baselines and reinforces that any claim that a formal regret metric captures deception better than existing approaches must be tested against strong LLM evaluators and human judgments on the same scenarios. A weaker but still relevant support is contemporaneous [(Singh et al., 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9045649163477319dafba4403dc915c3388dceda), which builds on scenario-based LLM manipulation reviews by using GPT-4 and Google Bard on deception/manipulation prompt scenarios organized by persuasion principles (manipulation/misinformation, authority, trust/social proof, lack of details, pronoun avoidance) and recording Yes/No exploitation/compliance labels; this supports including GPT-4 and Bard in scenario-based deception baselines, although it does not use LLaMA or a formal deception metric. The weakest support is earlier [(Hagendorff, 2023-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/aa5993501f4d0036d310e0292dea20e2c60b6c8f), which established controlled false-belief and deception tasks with 8 raw tasks, 120 GPT-4-generated variants each, 1,920 permuted binary-option tasks, temperature 0/0.0001, GPT-4 automatic classification, and manual double-checking across 10 LLMs; this supports controlled LLM deception evaluation and bias mitigation, but it does not include LLaMA or Bard and provides no quantitative outcomes in the supplied excerpt, so it only indirectly supports the proposed baseline comparison.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is from newer [(Hazra, and Majumder, 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2a3f459e09c1e0faff4628d79b43470a129be565), which, by providing quantitative benchmark results, shows that the earlier qualitative or task-based LLM deception evaluations are insufficient for validating a new formalism: even the best GPT-4 bottleneck model achieved only 39.3% accuracy and 77.3% accuracy@2, compared with human macro-average accuracy of 41.3% and random 33.3%/66.6%, while GPT-4 two-shot reached 34.7%/72.0% and GPT-4 chain-of-thought reached 32.0%/64.7%. This contradicts the proposed method's assumption that comparing against GPT-4, LLaMA, and Bard will straightforwardly demonstrate that the POMDP regret formalism captures deception better, because the related work shows LLM baselines require careful benchmarking, human comparison, and statistical reporting on a limited 150-session dataset. A second contradiction is from [(Singh et al., 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9045649163477319dafba4403dc915c3388dceda), which provides only qualitative/semi-quantitative Yes/No judgments for BARD and GPT-4, with no LLaMA, no sample sizes, no randomization, no p-values, and no sequential belief-update or regret metric; this contradicts the proposed method's need for a rigorous baseline comparison that can support a claim of superior deception capture. The weakest contradiction is from earlier [(Hagendorff, 2023-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/aa5993501f4d0036d310e0292dea20e2c60b6c8f), which lacks reported quantitative outcomes, does not include LLaMA or Bard, and evaluates crafted false-belief/deception tasks rather than the proposed POMDP regret-based deception formalism or human ratings; therefore it cannot substantiate the proposed baseline comparison beyond showing that LLM deception tasks can be constructed.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to specify the baseline comparison as a controlled evaluation on the same generated scenarios used in Experiments 1-3, where GPT-4, a clearly specified LLaMA/Llama-2 version, and Google Bard receive the same inputs (true speaker features, listener prior beliefs, revealed features, listener preferences, and dialogue context) and are prompted to output a deception rating or binary/ordinal label comparable to the 1-5 human Likert scale. Second, the method should define the statistical comparison explicitly: convert LLM outputs to a common scale, compute correlation coefficients (e.g., Spearman/Pearson) between LLM ratings and human ratings, compare these correlations to those for task regret, belief regret, and combined regret, and report p-values, confidence intervals, and multiple-comparison corrections. Third, following [(Hazra, and Majumder, 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2a3f459e09c1e0faff4628d79b43470a129be565), include human and random/chance baselines and report accuracy, accuracy@2, or AUC where applicable, because GPT-4 bottleneck performance was only 39.3% accuracy versus 41.3% human and 33.3% random, so LLM superiority must be demonstrated against both human and chance performance; also adopt its benchmark hygiene, such as anonymization, identity swapping, and leave-one-out or cross-validation controls where supervised baselines are used. Fourth, adopt the controlled task-generation and bias-reduction practices from [(Hagendorff, 2023-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/aa5993501f4d0036d310e0292dea20e2c60b6c8f), such as permuted answer options, fixed temperature (0 or 0.0001), explicit system prompts, and manual or automatic response classification with double-checking, and use the scenario categories from [(Singh et al., 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9045649163477319dafba4403dc915c3388dceda) to ensure the baseline prompts cover manipulation, misinformation, authority, trust/social proof, lack of details, and pronoun avoidance. Finally, report implementation details necessary for reproducibility: exact model versions/APIs, prompt templates, zero-shot/two-shot/chain-of-thought/bottleneck variants, sample sizes (1,500 interactions in Experiment 1, 30 participants in Experiments 2-3), hardware or API constraints, and statistical tests.

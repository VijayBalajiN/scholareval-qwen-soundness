## Context: The plan needs a predictor that combines instance and LLM information. Method: We will train a classifier that takes as input both instance-intrinsic features (such as text embeddings) and LLM-specific performance vectors on reference instances. The assessor will predict binary correctness scores for LLM-instance pairs.

### Support

The strongest support is that [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) specifies the same generic assessor design as the proposed method, namely a classifier h_phi over prompt-intrinsic features f(p_i) and LLM-specific binary correctness vectors g(m_j) on a small reference set D_ref, with a new LLM evaluated only on D_ref before predicting unseen instances. It also matches the proposed datasets and splits: HELM-Lite reduced to 6 binary-metric scenarios with 4,285 instances and 30 LLMs, and KindsOfReasoning with 22 reasoning datasets, 37,529 instances, and 14 OpenAI instruction-tuned models from text-ada-001 to gpt-4-0125-preview, including random and out-of-distribution instance splits and test LLMs from different producers or GPT-4 versions. This directly supports the proposed reference-vector plus instance-feature classifier, although the excerpt does not report AUC, reference-set size, classifier architecture, or hyperparameters. The next strongest support is [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2), which supports the proposed IRT-based reference-instance selection and small-evaluation characterization of LLMs by fitting item parameters from binary correctness on a training set of LLMs, estimating held-out LLM ability by maximizing IRT log-likelihood, and using p-IRT and gp-IRT estimators with a default variance adjustment factor of 4 for anchor-based sampling. Its random and date-based LLM split scenarios also support the proposed OOD and model-shift evaluation, although it targets scenario-level ability rather than instance-level binary correctness. Next, [(Ailem et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f187697a28fdc8d6d44dc3c1ea3a65ed85449c42) supports the proposed combination of prompt embeddings and LLM performance vectors by analyzing prompt-by-LLM matrices for ANLI with 1,200 test prompts, HellaSwag with 1,005, CommonsenseQA with 1,221, and CNN/Daily Mail with 1,150, using 8 to 14 LLMs, OpenAI ada-2 embeddings, cosine, Jaccard, or Hamming performance similarities, per-prompt regression, FDR adjustment, 1,000 permutations, and KS tests. It finds significant semantic-performance alignment for CNN/Daily Mail and for the ANLI reason component, showing that the two proposed feature families can contain related signal. Next, [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a) supports the feasibility of a lightweight cross-LLM binary correctness classifier and the use of tree-based models, reporting a Random Forest with 100 trees, minimum node size 5, and 50 percent feature subsampling trained on 194k instances and evaluated on a 38k order-matched test partition, with aggregated AUROC 0.929 and Brier score 0.110, compared with GPT-3 self-assessment AUROC 0.916 and Brier score 0.135, and a 1 percent reject threshold that rejects 21.7 percent of instances while catching 46 percent of failures and only 1.5 percent of correct answers. The experiments use GPT-3 Ada, Babbage, Curie, and Davinci at 0 to 10 shots and BIG-G dense and sparse models from 2M to 128B parameters at 0 to 3 shots, all at temperature 0 and without task fine-tuning, and the reported model is approximately 100 trees with about 20,000 nodes, implying roughly 1,450 comparisons per inference. This supports the proposed AUC-centric evaluation, binary correctness target, and cheap classifier, although it uses system metadata rather than reference-instance performance vectors or free-form text. Next, [(Shah et al., 2019-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/735a63b58349e07b84c2e31927ce1b1cfaf09980) supports binary failure prediction for model-instance pairs by training a separate predictor on frozen base-model representations in VQA v2, with 443,757 training, 214,354 validation, and 447,793 test questions, and reports qualitative improvements for BUTD and Pythia under cycle-consistent training. Next, [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) supports the proposed text-embedding and n-gram feature families by showing that character n-gram subword vectors with 300 dimensions, 5 negative samples, context window 1 to 5, subsampling threshold 1e-4, minimum word count 5, and learning rate 0.05 can outperform word-level baselines, with German GUR350 Spearman 66 at 5 percent data versus 62 for full-data CBOW and English RW 45 at 1 percent data versus 43 for full-data CBOW, and that 3 to 6 character n-grams are a reasonable default, while also reporting a processing speed of 105k words per second per thread versus 145k for the baseline. Next, [(Papineni et al., 2002-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d7da009f457917aa381619facfa5ffae9329a6e9) supports n-gram statistics as compact quality signals, with modified n-gram precision separating human from machine translations on a 500-sentence corpus, 20 block-wise 25-sentence evaluations with paired t-statistics at or above 1.7, an example t of 6, and linear regression correlations of 0.99 and 0.96 with human scores. Finally, [(Gardner et al., 2020-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9514630194a9fba9505b594ec921b247fecb48) supports the use of small instance sets to characterize model behavior, with contrast sets across 10 NLP datasets, up to about 1,000 total examples, averaging 1 to 5 examples per contrast set, construction costs of 1 to 3 minutes per example for most datasets and about 15 minutes per example for dependency parsing, and category-specific results such as MATRES 66.5 percent and 60.0 percent, DROP 47.3 and 67.5 F1, and English UD contrast consistency 17.3.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Ailem et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f187697a28fdc8d6d44dc3c1ea3a65ed85449c42) finds a significant relationship between prompt semantic similarity and LLM performance similarity only for CNN/Daily Mail, with the ANLI input prompt components not significant and HellaSwag and CommonsenseQA showing no strong evidence, despite using 1,200 ANLI prompts, 1,005 HellaSwag prompts, 1,221 CommonsenseQA prompts, 1,150 CNN/Daily Mail prompts, 8 to 14 LLMs, OpenAI ada-2 embeddings, per-prompt regression, FDR adjustment, 1,000 permutations, and KS tests, and without reporting numeric regression coefficients, effect sizes, or AUC. This contradicts the broad assumption that text embeddings and LLM performance vectors are generally complementary for predicting instance-level correctness, and it implies that the proposed assessor must test feature complementarity benchmark by benchmark rather than assume it. The next strongest contradiction is that [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a) achieves high cross-LLM discrimination without free-form text, using system metadata such as model identifier, architecture, dense/sparse indicator, and parameter count together with tabular instance features such as shot count and prompt-template id, and reports that system identifier or parameter count are the most informative system features. Its aggregated AUROC 0.929 and Brier score 0.110 are close to GPT-3 self-assessment AUROC 0.916 and Brier score 0.135, suggesting that reference-instance performance vectors and text embeddings may be less necessary when system metadata are available and that self-assessment is a strong baseline, although the proposed method is motivated by the unavailability of such metadata for proprietary models. No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported for the assessor comparisons. Next, [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) is methodologically aligned but does not provide quantitative outcomes, AUC values, baseline comparisons, ablations, reference-set sizes, classifier architecture, hyperparameters, or statistical significance, so it does not confirm that the proposed reference-vector plus instance-feature classifier achieves useful predictive performance or that the planned reference sizes of 30, 100, 300, and 1,000 are sufficient. Next, [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2) supports IRT-based selection but estimates scenario-level LLM ability rather than instance-level binary correctness, and the excerpt does not report numerical estimation error, AUC, rank correlation, or significance, so it does not directly validate instance-level prediction from a small reference vector. Next, [(Shah et al., 2019-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/735a63b58349e07b84c2e31927ce1b1cfaf09980) is model-specific rather than generic, using internal VQA representations for BUTD and Pythia without evaluating generalization to unseen LLM families, and the excerpt lacks numeric failure-prediction accuracy, AUC, or significance, so it does not support cross-LLM transfer of the proposed assessor. Next, [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) shows that character n-gram subword information can reduce performance on semantic analogies for German and Italian, that 2-grams are less useful for analogy tasks, and that results are point estimates without significance tests, implying that n-gram and embedding features may be task- and language-dependent rather than universally beneficial. Next, [(Papineni et al., 2002-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d7da009f457917aa381619facfa5ffae9329a6e9) is a reference-based translation metric rather than a trained LLM-instance predictor, and its 500-sentence corpus, 20 blocks of 25 sentences, and human-judgment regression do not establish that raw n-gram frequencies of prompts predict binary LLM correctness. Finally, [(Gardner et al., 2020-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9514630194a9fba9505b594ec921b247fecb48) uses small targeted contrast sets rather than representative reference instances, with up to about 1,000 total examples and 1 to 5 examples per contrast set, and reports category-specific accuracy, F1, or contrast consistency rather than predictor AUC, so it does not support the claim that a small reference set can generalize to arbitrary unseen instances unless selection is explicitly representative.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to implement the proposed assessor as the missing quantitative instantiation of [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), reporting AUC, Brier score, calibration, confidence intervals, and significance tests on HELM-Lite with 4,285 instances and 30 LLMs and KindsOfReasoning with 37,529 instances and 14 OpenAI instruction-tuned models, while varying reference sizes 30, 100, 300, and 1,000 and using random and out-of-distribution instance splits plus test LLMs from different producers or GPT-4 versions. Next, treat IRT as both a reference-selection method and a strong baseline by fitting item parameters on training LLMs, estimating held-out LLM ability with p-IRT and gp-IRT, using the default variance adjustment factor of 4 for anchor-based sampling, and comparing IRT against K-means, Factor Analysis, and random selection under random and date-based LLM splits as in [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2). Next, test whether instance embeddings and LLM performance vectors are complementary using the [(Ailem et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f187697a28fdc8d6d44dc3c1ea3a65ed85449c42) protocol, with pairwise cosine, Jaccard, or Hamming performance similarities, OpenAI ada-2 or other prompt embeddings, per-prompt regression, FDR adjustment, 1,000 permutations, and KS tests, and include ablations for OpenAI embeddings, Word2Vec, FastText, and n-gram frequencies rather than assuming all features help. Next, add the baselines implied by [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a), including LLM self-assessed probability, a system-metadata assessor when metadata are available, reference-performance-only, instance-feature-only, pooled-data-only, and random-reference assessors, and report AUC, Brier score, calibration, and reject-option behavior such as a 1 percent threshold that rejects 21.7 percent of instances while catching 46 percent of failures and 1.5 percent of correct answers. Next, configure text features using the [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) settings as a strong FastText baseline, with 300-dimensional vectors, character n-grams 3 to 6, 5 negative samples, context window 1 to 5, subsampling threshold 1e-4, minimum word count 5, and learning rate 0.05, while comparing against Word2Vec, OpenAI embeddings, and n-gram frequencies and monitoring the reported 105k words per second per thread cost. Next, include a tree-based classifier baseline using the [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a) Random Forest configuration of 100 trees, minimum node size 5, and 50 percent feature subsampling, with an 84/16 validation split and order-matched test partitions to prevent leakage, and compare it against the planned logistic regression L1 and L2 and XGBoost models. Next, adopt the failure-prediction evaluation framing of [(Shah et al., 2019-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/735a63b58349e07b84c2e31927ce1b1cfaf09980) by measuring calibration and failure detection, including Brier score, expected calibration error, and reject thresholds, and by considering internal LLM representations only as an auxiliary ablation when available. Next, use [(Gardner et al., 2020-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9514630194a9fba9505b594ec921b247fecb48) contrast sets as diagnostic probes for specific reasoning phenomena, but do not rely on them as the main reference set because they are targeted and small, with up to about 1,000 total examples and 1 to 5 examples per contrast set. Finally, use [(Papineni et al., 2002-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d7da009f457917aa381619facfa5ffae9329a6e9) n-gram precision as a derived feature or sanity-check baseline, and use block-wise paired t-tests and linear regression against human or gold judgments only where appropriate, rather than as the primary LLM-instance correctness metric.

## Context: The plan needs LLM-specific features that do not rely on unavailable architecture details. Method: Instead of using unavailable architectural features, we will characterize each LLM by its binary success vector on the reference instances, creating observational features that capture LLM behavior.

### Support

The strongest support is methodological and comes from [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which implements exactly the proposed LLM characterization: a small reference set D_ref is selected, each LLM is represented by its binary success vector on D_ref, the vector is concatenated with prompt-intrinsic features, and a generic classifier predicts binary correctness for LLM-instance pairs. It uses held-out LLM validation splits and evaluates new LLMs only on D_ref, matching the plan’s HELM-Lite 4,285-instance/30-LLM and KindsOfReasoning 37,529-instance/14-OpenAI-model settings and its goal of avoiding architecture details. However, the supplied excerpt reports no AUC, baseline comparison, reference-size ablation, or statistical significance, so this support is design-level rather than outcome-level. The strongest empirical support is [(Ye et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9bbe868a4d085b579183997af774309a0079e20a), which shows that a compact observed-performance profile over reference tasks can predict held-out task performance on 56,143 BIG-bench records covering 6 model families, 51 models, 134 tasks, 313 subtasks, and 0/1/2/3/5 shots. In easier random splits, gradient boosted trees and MLPs achieved RMSE below 0.05 and R2 above 0.95, and an 8-task best-of-5000 subset or random 16-task subset could match a 24-task BIG-bench Hard subset, while k-means was comparable to or better than best-of-5000. This supports the plan’s reference-instance selection and small-budget idea, but it uses task-level aggregated accuracy rather than instance-level binary success vectors and still uses model-family and parameter-count features. Next, [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a) supports the broader generic-assessor premise by showing that LLM-specific features are useful: a Random Forest with 100 trees, minimum node size 5, 50% variable sampling, and grid search on an 84/16 split achieved aggregated AUROC 0.929 and Brier 0.110 on 38k test instances, outperforming GPT-3 self-estimation AUROC 0.916 and Brier 0.135, with per-model AUROC from 0.908 for Ada to 0.944 for Davinci and BIG-G around AUROC 0.9/Brier 0.12. Its reject-rule example at a 1% threshold rejected 5,340 of 24,604 instances, correctly rejecting 5,114 failures (46% of failures) while rejecting 226 correct answers (about 1.5%), showing that instance-level prediction can be operationally useful. Finally, [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) supports the plan’s use of n-gram and subword instance features: 300-dimensional character n-gram representations improved word similarity and analogy performance, remained competitive with very little training data (e.g., English RW similarity 45 at 1% Wikipedia versus 43 for full-data cbow; German GUR350 66 at 5% versus 62 for full-data cbow), and showed that 3-6 character n-grams are generally useful, although 2-grams are weaker and longer n-grams can help morphology. This is indirect because it does not study LLMs or reference success vectors, but it justifies including n-gram frequencies as observable instance-intrinsic features.

### Contradictions

The strongest concern is that the most informative LLM-specific features in the closest generic-assessor evidence are unavailable system identifiers rather than observable reference behavior. [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a) found that system id or parameter count significantly improved the Random Forest assessor, while shots, prompt-template id, model family, and sparsity had smaller effects, and it did not test whether a binary success vector on reference instances could substitute for those features. Because the proposed method explicitly avoids architecture and system identifiers, this result suggests that the proposed observational vector may be a weaker proxy, especially for unseen LLMs, unless the reference set is large and representative. The next strongest limitation is that the closest empirical reference-profile evidence is task-level, not instance-level, and degrades under harder splits. [(Ye et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9bbe868a4d085b579183997af774309a0079e20a) used aggregated task performance, not binary instance outcomes, and prediction performance decreased and variance increased as splits moved from L1 to L2 to L3 to L3 composition; BIG-bench Lite and BIG-bench Hard were sub-optimal for recovering remaining-task performance, greedy search was unstable, and at budget 42 it underperformed best-of-5000. This contradicts the assumption that a small reference set will automatically transfer to instance-level prediction across unseen LLMs and OOD datasets, and it implies that reference selection must be validated on held-out LLMs rather than assumed from benchmark popularity. A further limitation is that the direct implementation of the proposed method lacks reported quantitative evidence. [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) describes the binary success vector over D_ref and the generic assessor on HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning, but the supplied excerpt does not report AUC, comparisons against LLM-specific assessors, random-reference baselines, reference-only or intrinsic-only baselines, or the effect of reference sizes 30, 100, 300, and 1000; it also does not specify exact reference-set sizes, classifier hyperparameters, hardware, or statistical significance. Therefore, while the method is plausible, the evidence that it outperforms baselines or generalizes to substantially different LLMs is not yet established. The weakest contradiction is indirect: [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) does not address LLM characterization and provides no evidence against the binary success vector, but its n-gram results are limited to word representations, with no confidence intervals or significance tests, and show that n-gram range can hurt semantic analogy if mistuned (e.g., German semantic analogy 62 for 3-6 versus 65 for 6-6; English semantic analogy 77 for 3-6 versus 80 for 5-5 or 6-6), so n-gram features should be treated as one ablated instance feature rather than a guaranteed solution.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to directly test whether the binary reference-instance success vector can replace unavailable architecture features by running the plan’s generic assessor with three LLM-feature conditions: (i) system id/parameter count when available, (ii) binary success vector only, and (iii) no LLM-specific features, using the same HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning splits, AUC as primary metric, and Brier or reject-rule operating points as secondary metrics. This should include held-out LLM splits with different providers/families, as in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), and report confidence intervals or significance tests, which are absent in [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a) and [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850). The second most important action is to validate reference selection and budget using the plan’s 30, 100, 300, and 1000 reference instances, comparing random selection, K-means, Factor Analysis, IRT, and best-of-N or greedy search, while following the caution from [(Ye et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9bbe868a4d085b579183997af774309a0079e20a) that fixed benchmark subsets can be sub-optimal and greedy search can be unstable; selection should be optimized on a held-out LLM/instance development split and evaluated on separate test LLMs, with R2/RMSE or AUC reported per scenario and across OOD splits. Third, the instance-intrinsic feature ablation should include n-gram frequencies, FastText, Word2Vec, and OpenAI embeddings, motivated by [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850), but should not assume n-grams are sufficient; report feature importance, class imbalance, and threshold behavior, and use temperature 0, no fine-tuning, and order-matched or leakage-controlled splits as in [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a). Fourth, the classifier experiments should specify and tune logistic regression L1/L2 and XGBoost hyperparameters, and compare against the Random Forest baseline from [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a) and the gradient boosted tree/MLP baselines from [(Ye et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9bbe868a4d085b579183997af774309a0079e20a), including LLM-specific assessors trained on full data, random-reference assessors, reference-only assessors, and intrinsic-only assessors. Finally, the paper should report exact reference-set sizes, dataset splits, hardware or compute, and OOD results for held-out reasoning types, because the current direct evidence in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) is incomplete and the plan’s minimum-reference-instance claim cannot be verified without those details.

## Context: The plan needs a small representative reference set to characterize LLMs without full evaluation. Method: From existing evaluation datasets, we will extract a small set of representative reference instances using various selection methods including K-means clustering, Factor Analysis, and Item Response Theory (IRT).

### Support

Read as an iterative progression from conceptual IRT to empirical subsampling and reference-instance assessment, the strongest support comes from [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which directly instantiates the proposed reference-instance method: it constructs a generic assessor from existing LLM evaluation matrices, draws a small reference subset, characterizes each LLM by a binary success vector on that subset, concatenates that vector with prompt-intrinsic features, trains a classifier on train LLMs and train instances, validates on held-out LLMs and instances, and predicts binary correctness for test LLMs on novel instances without test labels. Its setting matches the plan closely, using HELM-Lite with 4,285 instances across 6 scenarios and 30 LLMs, and KindsOfReasoning with 22 reasoning datasets, 37,529 instances, and 14 instruction-tuned OpenAI models, including hard LLM splits and out-of-distribution instance splits, and it situates the method within IRT and clustering benchmark subsampling. This is the strongest evidence because it supports the exact architectural hypothesis that a small reference success vector can serve as an LLM-specific feature for a generic assessor, although the supplied excerpt does not report AUC, minimum reference-set size, or statistical significance, so the support is design-level rather than quantitative. The next strongest support is [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2), which operationalizes IRT for LLM benchmark subsampling by fitting item parameters alpha_i and beta_i and model ability theta_l, selecting smaller example sets with stratified sampling and clustering, and estimating performance with p-IRT and gp-IRT on four benchmarks with latent dimension at most 16, random and date-based train-test model splits, and a convergence proposition under consistent theta_l estimation. This supports the plan's IRT-based reference selection and its emphasis on train/test model splits, but it is weaker for the proposed method because it estimates aggregate benchmark performance rather than training an instance-level generic assessor. A further supporting line is [(Ailem et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f187697a28fdc8d6d44dc3c1ea3a65ed85449c42), which builds prompt-by-model performance matrices, clusters them with spherical k-means using cosine-distance silhouette scores, selects medoid representatives, and tests weighted and incrementally grown prompt sets on ANLI with 1,200 prompts and binary accuracy, HellaSwag with 1,005 prompts and binary accuracy, CommonsenseQA with 1,221 prompts and binary accuracy, and CNN/Daily Mail with 1,150 prompts and ROUGE. Its permutation and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests show that prompt performance vectors are statistically correlated and that clustering yields higher silhouette scores than permuted vectors, supporting the idea that K-means-style representative prompts can preserve model-performance structure, but it evaluates ranking stability of known LLMs rather than predicting held-out LLM-instance correctness. The next strongest empirical support is [(Vivek et al., 2023-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d4085ae0f004624a3141734d3a88a9ebbc803a55), which selects 30 anchor points from 60 source models on the SST-2 validation set using a K-medoids objective solved with Partitioning Around Medoids over Pearson correlations of logit-transformed correct-class confidence, with per-class averaging when labels are unavailable, and reports that an Anchor Point Predictor achieved 0.09 MAE and 92 percent agreement for Falcon-7B, showing that a small anchor set can localize model weakness and support target-model prediction, but it is less aligned because it uses confidence correlations and source-model trends rather than binary success vectors, IRT, Factor Analysis, or a generic assessor for unseen LLMs. The weakest support is [(Lalor et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/176a0aacc7aa2fc576e6d0b0fc6d2d860c5fce85), which conceptually positions IRT as complementary to classification metrics and useful for selecting items covering model ability, but provides no dataset, model count, IRT hyperparameters, quantitative outcome, or implementation for reference-set extraction.

### Contradictions

Read iteratively, newer empirical work tempers earlier conceptual optimism. The strongest contradiction is from [(Ailem et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f187697a28fdc8d6d44dc3c1ea3a65ed85449c42), because although its clustering analysis shows correlated prompt performance vectors, the reduced benchmark produced non-trivial ranking changes, with some models moving up to 5 positions relative to full-benchmark rank, and its evaluation is limited to ranking stability of known LLMs rather than prediction for held-out LLMs or unseen instances. This directly challenges the plan's assumption that a small reference set can reliably characterize an LLM for a generic assessor, since ranking instability implies that the reference success vector may be sensitive to which instances are selected. The next strongest contradiction is from [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2), which warns that p-IRT can have low variance when theta_l is estimated from many scenarios but can be biased if the IRT model is misspecified, and therefore introduces gp-IRT to balance direct-sampling variance with IRT bias, including a variance-scaling heuristic such as dividing anchor-based variance by 4. This contradicts a naive use of IRT item parameters for selecting reference instances for unseen LLMs, especially under distribution shift, because the paper's date-based split explicitly tests more recent test models and item parameters are learned from training models rather than assumed stable across all LLM families. A further contradiction is from [(Vivek et al., 2023-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d4085ae0f004624a3141734d3a88a9ebbc803a55), whose anchor-point method assumes that linear trends fitted to source-model predictions explain target-model prediction variance, evaluates only Falcon-7B as the target model on SST-2, and does not provide quantitative results for the Anchor Point Weighted score in the supplied text. This limits generalizability to the proposed method, which requires binary success vectors, multiple unseen LLMs, and instance-level prediction across heterogeneous reasoning datasets rather than confidence-based prediction within one source-model pool. A weaker contradiction is from [(Yu et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ab83eae00db6a19de1ebdb23f7ceed29b5832675), which uses k-means or k-center clustering on Alpaca with 52K prompts and WizardLM with 196K prompts to select fine-tuning data for Llama-2-7B trained for 3 epochs with AdamW, learning rate 1e-5, 1,000 warm-up steps, maximum sequence length 2048, and effective batch size 64, not evaluation reference instances, and reports that downstream performance changes substantially with the number of clusters k, that kM-Random can be comparable to stronger baselines, and that quality-scored or iterative sampling can improve results. This does not directly contradict the plan, but it weakens the claim that K-means alone is sufficient for representative reference selection, because cluster count, sampling budget, and quality signals materially affect outcomes. The weakest contradiction is from [(Lalor et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/176a0aacc7aa2fc576e6d0b0fc6d2d860c5fce85), which provides no empirical evidence, baseline comparison, statistical test, dataset size, or implementation detail, so it cannot substantiate the feasibility of IRT-based reference extraction for LLM assessment.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to adopt the experimental protocol of [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) as the primary evaluation harness: use HELM-Lite with 4,285 instances, 6 scenarios, and 30 LLMs, and KindsOfReasoning with 22 reasoning datasets, 37,529 instances, and 14 instruction-tuned OpenAI models; create train/validation/test splits for instances and LLMs; ensure test LLMs are from different providers or model families; include out-of-distribution instance splits; report AUC as the primary metric with confidence intervals or significance tests; and ablate reference-set sizes 30, 100, 300, and 1000 against random selection, reference-only, pooled-only, and LLM-specific full-data assessors. The second most important action is to implement IRT in the manner of [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2), fitting item difficulty and discrimination parameters alpha_i and beta_i and model ability theta_l on training LLM correctness, using random and date-based model splits, constraining latent dimension to at most 16, validating IRT fit with cross-validated likelihood or residual diagnostics, and using IRT item parameters either to select reference instances that span ability ranges or to form p-IRT and gp-IRT baselines that expose bias from misspecification, including gp-IRT's variance-scaling heuristic such as dividing anchor-based variance by 4. The third most important action is to add the performance-vector clustering and ranking-stability evaluation from [(Ailem et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f187697a28fdc8d6d44dc3c1ea3a65ed85449c42): construct prompt-by-model binary success matrices, apply spherical k-means with cosine-distance silhouette scores, select medoids, test weighted benchmarks and incremental farthest-point growth, and report Spearman or Kendall rank correlation, top-k rank changes, and AUC on held-out LLMs so that reference selection is judged by both representativeness and predictive utility. The fourth most important action is to include the anchor-point method of [(Vivek et al., 2023-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d4085ae0f004624a3141734d3a88a9ebbc803a55) as a strong baseline or hybrid: when confidence scores are available, logit-transform correct-class probabilities, compute Pearson correlations across source models, solve a K-medoids objective with 30 anchors from a pool of about 60 source models, and report MAE, agreement, and AUC; when only binary success is available, adapt the objective to correlations or rank correlations of binary success vectors. The fifth action is to incorporate practical clustering-selection lessons from [(Yu et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ab83eae00db6a19de1ebdb23f7ceed29b5832675), including k-means versus k-center, cluster-size-proportional random sampling, silhouette-guided choice of k, and, if computationally feasible, quality-scored or iterative resampling using cheap scorers such as perplexity or reward models rather than expensive LLM scoring. Finally, because no supplied related work implements Factor Analysis for this task, the plan should explicitly justify Factor Analysis by fitting a latent-factor or factor-analysis model to the binary LLM-instance success matrix, comparing it against IRT and K-means using cross-validated AUC, reporting factor loadings or communalities used for instance selection, and documenting hardware, runtime, hyperparameters, and random seeds to ensure reproducibility.

## Context: The plan needs instance-intrinsic features for the classifier. Method: For instance features, we will test various text representations including OpenAI embeddings, Word2Vec, FastText, and n-gram frequencies.

### Support

The strongest support comes from [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which directly studies a feature-concatenation generic assessor that joins prompt-intrinsic features f(p_i) with an LLM-specific binary success vector on reference instances D_ref, trains on L_train x D_train pairs, validates on L_val/D_val, and evaluates on HELM-Lite with 4,285 instances and 30 LLMs and KindsOfReasoning with 37,529 instances and 14 OpenAI instruction-tuned models; it explicitly allows prompt-only features such as similarity between vector embeddings of p_i and reference instances, which supports the plan's use of OpenAI, Word2Vec, FastText, and n-gram features as instance-intrinsic inputs. The next strongest support is [(Ye et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1dcb8d28c3c000bc3365dab82c81eddd14c31aec), which uses OpenAI-256E embeddings with a linear CTR model on 17,681 headline A/B tests and 77,245 headlines, achieving 46.28% test accuracy versus 33.02% random, 39.96% GPT-4 prompting, and 46.86% LoRA fine-tuning, and reports an API cost of about $0.29, supporting OpenAI embeddings as cheap, deployable instance features for a linear predictor even though the target is click preference rather than LLM correctness. Stronger indirect support for n-gram and embedding features in a predictive classifier comes from [(Cer et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a23fa96e7217ba0e9405d9e1fe3cdedd57b6e096), whose STS system combined n-gram overlap, edit distance, longest common prefix/suffix/substring, pooled word-embedding similarities, and XGBoost/gradient-boosting/random-forest regressors to reach an average Pearson correlation of 73.16 across tracks, far above a binary word-occurrence cosine baseline of 53.70, showing that surface n-gram and embedding features can be predictive when paired with a strong learner. Support for FastText and bigram features in binary short-text prediction appears in [(Bustos, and Pertusa, 2018-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/67b2ff7ab7cde4d9ef15a02afe9dbc2e67613391), where a CNN using bigrams and pre-trained word embeddings reached validation accuracy 0.91, F1 0.91, and kappa 0.83 on 814,295 validation samples, while kNN reached F1 0.92-0.93 on 1,000,000 and full 4.1M-sample settings, indicating that FastText-style embeddings and n-gram features can be effective for large binary text classification. Smaller but relevant support is [(Tiun et al., 2020-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/291b7b24f7090428c319d73e681ded245b55c470), where fastText with a linear classifier achieved F1 92.8% on 625 short requirement texts, outperforming a CNN/Word2vec benchmark of 91.6%, while TFIDF with logistic regression was the best traditional feature/classifier combination, supporting both FastText and statistical n-gram features on short prompts. For FastText specifically, [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) shows that character n-gram subword vectors outperformed word-level CBOW/skip-gram on similarity and syntactic analogy, with stronger gains in morphologically rich languages and robustness to reduced data, e.g., German GUR350 similarity 66 at 5% data versus 62 for full-data CBOW, supporting FastText as a candidate for rare-token or OOV-sensitive prompt features. For Word2Vec, [(Baroni et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3cfbb77e5a0e24772cfdb2eb3d4f35dead54b118) provides strong evidence that prediction-trained Word2Vec vectors are generally strong and robust, with the best model using 400 dimensions, negative sampling, and frequent-word subsampling, while count-based PMI/SVD vectors remain competitive on selectional preference, supporting inclusion of both Word2Vec and frequency-based n-gram features. [(Mikolov et al., 2013-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/87f40e6f3022adbc1f1905e3e506abad05a9964f) further supports Word2Vec as a tunable representation, showing that negative sampling, frequent-word subsampling, 300 dimensions, context size 5, and large corpora improve analogy performance, with a 33-billion-word, 1000-dimensional phrase model reaching 72% phrase analogy accuracy. [(Pennington et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f37e1b62a767a307c046404ca96bc140b3e68cb5) supports the broader use of static embeddings as classifier features, reporting GloVe 42B Spearman correlations of 75.9, 83.6, 82.9, 59.6, and 47.8 on five similarity sets versus CBOW* 100B at 68.4, 79.6, 75.4, 59.4, and 45.5, and showing that dimensionality, corpus size, and context window matter. Additional support for n-gram and subword features comes from [(Salle, and Villavicencio, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6d9837a9f89b53fdd1d1b25455f7fee39f4e2e7c), where n-gram LexVec improved rare-word similarity from 0.461 to 0.522 and fastText improved syntactic analogy to 71.1 on GSyn and 59.6 on MSR, and from [(Wohlgenannt et al., 2019-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a0fd1ccf33078b55448155940ba3064de22754f2), where fastText with 300,000 vectors and 300 dimensions was best for Wikidata property alias matching, ranking the correct property first in over 70% of single-entity alias cases and achieving MRR 0.80. Document-level evidence that simple n-gram and word-averaging features can be competitive is provided by [(Lau, and Baldwin, 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66d0c1a5933caa3e5f1e21d7cb676ca2bfab32ab), which found unigram/bigram/trigram combinations best among n-gram orders and word averaging best for shorter documents, relevant to prompt-length variation. Weaker but still supportive evidence includes [(Hill et al., 2014-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7a96765c147c9c814803c8c9de28a1dd069271da), where a neural word-vector model achieved Spearman 0.282 on SimLex-999 versus 0.194 for a vector space model and 0.233 for LSA; [(Salle et al., 2016-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6bdd4b7d879a68a143edba8f7f35839bfa5241f6), where LexVec, skip-gram, and GloVe captured different semantic/syntactic structure; [(Konkol et al., 2017-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/edf420ee068c12950645b43787ce9f73c3914fff), where FastText was second only to LSA for recovering city coordinates from word embeddings; [(Mikolov et al., 2013-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f6b51c8753a871dc94ff32152c00c01e94f90f09), which qualitatively showed that Word2Vec vectors encode analogical structure; and [(Řehůřek, and Sojka, 2010-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/83a6cacc126d85c45605797406262677c256a6af), which demonstrates scalable TF-IDF, LSA, and LDA vector-space implementations with about 480 MB RAM for 315,167 word types and 200 topics, supporting practical deployment of n-gram and latent vector features across many LLM-instance pairs.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction to the assumption that FastText or n-gram features will automatically improve the classifier is [(Salle, and Villavicencio, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6d9837a9f89b53fdd1d1b25455f7fee39f4e2e7c), which found that although n-gram LexVec and fastText improved some similarity and analogy scores, downstream VecEval classifier gains were minimal: the only statistically significant difference was a small chunking gain of 90.6 versus 90.4, while sentiment, question classification, and NLI accuracies were close, and out-of-vocabulary words were mapped to an unknown token so frequent in-vocabulary words dominated. This cautions that adding FastText or subword n-gram features to a classifier may yield limited AUC gains if prompts are dominated by frequent tokens or OOV handling is poor. A second important caution is [(Cer et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a23fa96e7217ba0e9405d9e1fe3cdedd57b6e096), where a simple binary word-occurrence cosine baseline achieved only 53.70 average Pearson correlation versus 73.16 for a feature-engineered deep/boosting ensemble, suggesting that raw n-gram or embedding features alone may be insufficient and that the plan should not expect a single text representation to be enough. [(Hill et al., 2014-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7a96765c147c9c814803c8c9de28a1dd069271da) contradicts the assumption that one embedding objective will transfer uniformly: a global-context neural model performed well on association-based WS-353 with correlation about 0.442 but poorly on similarity-focused SimLex-999, showing that association-focused or context-heavy representations can reduce performance on the target semantic relation. [(Baroni et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3cfbb77e5a0e24772cfdb2eb3d4f35dead54b118) also cautions against privileging Word2Vec alone, because count-based PMI/SVD vectors were much more competitive on selectional preference tasks and the best count model achieved average rank 35, while Word2Vec was not uniformly best across all tasks. For Word2Vec specifically, [(Pennington et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f37e1b62a767a307c046404ca96bc140b3e68cb5) shows that word2vec CBOW was outperformed by GloVe on similarity and analogy, that word2vec performance decreased when negative samples increased beyond about 10, and that vectors larger than about 200 dimensions showed diminishing returns, so an untuned Word2Vec feature may be suboptimal. [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) provides a FastText-specific contradiction: the subword model did not improve semantic analogies under the default 3-6 character n-gram setting, sometimes degraded German and Italian performance, and was slower, processing about 105k words per second per thread versus 145k for the word2vec baseline. [(Konkol et al., 2017-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/edf420ee068c12950645b43787ce9f73c3914fff) shows that embedding choice is task-dependent, with LSA best and FastText second for geographic coordinate recovery while GloVe performed significantly worse, contradicting any assumption that FastText will dominate all text-feature settings. [(Lau, and Baldwin, 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66d0c1a5933caa3e5f1e21d7cb676ca2bfab32ab) cautions that document representation performance depends on length and training domain, with dbow better for longer documents and word averaging better for shorter documents, so a single prompt-level aggregation may not generalize across HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning. [(Tiun et al., 2020-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/291b7b24f7090428c319d73e681ded245b55c470) is a weaker contradiction because its 625-sample dataset is small and its TFIDF/SVM result was poor at F1 38.42%, while Doc2vec ranged from 73.47% to 82.66%, indicating that small-text results and classifier choice can be unstable. Finally, [(Řehůřek, and Sojka, 2010-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/83a6cacc126d85c45605797406262677c256a6af) provides no quantitative quality metric, baseline comparison, or statistical significance for its TF-IDF, LSA, or LDA representations, so it supports scalability but not predictive quality for LLM-instance classification.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to follow the directly related design in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) by implementing the generic assessor as a feature-concatenation model over prompt-intrinsic features f(p_i) and LLM reference-success vectors g(m_j), and by adding explicit similarity features between each target prompt and the selected reference instances using OpenAI, Word2Vec, FastText, and n-gram representations, because the plan's HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning setup matches that paper's datasets and the reference-vector formulation. The next most important action is to standardize the four proposed instance representations with prompt-appropriate aggregation: for OpenAI embeddings, use the returned sentence vector directly or reduce/whiten dimensions; for Word2Vec and FastText, compute mean, max, and sum of token vectors, handle OOV by subword vectors or null vectors, and include unigram, bigram, and trigram frequency features with raw counts, TF-IDF, and PMI weighting, because [(Cer et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a23fa96e7217ba0e9405d9e1fe3cdedd57b6e096), [(Bustos, and Pertusa, 2018-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/67b2ff7ab7cde4d9ef15a02afe9dbc2e67613391), and [(Lau, and Baldwin, 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66d0c1a5933caa3e5f1e21d7cb676ca2bfab32ab) show that n-gram overlap, bigrams, and unigram/bigram/trigram combinations can be predictive for short text. Third, tune the representations using the hyperparameter evidence from the literature: for Word2Vec, test negative sampling counts around 5-10, frequent-word subsampling, dimensions 100/300/400, and context size 5, as in [(Mikolov et al., 2013-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/87f40e6f3022adbc1f1905e3e506abad05a9964f) and [(Baroni et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3cfbb77e5a0e24772cfdb2eb3d4f35dead54b118); for FastText, test character n-gram ranges 3-6, dimensions 100/300, vocabulary around 300,000, and pretrained versus in-domain training, as in [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850), [(Wohlgenannt et al., 2019-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a0fd1ccf33078b55448155940ba3064de22754f2), and [(Salle, and Villavicencio, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6d9837a9f89b53fdd1d1b25455f7fee39f4e2e7c); for OpenAI embeddings, test 256-dimensional and higher-dimensional variants and compare against linear models, as in [(Ye et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1dcb8d28c3c000bc3365dab82c81eddd14c31aec). Fourth, add the plan's required baselines and ablations in a way that isolates the contribution of text features: no instance features, reference-only, random reference selection, pooled-only, LLM-specific assessors, and each text representation alone and in combination, reporting AUC with confidence intervals, multiple random seeds, and significance tests, because [(Cer et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a23fa96e7217ba0e9405d9e1fe3cdedd57b6e096) shows simple baselines can be weak and [(Salle, and Villavicencio, 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6d9837a9f89b53fdd1d1b25455f7fee39f4e2e7c) shows downstream gains may be small without significance testing. Fifth, consider adding lightweight surface features such as prompt length, n-gram overlap with reference instances, edit distance, longest common prefix/suffix/substring, and pooled embedding similarities, because [(Cer et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a23fa96e7217ba0e9405d9e1fe3cdedd57b6e096) found such features useful in a strong STS system. Sixth, monitor computational and memory constraints: use cached embeddings, sparse n-gram matrices, and modest dimensions, because [(Řehůřek, and Sojka, 2010-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/83a6cacc126d85c45605797406262677c256a6af) reports about 480 MB for LSA/LDA transformation models with 315,167 word types and 200 topics, [(Wohlgenannt et al., 2019-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a0fd1ccf33078b55448155940ba3064de22754f2) reports about 650 MB for fastText with 300,000 vectors and 300 dimensions, and [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) reports slower subword training. Finally, if the four proposed representations underperform, add optional GloVe, LSA, or TF-IDF/PMI baselines, because [(Pennington et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f37e1b62a767a307c046404ca96bc140b3e68cb5), [(Konkol et al., 2017-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/edf420ee068c12950645b43787ce9f73c3914fff), and [(Baroni et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3cfbb77e5a0e24772cfdb2eb3d4f35dead54b118) show that alternative count-based or distributional representations can be competitive on different tasks.

## Context: The plan needs a base model for the generic assessor. Method: For the classifier, we will experiment with logistic regression (with L1 and L2 penalties) and XGBoost.

### Support

As a prior point in the iterative development of generic assessors, the strongest support for the proposed classifier choice is indirect but highly relevant: [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) describes a generic assessor that trains a classifier on LLM-instance pairs using prompt-intrinsic features plus an LLM-specific binary success vector on a small reference set, which is exactly the input structure proposed in the research plan. This related work also uses the same two dataset collections and constraints: HELM-Lite with 4,285 binary instances from 6 scenarios and 30 LLMs, and KindsOfReasoning with 22 reasoning datasets, 37,529 instances, and 14 OpenAI instruction-tuned models from text-ada-001 to gpt-4-0125-preview. It further validates the proposed split logic by using validation LLM splits and validation instances for model selection, and by testing new LLMs that are substantially different from training and validation LLMs, including provider-level differences in HELM-Lite and gpt-4 versions in KindsOfReasoning. A weaker but still relevant support is that the related work treats the classifier generically rather than specifying a model family, which leaves logistic regression with L1/L2 penalties and XGBoost as plausible base classifiers for the same binary correctness prediction task; however, because the excerpt does not report AUC, accuracy, or direct comparisons for these classifiers, the support for their specific choice is not quantitative.

### Contradictions

There is no direct contradiction in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) to the proposed use of logistic regression with L1/L2 penalties and XGBoost, because the paper does not report classifier-specific outcomes, hyperparameters, or statistical comparisons for those models. The main tension is evidential rather than empirical: the related work establishes the generic assessor framework but leaves the classifier unspecified, so it cannot confirm that a linear regularized model or a gradient-boosted tree will outperform other classifiers, or that XGBoost will be stable when the LLM population is small, namely 30 LLMs in HELM-Lite and 14 OpenAI models in KindsOfReasoning, and the LLM-specific feature is a short binary reference-success vector. A second, weaker tension is that the related work emphasizes that test-instance ground truth is unavailable for a new LLM, so the classifier must rely only on reference-instance success and intrinsic prompt features; if the proposed logistic regression or XGBoost implementation uses features or splits that leak future LLM-specific information, it would contradict the intended evaluation protocol, although the supplied excerpt does not provide implementation details that would confirm such leakage.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to treat logistic regression and XGBoost as competing base classifiers in a controlled ablation rather than as validated choices, following the evaluation protocol in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970): train on the same training LLMs and instances, select hyperparameters using the same validation LLM split and validation instances, and evaluate on held-out test LLMs and test instances without using test-instance ground truth for the new LLM. For logistic regression, standardize or scale instance features, test L1 and L2 penalties with a small grid, and report AUC with confidence intervals or paired bootstrap significance tests across instances and LLMs. For XGBoost, constrain the model to avoid overfitting the small LLM population, tune depth, learning rate, subsampling, and regularization, and compare it against logistic regression under identical feature sets, reference-instance counts of 30, 100, 300, and 1000, and OOD splits. A secondary action is to add a simple baseline such as an intercept-only or reference-only classifier to show whether the added complexity of XGBoost yields a meaningful AUC gain over a regularized linear model. A final, lower-priority action is to document reproducibility details that the related work does not specify, including random seeds, class-imbalance handling, computational resources, and whether AUC is computed per scenario or dataset and then aggregated, so that the classifier comparison can be interpreted in the same HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning settings.

## Context: The plan needs empirical benchmarks covering multiple providers and reasoning types. Method: We will conduct experiments on two dataset collections: 1. HELM-Lite: A subset of scenarios with binary performance metrics, containing 4,285 instances across 6 scenarios, with results available for 30 LLMs from different providers. 2. KindsOfReasoning: A new collection we will create comprising 22 existing reasoning datasets (37,529 instances total) covering logical, common sense, inductive, deductive, abductive, counterfactual, causal, analogical, spatial, and arithmetic reasoning. We will evaluate all instruction-tuned OpenAI models from text-ada-001 to gpt-4-0125-preview.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed empirical method comes from [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which directly instantiates the two collections named in the plan: a HELM-Lite subset with binary metrics, 6 scenarios, 4,285 instances, and 30 multi-provider LLMs, plus a KindsOfReasoning collection of 22 reasoning datasets, 37,529 instances, and 14 instruction-tuned OpenAI models from text-ada-001 through gpt-4-0125-preview. This work validates the proposed data scale, binary instance-level correctness, random and out-of-distribution instance splits, and LLM train/validation/test design in which held-out LLMs are from different producers for HELM-Lite and GPT-4 versions are held out for KindsOfReasoning. It therefore supports the feasibility of using these exact benchmarks to train and evaluate a generic assessor that predicts LLM-instance correctness from reference-instance success vectors and intrinsic instance features. This also fits an iterative trajectory in which older task-level work, such as [(Burnell et al., 2023-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00557300321dc60998e0f42853f4bba52d6e53db), first showed latent structure in LLM performance, and newer instance-level benchmark work moved toward the binary LLM-instance pairs required by the proposed generic assessor. A second strong support is [(Burnell et al., 2023-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00557300321dc60998e0f42853f4bba52d6e53db), which used 29 HELM LLMs, task-level correlations, Hull-selected three-factor EFA, and Bayesian factor analysis, finding mean task correlation r=0.56, median 0.6, and a stable three-factor solution except for LSAT. This supports the plan's use of factor analysis as a reference-instance selection strategy because it shows that LLM performance vectors contain low-dimensional latent structure that can be recovered from relatively small model samples. Additional support comes from [(Liu et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/806b5882c983bd156a8c10bcd34fe285d8a0593b), whose GLoRE benchmark shows large provider- and dataset-dependent accuracy differences, such as GPT-4 average 66.34%, QwQ-32B 78.95%, DeepSeek R1 75.14%, RoBERTa-base 48.76% on LogiQA 2.0, and a roughly 9 percentage point GPT-4 gain with 5-shot examples, motivating instance-level prediction rather than relying on a single global model score. Further support is provided by [(Ding et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ea987db9daa0af1e495b29e2a03dff975641181b), which shows that arithmetic, commonsense, and logical performance varies across ChatGPT, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, and Qwen1.5 models under few-shot, zero-shot, break-the-chain, no-steps, more-tokens, and shortcut prompts, with ShortcutQA relative improvements up to 28.56%, indicating that prompt and model identity are important predictors of success. The weakest but still relevant support is [(Han et al., 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0c7ca22dfbe1ce02cb0f0658292499457db8ec6e), which shows that reasoning performance depends on model identity, visual information, prompt strategy, and complexity, with GPT-4V scoring 77.44 versus Qwen-VL-Chat 37.39, CoT improving GPT-4V by 3.7 points, and scaling Qwen-VL from 7B to 14B increasing score from 35.32 to 37.39; although this is multimodal and open-ended, it reinforces the plan's motivation that reasoning type and model behavior must be captured by instance and LLM features.

### Contradictions

The most direct contradiction or caution concerns the proposed use of factor analysis with only about 30 LLMs. [(Burnell et al., 2023-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00557300321dc60998e0f42853f4bba52d6e53db) used 29 LLMs and found that the frequentist three-factor EFA had poor fit, CFI=0.70, TLI=0.61, RMSEA=0.26, and that the Bayesian solution differed on LSAT, which loaded moderately on reasoning in the frequentist analysis but was not assigned to a factor in the Bayesian analysis. This suggests that factor-based reference-instance selection may be unstable when the LLM sample is small, even if the overall three-factor structure is plausible; the proposed method should not assume that 30 LLMs provide reliable loadings without robustness checks. A second important limitation is that the closest benchmark work, [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), reports the construction of HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning but does not report AUC, classifier accuracy, reference-instance-size ablations, baseline comparisons, statistical significance, hardware, or classifier hyperparameters. Therefore, while it supports the data and split design, it does not provide evidence that a generic assessor can actually predict unseen LLM-instance correctness, and the proposed method must supply the missing predictive validation. A third caution comes from [(Liu et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/806b5882c983bd156a8c10bcd34fe285d8a0593b), which does not report confidence intervals, variance across repeated runs, or significance tests, and shows unstable task behavior such as LLaMA-30b-supercot and Falcon-40b-instruct scoring around 20% on four-way multi-choice reading comprehension, below the 25% random-guessing level. This contradicts any assumption that raw benchmark accuracy is a clean, low-noise signal for training an assessor unless prompts, sampling, and answer extraction are tightly controlled. A fourth caution is from [(Ding et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ea987db9daa0af1e495b29e2a03dff975641181b), which demonstrates that prompt strategy can materially change outcomes, for example zero-shot no-steps or more-tokens improving MultiArith to 91.17 or 93.50 versus 83.33 for zero-shot CoT, GSM8K to 37.53 or 38.89 versus 32.68, and CommonsenseQA to 75.92 or 74.28 versus 70.52, while also evaluating only one OpenAI model rather than the full proposed OpenAI family. This undermines the stability of a single binary LLM-instance label unless the evaluation protocol is fixed and prompt sensitivity is reported. The weakest contradiction is from [(Han et al., 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0c7ca22dfbe1ce02cb0f0658292499457db8ec6e), which uses LLM-judged open-ended scores rather than binary correctness, does not disclose the judge model, seeds, number of judge runs, or significance tests, and evaluates multimodal models rather than the proposed text-only OpenAI instruction-tuned population; this cautions that label construction and judge bias can materially affect the target variable, but it is less directly relevant than the other works.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to adopt and explicitly report the exact benchmark construction and split protocol from [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970): use the 4,285-instance HELM-Lite binary subset across 6 scenarios and 30 multi-provider LLMs, and the 37,529-instance KindsOfReasoning collection across 22 reasoning datasets and 14 instruction-tuned OpenAI models from text-ada-001 to gpt-4-0125-preview; define binary correctness per instance; create random and OOD instance splits; and create LLM train/validation/test splits with held-out providers for HELM-Lite and held-out GPT-4 versions for KindsOfReasoning. The second most important action is to make factor-analysis reference selection robust to the small-LLM-sample problem identified by [(Burnell et al., 2023-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00557300321dc60998e0f42853f4bba52d6e53db): report frequentist fit indices such as CFI, TLI, and RMSEA, run Bayesian or regularized factor analysis, use cross-validated or bootstrap factor-count selection, compare factor-based selection against K-means, IRT, and random baselines, and report loading stability and sensitivity to the LSAT-like edge cases. The third action is to standardize the evaluation protocol to address the prompt and sampling instability shown by [(Liu et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/806b5882c983bd156a8c10bcd34fe285d8a0593b) and [(Ding et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ea987db9daa0af1e495b29e2a03dff975641181b): fix prompts, few-shot counts, decoding parameters, answer extraction, and model versions; use temperature zero or multiple repeated runs when stochastic; report AUC with bootstrap confidence intervals and significance tests against LLM-specific, random-reference, reference-only, and pooled-only baselines; and include prompt-condition ablations if the target deployment may vary prompts. The fourth action is to strengthen label quality in the spirit of [(Han et al., 2023-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0c7ca22dfbe1ce02cb0f0658292499457db8ec6e): prefer deterministic binary extraction for multiple-choice, true/false, and arithmetic tasks; if an LLM judge is required for open-ended items, specify the judge model, prompt, number of runs, inter-rater agreement, and comparison to human labels, and avoid using open-ended judge scores as the primary binary target. The fifth action is to make the generalization claims precise: treat HELM-Lite as cross-provider evidence and KindsOfReasoning as within-OpenAI-family evidence, then add out-of-distribution LLM tests on non-OpenAI models or newer families if available, and report performance by reasoning category, dataset, model provider, and reference-instance size 30, 100, 300, and 1000. The least important but still useful action is to document reproducibility details missing from the related work, including hardware, software versions, random seeds, embedding dimensions, logistic regression penalties, XGBoost hyperparameters, and computational cost, so that the proposed generic assessor can be reproduced and compared to the baselines in the research plan.

## Context: The plan needs to test generalization to unseen LLMs and instances. Method: We will create train/validation/test splits for both instances (56%/14%/30%) and LLMs, ensuring test LLMs are substantially different from training LLMs (e.g., different providers or model families).

### Support

The strongest support comes from [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which directly implements the proposed split design for a generic assessor on the same two collections named in the plan: HELM-Lite with 4,285 binary-correctness instances across 6 scenarios and 30 LLMs, and KindsOfReasoning with 37,529 instances across 22 reasoning datasets and 14 instruction-tuned OpenAI models. It creates instance-level random and out-of-distribution splits, where entire datasets are reserved for testing, and LLM train/validation/test splits in which test LLMs are substantially different, holding out two producers in HELM-Lite and all GPT-4 model versions in KindsOfReasoning. This is the closest analogue to the proposed 56%/14%/30% instance split and provider/family-level LLM split, and it validates the core idea that a generic assessor can be trained on LLM-instance pairs using LLM-specific reference-set performance and instance-intrinsic prompt features while evaluating on unseen, substantially different LLMs. A second line of support is [(Ye et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9bbe868a4d085b579183997af774309a0079e20a), which shows that holding out model families and tasks is a meaningful hard generalization setting and that multi-group training across model families improves prediction, with Gopher R2 improving from 0.74 to 0.87 when moving from single-group to multi-group training. It also supports the plan's reference-instance selection component by showing that small task subsets can recover remaining-task performance: an 8-task Best of 5000 subset and a 16-task random subset can match a 24-task BIG-bench Hard subset, while k-means and k-means plus task value are competitive or better than random and Best of 5000. Finally, [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2) supports using held-out LLMs and a smaller anchor set, especially through IRT-based selection and date-based LLM splits that emulate distribution shift to newer unseen models; although it does not provide numerical results in the excerpt, its p-IRT and gp-IRT estimators align with the plan's IRT reference-selection and binary-correctness setting.

### Contradictions

The strongest caution is that the most direct evidence of hard generalization is negative: [(Ye et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9bbe868a4d085b579183997af774309a0079e20a) reports that performance decreases and standard deviation increases in harder L1 to L3 to L3 Composition settings that hold out model family, parameter count, task, and shot combinations, and that BIG-bench Hard can misrank models, for example recovered performance suggesting BIG-G T=1 2B beats GPT-3 Large while ground truth says the opposite. This contradicts an overly optimistic assumption that a small reference set and a provider/family-level LLM split will automatically preserve instance-level ranking or calibration for unseen LLMs. A second contradiction is that the same paper predicts task-level accuracy from model and task configuration features rather than binary instance-level correctness from a binary success vector on reference instances, and it does not use a 56/14/30 instance split, so its held-out model-family results are an analogue rather than direct validation of the proposed method. Third, [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2) is less directly supportive because it estimates aggregate benchmark performance from anchor examples rather than predicting correctness for individual LLM-instance pairs, and the excerpt provides no quantitative outcomes, significance tests, dataset sizes, or hyperparameters; its date-based split also tests temporal model shift rather than the provider/family split specified in the plan. Finally, although [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) matches the proposed split logic, the excerpt does not report the exact 56/14/30 instance ratios, classifier hyperparameters, AUC, failure-rejection rate, or statistical significance, and its KindsOfReasoning LLM split is limited to a single OpenAI family, so it does not by itself prove that the proposed provider/family split will generalize across heterogeneous providers.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to implement the split protocol exactly as a controlled experiment: use 56%/14%/30% instance splits for both random and OOD settings, with OOD test instances drawn from held-out datasets or reasoning types, and use LLM splits that hold out providers/families, following [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) by holding out two producers in HELM-Lite and all GPT-4 versions in KindsOfReasoning, while also adding a date-based or version-based LLM split inspired by [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2) to test newer-model shift. The next priority is to report generalization degradation explicitly, using multiple LLM split levels analogous to [(Ye et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9bbe868a4d085b579183997af774309a0079e20a)'s L1-L3 Composition settings, such as hold-out by provider, family, version, and parameter count, and to report AUC with confidence intervals, paired significance tests, and model-ranking metrics because Ye et al. show small-bench recovery can misrank models. For reference-instance selection, the plan should compare random, k-means, k-means plus task value, Best of 5000, IRT anchor selection, and Factor Analysis, while avoiding unstable greedy search unless stabilized, and it should optimize not only prediction R2 or AUC but also ranking recovery and robustness across held-out LLM folds, as in Ye et al. and Polo et al. Finally, include the plan's baselines, namely LLM-specific assessors, random reference selection, reference-only assessors, and pooled no-LLM-feature assessors, and ablate reference sizes 30, 100, 300, and 1000 with multi-group training across LLMs, because Ye et al. show multi-group training improves generalization and Polo et al. suggest IRT-based estimators can reduce variance when the model is well specified.

## Context: The plan needs to show the generic assessor outperforms simpler alternatives. Method: We will compare our generic assessor against: - LLM-specific assessors trained on full evaluation data - Random reference instance selection - Assessors using only reference instance performance - Assessors using only pooled training data without LLM-specific features.

### Support

The strongest support comes from the most directly aligned and newer work, [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which studies exactly the proposed generic-assessor method: a classifier predicts binary correctness from prompt-intrinsic features plus an LLM-specific binary success vector on a small reference subset, trained on held-out LLM-instance pairs and applied to new LLMs without labels on test instances. Its experimental setting matches the plan's datasets and splits, using HELM-Lite with 4,285 binary-metric instances and 30 LLMs, and KindsOfReasoning with 22 datasets, 37,529 instances, and 14 instruction-tuned OpenAI models from text-ada-001 to gpt-4-0125-preview, with held-out producer groups for HELM-Lite and all gpt-4 versions for KindsOfReasoning. This directly supports the proposed baseline structure, especially the contrast between a generic assessor and an LLM-specific assessor trained on full evaluation data, and the ablations of reference-only, pooled-instance-only, and LLM-specific-feature removal. A second, earlier but quantitative line of support is [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a), which builds a lightweight cross-LLM Random Forest assessor on pooled GPT-3 and BIG-G data-wrangling records, using 194k training and 38k test instances, 100 trees, minimum node size 5, and 50 percent feature subsampling, and reports aggregated AUROC 0.929 and Brier 0.110, outperforming GPT-3 self-estimated probability with aggregated AUROC 0.916 and Brier 0.135. Its reject-rule example on 24,604 GPT-3 test instances shows that a 1 percent predicted-success threshold rejects 5,340 instances, 21.7 percent, including 5,114 correct rejections, about 46 percent of failures, while rejecting only 226 correct answers, about 1.5 percent, supporting the practical value of instance-level prediction. The weakest but still relevant support is older word-representation work, [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850), which shows that character n-gram representations can remain useful under limited training data, for example German GUR350 similarity with 5 percent of Wikipedia reaches Spearman 66 versus CBOW on 100 percent at 62, and English RW with 1 percent reaches 45 versus full-data CBOW at 43; this supports the plan's use of n-gram frequencies and subword-like text features as instance-intrinsic features, although the domain is word representation rather than LLM assessment.

### Contradictions

The most important contradiction is not a direct empirical refutation but a critical evidentiary gap in the newest directly aligned work: [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) specifies the proposed generic-assessor design and held-out LLM evaluation, but the supplied excerpt reports no quantitative outcomes, no AUC values, no baseline deltas, no minimum reference size, no classifier choice, and no statistical significance tests. Therefore it does not substantiate the plan's central claim that the generic assessor outperforms simpler alternatives such as random reference selection, reference-only assessors, pooled-data-only assessors, or LLM-specific full-data assessors. A second caution comes from the earlier cross-LLM assessor work, [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a), whose feature ablation found that system id or parameter count improved performance significantly, while shot count, prompt-template id, model family, and sparsity had smaller effects. This suggests that LLM-specific information can be highly informative, but it was operationalized through system identifiers and parameter counts rather than the proposed small reference-instance success vectors, and the paper did not test random-reference, reference-only, or LLM-specific full-data baselines; it also reported no statistical significance tests, had a text-table discrepancy in which the assessor was numerically better than GPT-3 self-estimation but described as comparable, and provided no exact BIG-G values. A third, weaker contradiction is that the older n-gram work, [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850), shows n-gram features are not universally optimal: the best character n-gram range depends on task and language, 2-grams were not informative, and the subword model can degrade semantic analogy performance for German and Italian, while also being slower at about 105k words/second/thread than the 145k words/second/thread baseline. This contradicts any assumption that n-gram frequencies or a fixed 3-6 range will automatically be the best instance feature for all reasoning scenarios.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to make the proposed baseline comparison empirically decisive by reporting AUC, confidence intervals, and significance tests for the generic assessor against each named baseline: LLM-specific full-data assessors, random reference selection, reference-only assessors, and pooled-data-only assessors without LLM-specific features, following the held-out LLM and instance split design in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970). The second priority is to add calibration and operational metrics beyond AUC, motivated by [(Zhou et al., unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb3c188768fd4481bf83353caa94eb1f65f0d30a), including Brier score with calibration/refinement decomposition, reliability curves, and reject-rule analyses at multiple predicted-success thresholds, because that work showed Brier differences were driven mainly by calibration and that a 1 percent threshold could reject 21.7 percent of instances while catching about 46 percent of failures with only 1.5 percent correct-answer rejection. The third priority is to treat instance features as an ablation rather than a fixed choice, using the evidence from [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) to test n-gram ranges, subword features, OpenAI embeddings, Word2Vec, and FastText separately, and to report per-dataset or per-reasoning-type performance because n-gram usefulness was task- and language-dependent. Finally, to improve reproducibility, specify the exact reference sizes 30, 100, 300, and 1000, selection methods K-means, Factor Analysis, IRT, and random; classifier hyperparameters for logistic regression L1/L2 and XGBoost; class-imbalance handling; order-matched or LLM-held-out splits; and computational constraints, while avoiding reliance on unavailable system identifiers or parameter counts as primary LLM features.

## Context: The plan needs a metric comparable across scenarios and class distributions. Method: We will use Area Under the Curve (AUC) as our primary metric, as it allows comparison across different scenarios and class distributions while being insensitive to monotonic transformations.

### Support

The strongest support for using AUC as the primary metric comes from [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591), which evaluates LLM-generated answers by assigning binary correctness labels to sampled generations and computing AUROC between uncertainty/confidence scores and correctness across CoQA development split with 7,983 questions, Natural Questions validation split with 3,610 questions, and TriviaQA rc.nocontext validation split with 9,960 deduplicated questions. This work directly mirrors the proposed plan's binary LLM-instance correctness prediction setting, uses 20 generations by default, repeats experiments 10 times with a random 1,000-question calibration set, and tests OPT-13B, LLaMA-13B, LLaMA2, and gpt-3.5-turbo, making it the most detailed evidence that AUROC is a practical threshold-free metric for ranking correctness predictors in LLM evaluation. It also evaluates multiple score families, including set-size NumSet, graph-based Degree, Eccentricity, and Eigenvalue measures built from Jaccard, NLI entailment, or NLI contradiction similarities, ROUGE-L, semantic entropy, and self-reported probability of truth, which supports the proposed plan's need for a metric that can compare heterogeneous instance features and classifier outputs without requiring calibrated probabilities. The second strongest support is [(Kuhn et al., 2023-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/507465f8d46489a68a527cb5304d76bdb6c31ed9), which uses AUROC to evaluate semantic entropy for free-form question answering on CoQA development split of about 8,000 questions and an 8,000-question TriviaQA subset, with OPT 2.7B, 6.7B, 13B, and 30B models used in pretrained form without fine-tuning, ensembling, or Bayesian modification, and correctness defined by fuzzy matching with Rouge-L greater than 0.3. Although the excerpt does not provide numeric AUROC values, it explicitly argues that AUROC is preferred over calibration metrics such as Brier score for free-form text and that perfect uncertainty scoring is 1 while random scoring is 0.5, which aligns closely with the proposed rationale that AUC is comparable across scenarios and does not require calibrated probabilities. The third support is [(Çakar et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/95113665ab756f40afefe92dd4b1376525d1d279), which provides quantitative AUC evidence in an imbalanced binary classification setting, reporting CatBoost accuracy of 0.80 and AUC of 0.79 under oversampling, using 36 participants, 1,365 initial observations, 64 fNIRS features from 16 optodes sampled at 2 Hz, and a participant-level stratified hold-out with 987 training and 195 test observations. This is less directly relevant because the domain is fNIRS loan-offer acceptance rather than LLMs, but it supports the claim that AUC is useful when class distributions are imbalanced and when independent test splits are required. The weakest but still relevant support is [(Chen et al., 2024-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2845dce9ac7c408c63263ce13b2df248c5f6b362), which uses AUC to evaluate whether entropy-based uncertainty scores predict correctness for black-box LLM molecular chemistry tasks, including SMILES generation with Tanimoto similarity, hierarchical clustering, invalid SMILES assigned near-zero similarity epsilon, and GPT-4 prompt rephrasing with up to n alternative SMILES variants and m repeated responses. This supports the threshold-free interpretation of AUC in LLM correctness prediction, but the excerpt does not report numeric AUC values, baseline comparisons, statistical significance, concrete m and n values, dataset sizes, or hardware specifications, and the task-specific chemistry pipeline is less directly connected to the proposed generic cross-LLM assessor.

### Contradictions

The strongest limitation is that the related work does not show that AUC alone is sufficient for the proposed high-stakes use case. [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591) introduces AUARC as an alternative metric in which high-uncertainty samples are rejected and the accuracy of the remaining samples is measured, with oracle and random/base-accuracy reference points. This suggests that AUC, while useful for ranking correctness predictors, may not capture selective prediction performance, abstention quality, or the cost of acting on incorrect LLM outputs, which are central to the research plan's goal of predicting whether an LLM will succeed before executing subsequent actions. The second strongest limitation is that AUC can mask subgroup and imbalance effects. [(Çakar et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/95113665ab756f40afefe92dd4b1376525d1d279) reports an overall CatBoost AUC of 0.79 but also reports subgroup accuracies that vary widely, such as females 0.98 versus males 0.63, younger participants 0.86 versus older participants 0.75, and high school graduates 0.81 versus bachelor's degree holders 0.80. Although the domain is fNIRS, this demonstrates that a single global AUC may hide performance disparities that would matter if the generic assessor is deployed across different LLM families, scenarios, or user populations. The third limitation is that AUC comparability depends on how binary correctness is constructed. [(Kuhn et al., 2023-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/507465f8d46489a68a527cb5304d76bdb6c31ed9) defines correctness for free-form answers using fuzzy matching with Rouge-L greater than 0.3, while [(Chen et al., 2024-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2845dce9ac7c408c63263ce13b2df248c5f6b362) uses accuracy for classification tasks and exact-match accuracy for SMILES-generation tasks. If the proposed plan uses HELM-Lite binary metrics and KindsOfReasoning datasets with different answer formats, AUC values may not be directly comparable unless the correctness labels, matching thresholds, and scenario-level definitions are standardized. The weakest limitation is that none of the related works directly validates AUC for a generic assessor trained on a small reference set to predict a held-out LLM. [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591) and [(Kuhn et al., 2023-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/507465f8d46489a68a527cb5304d76bdb6c31ed9) evaluate uncertainty or confidence scores for specific models and datasets, and [(Chen et al., 2024-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2845dce9ac7c408c63263ce13b2df248c5f6b362) evaluates task-specific uncertainty scores, so the evidence supports the metric but not the broader cross-LLM transfer claim. Additionally, the excerpts for [(Kuhn et al., 2023-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/507465f8d46489a68a527cb5304d76bdb6c31ed9), [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591), and [(Chen et al., 2024-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2845dce9ac7c408c63263ce13b2df248c5f6b362) do not provide numeric AUC outcomes or statistical significance in the supplied text, limiting the strength of the empirical support.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to add selective-prediction metrics alongside AUC, especially AUARC or accuracy at a fixed rejection rate, because [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591) shows that AUC is often complemented by measuring accuracy after rejecting high-uncertainty samples and comparing against oracle and random/base-accuracy references. This would make the proposed generic assessor more aligned with high-stakes deployment, where the system may abstain or defer rather than execute an action. The second most important action is to report AUC with uncertainty estimates and repeated evaluation, following the protocol in [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591), which repeats experiments 10 times and uses a random 1,000-question calibration set; the proposed plan should therefore use multiple random seeds, report mean AUC with confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals, and state statistical significance or effect sizes when comparing reference selection methods, instance features, and classifiers. The third action is to enforce LLM-level and instance-level independent splits analogous to the participant-level stratified hold-out in [(Çakar et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/95113665ab756f40afefe92dd4b1376525d1d279), which used 987 training and 195 test observations from participants not seen during training; the proposed plan should ensure that test LLMs and test instances do not leak into training, validation, or reference selection, and should stratify by scenario, provider, and correctness class. The fourth action is to explicitly handle class imbalance and subgroup performance, because [(Çakar et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/95113665ab756f40afefe92dd4b1376525d1d279) compares original, undersampling, and oversampling conditions and reports subgroup accuracy disparities; the proposed plan should report class distributions for HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning, use stratified splits or class-weighted logistic regression and XGBoost where appropriate, and report subgroup AUC or balanced accuracy by scenario, reasoning type, LLM family, and correctness rate. The fifth action is to standardize binary correctness labels and report threshold sensitivity, because [(Kuhn et al., 2023-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/507465f8d46489a68a527cb5304d76bdb6c31ed9) uses fuzzy Rouge-L greater than 0.3 while [(Chen et al., 2024-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2845dce9ac7c408c63263ce13b2df248c5f6b362) uses exact-match or task-specific accuracy; the proposed plan should document how each scenario's binary metric is derived, test alternative matching thresholds for free-form tasks, and avoid comparing AUC across scenarios with incompatible label definitions. The sixth action is to include uncertainty-score baselines such as semantic entropy, predictive entropy, length-normalized predictive entropy, p(True), ROUGE-L, NumSet, Degree, Eccentricity, and Eigenvalue measures, as evaluated in [(Kuhn et al., 2023-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/507465f8d46489a68a527cb5304d76bdb6c31ed9) and [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591), to show whether the generic assessor adds value over simpler per-instance or per-LLM uncertainty estimates. The least important but still useful action is to document computational and API constraints, because [(Lin et al., 2023-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad934a9344f68fcc0b9aa704102aa48c39c5b591) uses gpt-3.5-turbo via API without specifying hardware or cost, and the proposed evaluation of all instruction-tuned OpenAI models on 37,529 KindsOfReasoning instances may require careful budgeting, caching, and rate-limit management.

## Context: The plan needs to identify the minimum reference set and best configuration. Method: We will systematically test different numbers of reference instances (30, 100, 300, 1000) and various combinations of instance features, reference selection methods, and base classifiers.

### Support

The strongest support comes from the closest related work, [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which implements the same generic instance-level assessor framework as the proposed plan: it defines prompt-target pairs with binary correctness labels, selects a reference subset from training instances, forms an LLM-specific binary success vector on that reference set, concatenates it with intrinsic prompt features, and trains a classifier to predict correctness for held-out LLMs and instances. This newest instance-level framework builds on the older data and feature ablation of [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) and the intermediate IRT active-testing pipeline of [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2), making the related work a coherent iterative line from word-vector feature and data ablation to LLM subset evaluation to instance-level generic assessment. The Pacchiardi paper also uses the same two dataset collections named in the plan, HELM-Lite with 4,285 instances across 6 scenarios and 30 LLMs, and KindsOfReasoning with 22 reasoning datasets totaling 37,529 instances evaluated on instruction-tuned OpenAI models from text-ada-001 to gpt-4-0125-preview, and it uses random and out-of-distribution instance splits plus LLM splits that separate test LLMs from training and validation LLMs. This directly supports the proposed three-component method, the use of reference-instance performance as LLM characterization, and the evaluation design for binary correctness prediction, although the excerpt does not report AUC values. The next strongest support is [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2), which provides a newer IRT and active-testing pipeline for estimating LLM performance from a small subset of benchmark examples, using anchor points, IRT item parameters, a direct weighted sample estimator, a p-IRT ability estimator, and a gp-IRT variance-bias combination, with the direct estimator variance divided by four when anchor points are used. This supports the proposed inclusion of IRT as a reference-instance selection method and the broader goal of reducing reference-instance count, especially because it explicitly considers random and date-based LLM splits to capture distribution shift. The weakest but still relevant support is the older [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850), which tests nested training-data fractions of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, and 50 percent and character n-gram ranges from 2 to 6 for 300-dimensional word vectors, showing that a subword model can saturate with small data and that n-gram feature configuration matters. Although it does not evaluate LLMs, reference selection, or AUC, it supports the proposed ablation over reference-instance counts and n-gram frequency features by demonstrating that data sufficiency and feature-range choices should be empirically tested rather than assumed.

### Contradictions

The strongest challenge is that the closest related work, [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), does not provide quantitative evidence that the proposed reference counts of 30, 100, 300, and 1000 are sufficient, nor does it identify the minimum useful reference set or the best combination of instance features, reference selection method, and classifier. The excerpt reports no AUC values, accuracy values, reference-set-size comparisons, statistical significance, baseline comparisons, classifier hyperparameters, exact feature dimensions, reference-instance selection algorithms, or hardware, so the proposed plan's central ablation remains unvalidated by the most directly related framework. The next strongest contradiction is that [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2) uses IRT and active testing to estimate aggregate benchmark performance from anchor points, not to train an instance-level classifier that predicts correctness for individual prompts using text features and LLM-specific reference success vectors. Its p-IRT and gp-IRT estimators are designed for model-level ability estimation, and the excerpt reports no quantitative outcomes, so using IRT as a drop-in reference-selection method for instance-level AUC prediction is not directly supported and may require adaptation. The weakest but still relevant contradiction comes from [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850), which shows that n-gram feature configuration is task and language dependent: the default 3-6 range is not universally optimal, German GUR350 improves from 57 with 2-2 to 71 with 4-6 or 5-6, English semantic analogies prefer 5-5 or 6-6, and no automatic validation procedure was used. This suggests that n-gram frequency features in the proposed instance-feature set may be unstable if fixed without validation, and the paper's lack of statistical significance tests and its nested data fractions, which increase out-of-vocabulary rates as data shrinks, warn against overinterpreting small-data or feature-range ablations without proper controls.

### Suggested Action

Most importantly, the proposed method should explicitly report the missing quantitative ablation that the closest related work does not provide: test reference-instance counts of 30, 100, 300, and 1000 on HELM-Lite and KindsOfReasoning, using the planned 56 percent, 14 percent, and 30 percent instance splits and LLM splits where test LLMs are substantially different from training LLMs, and report AUC with bootstrap confidence intervals, per-scenario results, aggregate results, statistical significance tests, and a saturation curve that defines the minimum reference set as the smallest count whose AUC is within a pre-registered epsilon of the 1000-reference assessor. This should compare K-means, Factor Analysis, IRT, and random reference selection against the planned baselines, including LLM-specific assessors trained on full evaluation data, random reference selection, assessors using only reference-instance performance, and assessors using only pooled training data without LLM-specific features. A second priority is to adapt the IRT and active-testing evidence from [(Polo et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4c0f88e80320885e8289a9af781a1101717104f2) to the instance-level setting: use IRT anchor points and item parameters estimated from training LLMs, evaluate whether IRT-selected references improve instance-level AUC over K-means, Factor Analysis, and random selection, and include a date-based LLM split for the OpenAI instruction-tuned models to test distribution shift. A third priority is to address the n-gram feature sensitivity shown by [(Bojanowski et al., 2016-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e2dba792360873aef125572812f3673b1a85d850) by ablating n-gram ranges such as 2-2, 3-3, 3-6, 4-6, 5-6, and 6-6 for prompt n-gram frequency features, selecting ranges on the validation LLM and instance split rather than assuming a default, and reporting out-of-vocabulary behavior and computational overhead. Finally, the plan should add reproducibility and robustness controls: report classifier hyperparameters for logistic regression with L1 and L2 penalties and XGBoost, feature dimensions for OpenAI embeddings, Word2Vec, FastText, and n-gram frequencies, random seeds, hardware and training time, and out-of-distribution splits where test datasets come from different reasoning types or domains, so that the claimed minimum reference set and best configuration are supported by granular, statistically rigorous evidence.

## Context: The plan needs to assess robustness to task distribution shift. Method: We will evaluate robustness by creating OOD splits where test datasets come from different reasoning types or domains than training data.

### Support

The strongest support is methodological and comes from [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), which uses the same two collections named in the plan, HELM-Lite with 4,285 instances and 30 LLMs and KindsOfReasoning with 22 datasets, 37,529 instances, and 14 OpenAI instruction-tuned models, and constructs dataset-level OOD splits in which D_test is held out while D_train and D_val are drawn from the remaining datasets; its generic assessor also combines prompt-intrinsic features with LLM-specific reference-instance successes, making it the closest prior implementation of the proposed OOD robustness test, although the excerpt reports no OOD AUC. The strongest empirical support for domain-shift OOD splits is [(Yang et al., 2022-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d15091e73f7295ba8c0bdbabe0b7188307c96039), whose GLUE-X benchmark keeps GLUE training data but replaces test sets with 15 domain-shifted datasets and shows an average ID-to-OOD accuracy decay of 20.05 percent across 19 models, with ELECTRA-large falling from 89.18 to 74.62, T5-large from 87.70 to 72.81, BERT-base from 82.08 to 64.10, DistilBERT-base from 80.21 to 61.94, and GPT-2 from 79.30 to 61.16, while ALBERT-base reaches 65.30 OOD accuracy and Friedman ranks remain relatively stable. Next, [(Fatemi et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9283b8c7e6ad6ae86be059a26595de0d7a427a10) provides direct evidence that LLM task accuracy changes when the input distribution changes, using GraphQA tasks across Erdős-Rényi, complete, path, and star graph generators and cross-generator few-shot examples; cycle check drops from 91.7 percent on complete graphs to 5.9 percent on path graphs, improves to 19.7 percent with mixed cyclic and acyclic few-shot examples, and edge existence is 60.0 percent on path graphs but 19.8 percent on complete graphs. [(Søgaard et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d0fcdf47561ff742c9a72495102f16646eca43b7) supports the need for OOD-style splits by showing that random and standard splits underestimated error on new samples by more than 20 percent in several of seven NLP tasks, that a BERT model reduced 80 percent of random-baseline error under random splitting but only 45 percent on average across five new samples in PROBING-BSHIFT, and that heuristic and Wasserstein-based splits can still be optimistic. [(Nie et al., 2019-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/207da6d2c07289bf72a2b5974bb3f011ebb5dd0d) supports the feasibility of defining OOD conditions by reasoning type through a six-category ontology applied to 500 verified development examples per ANLI round, with numerical and quantitative reasoning at 38, 32, and 10 or 17 percent across rounds and outside-knowledge reasoning at 53, 59, and 63 percent, although it analyzes verified errors rather than full test distributions. [(Godbole, and Jia, 2022-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/49aec6fb44ab52181960512a6067eded0ce4182b) supports constructed harder splits by showing that likelihood-based splits lower T5-base SPIDER exact-match accuracy by 12.6 points relative to random splits, remain 7.3 and 5.1 points lower under length-controlled variants, and lower ELECTRA-large SNLI and BOOLQ accuracy by 8.4 and 2.9 points, while GPT2-large likelihood scores correlate at Pearson r 0.96 for fine-tuned and 0.99 for pre-trained models. Weaker support comes from [(Gehrmann et al., 2022-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4e9d556e9725a5fdb2e133b61243ff7c1ca8aeb), whose audit of 20 summarization papers found 27 dataset evaluations concentrated on CNN/DM five times and XSum four times with only two non-English datasets, motivating broader domain coverage, and from [(Scao et al., 2022-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/964bd39b546f0f6625ff3b9ef1083f797807ef2e), which evaluates held-out multilingual tasks after xP3 finetuning over 83 datasets, 46 languages, and 16 tasks, but reports only qualitative improvements and random baselines of 33 percent for NLI and 50 percent for coreference resolution.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that constructed OOD splits are not automatically reliable proxies for real distribution shift. [(Søgaard et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d0fcdf47561ff742c9a72495102f16646eca43b7) found that random and standard splits consistently underestimated error on new samples, with absolute differences in error reduction often exceeding 20 percent, and that heuristic longer-than-training splits were more optimistic than new samples in 5 of 7 tasks while approximate Wasserstein adversarial splits were more optimistic in 4 of 7 tasks; it also reports a misleading ranking effect in POS tagging where removing the CRF inference layer gave 0.952 on random splits and 0.930 on a new sample, with the new-sample result reported as significantly better than the corresponding CRF-layer result. This directly cautions against treating the proposed reasoning-type or domain OOD AUC as sufficient evidence of robustness. The next strongest limitation is that the closest direct work, [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970), does not report quantitative OOD AUC, baseline comparisons, or significance values, and the excerpt does not specify whether the held-out datasets are selected by explicit reasoning type or domain, so it supports the split design but does not confirm that dataset-level OOD testing improves the generic assessor. [(Godbole, and Jia, 2022-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/49aec6fb44ab52181960512a6067eded0ce4182b) contradicts the assumption that any constructed OOD split cleanly measures target distribution shift, because likelihood splits can be confounded by length and scorer choice: length-controlled variants remain harder than random splits, GPT2-large changes evaluation sets by 16 percent for ll_split and 10 percent for ll_split pt, and T5-base accuracy drops an additional 4.2 percent on GPT2-large ll_split. [(Nie et al., 2019-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/207da6d2c07289bf72a2b5974bb3f011ebb5dd0d) provides a weaker but important caveat that reasoning-type labels may be noisy and biased if derived from verified model errors: only 500 examples per round are annotated, examples may receive multiple tags, ambiguous or debatable pairs are flagged at 3 percent per round, and the reported round-3 numerical-reasoning value is inconsistent between the table and text, 10 percent versus 17 percent. [(Yang et al., 2022-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d15091e73f7295ba8c0bdbabe0b7188307c96039) is less contradictory but still limits extrapolation because it evaluates static GLUE-style tasks with 19 pretrained models, does not report confidence intervals or significance tests, discretizes SICK similarity labels to five classes, and does not train a generic LLM-instance assessor. [(Scao et al., 2022-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/964bd39b546f0f6625ff3b9ef1083f797807ef2e) provides only weak contradictory evidence because it lacks exact held-out task accuracies, statistical tests, and explicit OOD splits by reasoning type or domain, and [(Gehrmann et al., 2022-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4e9d556e9725a5fdb2e133b61243ff7c1ca8aeb) is descriptive only, with no model performance comparison or statistical test, so it motivates domain diversity but does not validate the proposed OOD evaluation.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to operationalize the OOD split as an explicit reasoning-type or domain holdout rather than an unspecified dataset-level split, and to validate it against genuinely held-out tasks or new samples. For KindsOfReasoning, the plan should define train, validation, and test sets by the stated reasoning categories, such as logical, common sense, inductive, deductive, abductive, counterfactual, causal, analogical, spatial, and arithmetic reasoning, and for HELM-Lite it should map the 6 scenarios to domain or task-type labels; this follows the direct design in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) and the domain-shift template in [(Yang et al., 2022-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d15091e73f7295ba8c0bdbabe0b7188307c96039), while addressing the optimism and ranking instability shown by [(Søgaard et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d0fcdf47561ff742c9a72495102f16646eca43b7). The second priority is to report OOD AUC together with ID-to-OOD decay, ranking stability, and uncertainty, because [(Yang et al., 2022-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d15091e73f7295ba8c0bdbabe0b7188307c96039) shows 20.05 percent average decay and rank changes, and [(Søgaard et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d0fcdf47561ff742c9a72495102f16646eca43b7) shows that aggregate performance can mislead rankings; the plan should therefore compare the generic assessor against LLM-specific assessors, random reference selection, reference-only, and pooled-only baselines under the same OOD splits, and report confidence intervals, significance tests, and Friedman or equivalent rank comparisons across LLMs and scenarios. Third, the method should control for confounds that can make OOD splits harder or easier for reasons unrelated to reasoning type, including sequence length, token count, likelihood, dataset difficulty, and label distribution, using length-controlled or likelihood-controlled variants and covariate checks inspired by [(Godbole, and Jia, 2022-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/49aec6fb44ab52181960512a6067eded0ce4182b) and the heuristic length splits in [(Søgaard et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d0fcdf47561ff742c9a72495102f16646eca43b7). Fourth, the reasoning-type labels should be made explicit and quality-controlled, using an ontology similar to [(Nie et al., 2019-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/207da6d2c07289bf72a2b5974bb3f011ebb5dd0d), with multiple tags allowed, ambiguity flags, and annotation statistics, so that OOD splits are not based on noisy or verified-error-only distributions. Fifth, the reference-instance selection ablations should test whether reference instances from the same or different reasoning types or domains improve OOD AUC, because [(Fatemi et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9283b8c7e6ad6ae86be059a26595de0d7a427a10) shows cross-generator few-shot examples can partially mitigate distribution shift, for example improving path-graph cycle check from 5.9 to 19.7 percent; the plan should compare random, K-means, Factor Analysis, and IRT selection at 30, 100, 300, and 1000 reference instances under both in-distribution and OOD conditions. Finally, the paper should report full reproducibility details missing from the closest prior work, including exact OOD split ratios, dataset-level holdout criteria, classifier hyperparameters for logistic regression and XGBoost, instance feature representations, LLM split definitions, hardware or compute constraints, and quantitative OOD AUC values, as motivated by the incomplete reporting in [(Pacchiardi et al., 2024-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/957610a39fc7dba4110baa7d112625dc9ec62970) and the narrow dataset concentration documented by [(Gehrmann et al., 2022-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4e9d556e9725a5fdb2e133b61243ff7c1ca8aeb). A lower-priority action is to include held-out task types or multilingual domains where available, following [(Scao et al., 2022-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/964bd39b546f0f6625ff3b9ef1083f797807ef2e), but this is less central than reasoning-type OOD validation and reporting.

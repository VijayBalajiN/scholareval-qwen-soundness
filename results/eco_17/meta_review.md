## Context: To test whether optical near-infrared insect sensors can automate measurement of an insect biodiversity metric. Method: We will deploy autonomous near-infrared sensors alongside conventional sampling methods (Malaise traps and sweep nets) in agricultural fields across Kansas, USA.

### Support

The related work forms an iterative sequence from controlled optical feature extraction to long-term field operation to multimodal classification, and the strongest support for the proposed Kansas deployment comes from [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f), which operated a 5 mW 980 nm continuous-wave laser over a 36 m mowed-vegetation path, used a 950-1000 nm bandpass filter, a 90 kHz silicon photodiode, and a 16-bit digitizer at 30,517 Hz, and ran for 148 consecutive days with downtime below 6.5 percent in a semi-urban field. It detected insect passages as 20 ms to 1 s signal drops, applied a 10-900 Hz bandpass filter, a 5 s sliding mean plus 2.5 standard deviation threshold, and harmonic-series confirmation to estimate wingbeat frequency, directly supporting the proposed use of near-infrared field sensors and WBF extraction in agricultural settings. The next strongest support is [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b), which recorded 427 valid mosquito transits with a 1320 nm 3.6 W laser, a 67 kHz InGaAs detector, and 250 MS/s digitization, separated a Gaussian body backscatter component from a periodic wing modulation, extracted wingbeat frequencies from 200 to 900 Hz, and achieved 96.5 percent overall gender accuracy with a Naive Bayes classifier using WBF alone. This supports the proposed extraction of WBF and body-to-wing ratio from backscattered time signals, although it used a controlled single-insect tube, a high-power laser, and a 1320 nm wavelength rather than 810 nm and 970 nm LEDs in 5 to 70 L field volumes. The weakest but still relevant support is [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949), which showed that wingbeat-only signals could support insect classification with a linear support vector machine at 0.68 accuracy, although the study was a small proof-of-concept, did not use near-infrared light, did not estimate body-to-wing ratio, did not use DBSCAN, and did not compare with Malaise traps or sweep nets.

### Contradictions

Read as an iterative sequence, the strongest contradiction is that WBF-based discrimination becomes weak when many species or species-gender classes are considered: [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b) reported only 62.3 percent overall accuracy for a six-class species/gender task, with class accuracies from 36.2 to 88.6 percent, and attributed the decline to overlap among WBF distributions as the number of classes increased. This challenges the proposed assumption that DBSCAN clusters of WBF and body-to-wing ratio can serve as a reliable proxy for species richness across diverse agricultural insect communities. The next strongest contradiction is that wingbeat-only performance can be substantially lower than richer modalities: [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949) achieved 0.68 accuracy with a wingbeat-only linear support vector machine versus 0.92 accuracy for camera-only MobileNet, with no fused camera-plus-wingbeat result reported and a small proof-of-concept dataset, suggesting that WBF and body-to-wing features alone may be insufficient for robust species-level clustering. A further, less direct contradiction is that field near-infrared signal-derived physical estimates can be error-prone for small insects: [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f) reported a worst-case dry-mass relative error of 113 percent for mosquitoes, corresponding to about a 1 mg overestimate, while bumblebees had a 3.2 percent relative difference, and the study did not test DBSCAN, species richness, Shannon or Simpson indices, or correlation with Malaise traps or sweep nets. Additional limitations relevant to the proposed plan are that the 2018 laboratory system observed no simultaneous multi-insect events among 427 transits but explicitly noted that such events would need to be flagged, whereas field sensors in Kansas may encounter overlapping insects; the 2023 field system used a 980 nm laser rather than 810 nm and 970 nm LEDs and a 30,517 Hz sampling rate rather than the proposed LED backscatter setup; and discarding observations without clearly identified wingbeats or body-to-wing ratios may bias richness estimates toward detectable, cooperative, or larger insects.

### Suggested Action

To make the proposed method consistent with this iterative evidence, the most important improvement is to validate DBSCAN cluster count as a biodiversity proxy against taxonomic identity and stability rather than relying only on Spearman correlation with conventional methods. The plan should use the 70 percent testing set, nested cross-validation, multiple random 30/70 splits, and report cluster-count distributions, epsilon and min_samples sensitivity, silhouette or stability metrics, and comparisons with HDBSCAN, k-means, or Gaussian mixture models, while avoiding tuning DBSCAN solely to maximize Spearman correlation on the optimization set. The second priority is to enrich and calibrate the optical features: add event duration, amplitude, harmonic structure, body extinction or mass proxy, and multi-insect flags, and calibrate body-to-wing ratio and mass-like features against known species using controlled flights and 0.1 mg scale measurements as in [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f) and [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b). The third priority is to align sensor and conventional sampling temporally and spatially: use 24-hour windows matched to Malaise traps, distance bands matched to sweep-net transects, rarefaction or effort-standardized richness, and mixed-effects models that include crop, month, field, weather, sensor downtime, and detection volume as covariates rather than only ANOVA and TukeyHSD. The fourth priority is to test the specific 810 nm and 970 nm LED backscatter system in field conditions, including signal-to-noise, 5 to 70 L measurement volumes, multi-insect separation, false positives, and species-level confusion matrices, using harmonic-series detection and high-speed digitization similar to [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f). A final, less critical but useful action is to document hardware specifications, power consumption, cloud neural-network thresholds, and reproducibility details so that the proposed autonomous sensor can be compared with the controlled and field systems in the related work.

## Context: To acquire raw optical signals from flying insects. Method: The sensors use light-emitting diodes to transmit infrared light (810 nm & 970 nm), creating a measurement volume between 5 and 70 L, depending on insect size. Insects flying in front of the sensor back-scatter light, which is recorded by a photodiode as a time signal.

### Support

The strongest support is that [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949) demonstrates that optical insect signals, particularly wingbeat-related signals, can be acquired from live insects and used for automated classification, which is directly relevant to the proposed plan's reliance on wing-beat frequency (WBF) as a discriminative optical feature. In their controlled 60 x 45 x 20 mm arena, insects passed photoelectric lock-in amplifier light barriers, triggered LED illumination and a 500 microsecond flash with a 23.5 millisecond exposure, and were recorded by a 10 MP teleobjective camera at f/8 with approximately 10 micrometer resolution; a wingbeat-only linear support vector machine achieved 0.68 accuracy, while a camera-only MobileNet achieved 0.92 on a small test set. This supports the broader hypothesis that optical wingbeat information contains species-relevant variation, although the 0.68 wingbeat-only result is only moderate and does not establish that a simple photodiode time signal can support the proposed biodiversity metric. A second, weaker but still relevant support is the paper's data-fusion architecture, which explicitly accepts camera-only, wingbeat-only, or combined inputs, suggesting that combining optical wingbeat signals with other modalities may improve identification; however, the authors did not report a combined accuracy, so this support remains conceptual. The weakest support is the controlled, nonlethal laboratory proof-of-concept itself: it shows that small-scale optical insect sensing hardware can be built around light barriers, LED illumination, and photodiode-like photoelectric detection, but it does not use 810 nm or 970 nm backscatter, does not define a 5 to 70 L measurement volume, and does not validate field deployment in Kansas agricultural fields.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the related work does not test the proposed sensor modality: it uses a 10 MP camera, LED flash, and optoacoustic wingbeat sensor rather than 810 nm and 970 nm LEDs with a photodiode recording backscatter as a time signal, and it does not characterize a 5 to 70 L measurement volume or insect-size-dependent backscatter. Therefore, it provides no direct evidence that the proposed raw optical time signals will contain sufficient WBF and body-to-wing ratio (BWR) information for clustering. The next major contradiction is that the paper does not extract WBF or BWR, does not apply DBSCAN, and does not use cluster count as a proxy for species richness; instead it classifies insects with MobileNet or a linear support vector machine, so it does not validate the proposed biodiversity-metric pipeline. A further contradiction is the absence of field biodiversity validation: the experiment was a laboratory proof of concept with a small multisensor dataset, and the authors did not report correlations with Malaise traps, sweep nets, species richness, Shannon or Simpson indices, statistical significance, confidence intervals, sample sizes, species composition, wingbeat signal length, feature extraction details, support vector machine hyperparameters, MobileNet training details, or DBSCAN parameters. This limits the ability to generalize to the proposed 20-site, two-season, crop-type field design. Finally, the wingbeat-only linear support vector machine accuracy of 0.68, without reported significance or combined camera-plus-wingbeat performance, contradicts the assumption that wingbeat-derived optical features alone will reliably support automated richness estimation, especially when the wingbeat convolutional neural network was not evaluated because the dataset was too small.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to add a dedicated validation stage for the proposed 810 nm and 970 nm photodiode backscatter sensor before or alongside the full field experiment: collect labeled insects in a controlled arena or field cage, record photodiode time signals, and explicitly quantify WBF and BWR extraction accuracy, measurement-volume calibration, and cluster validity against known species or morphospecies. This should include reporting sample sizes, species composition, signal length, noise levels, and extraction error, and should compare DBSCAN cluster counts to conventional richness using Spearman correlations with confidence intervals and significance tests. The second priority is to adopt the related work's data-fusion and baseline-comparison logic by evaluating camera-only, wingbeat-only, and combined models, and by comparing DBSCAN with simpler baselines such as linear support vector machines, MobileNet, or one-dimensional time-series classifiers, so that the proposed clustering approach is not assumed to be superior without evidence. The third priority is to improve reproducibility and statistical rigor by documenting hardware specifications, LED wavelengths, photodiode gain, sampling rate, arena or field geometry, DBSCAN epsilon and min_samples tuning procedure, stochastic gradient descent optimization, 30/70 split, cross-validation, and ANOVA/TukeyHSD assumptions. The least critical but still useful action is to pilot the sensor in a small number of Kansas agricultural fields with concurrent Malaise traps and sweep nets to test whether the 5 to 70 L measurement volume and continuous three-day monitoring produce detectable seasonal, crop, and field effects before scaling to all 20 sites.

## Context: To convert optical backscatter signals into biologically meaningful insect traits. Method: We will extract wing-beat frequency (WBF) and body-to-wing ratio (BWR) from the optical sensor data.

### Support

The strongest support for extracting wing-beat frequency and a body-to-wing ratio from optical backscatter comes from [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b), which used a continuous-wave 1320 nm laser, a 2 cm FWHM beam, an InGaAs amplified photodetector with 67 kHz DC bandwidth, and a 16-bit digitizer at 250 MS/s with 125 MHz bandwidth to record 427 single-mosquito transits in a controlled 1.25 m by 12.5 cm Plexiglas tube. The study modeled the background-subtracted backscatter as a slowly varying body component plus a rapidly oscillating wing component, interpolated local minima to estimate body backscatter, subtracted that body estimate to isolate wing modulation, and used a Fourier transform to extract fundamental wing-beat frequencies between 200 and 900 Hz. This directly supports the proposed WBF extraction from near-infrared backscatter and provides a concrete body/wing signal-separation procedure that is conceptually analogous to deriving a body-to-wing ratio. The same study also showed that WBF is biologically informative: a Naive Bayes classifier using WBF alone achieved 95.8% accuracy for males, 97.6% accuracy for females, and 96.5% overall accuracy for sex classification. The newer multisensor work by [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949) provides weaker but complementary support by treating wingbeat signals as a biologically meaningful insect trait in a small arena with a 60 x 45 x 20 mm camera volume, 500 microsecond LED flash, 23.5 millisecond exposure, 10-megapixel camera, and optoacoustic wingbeat sensor. Its camera-only MobileNet achieved 0.92 test-set accuracy, demonstrating that insect physical traits can support classification, and its wingbeat-only linear support vector machine achieved 0.68 accuracy, showing that wingbeat-derived features can be usable even with limited data. Although Tschaikner et al. did not use optical backscatter or explicitly compute a body-to-wing ratio, the work supports the broader premise that WBF and body-related physical traits can be extracted from sensor signals and used for insect discrimination.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that WBF alone may be insufficient for species-richness estimation because [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b) reported substantial overlap among species and sex wing-beat frequency distributions: six-class species/gender classification accuracy was only 62.3%, with class accuracies ranging from 36.2% to 88.6%, despite high sex-classification accuracy. This suggests that clustering WBF in the field may merge multiple species or split one species, undermining the use of DBSCAN cluster count as a proxy for species richness. Genoud et al. also did not compute an explicit body-to-wing ratio, did not use DBSCAN, did not evaluate biodiversity metrics, and observed no simultaneous multi-mosquito transits among the 427 recorded events, so it does not validate the proposed multi-insect, field-deployed, 810 nm and 970 nm LED, or BWR-based pipeline. The newer work by [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949) further contradicts the feasibility of relying primarily on WBF: wingbeat-only linear support vector machine accuracy was only 0.68, while camera-only MobileNet accuracy was 0.92, and the wingbeat CNN was not evaluated because the available dataset was too small. No combined camera-plus-wingbeat accuracy was reported. This indicates that wingbeat features may be weak or data-limited compared with morphological visual features, and that small datasets can prevent robust model training. Tschaikner et al. also used optoacoustic sensing rather than optical backscatter, a small proof-of-concept dataset, a Raspberry Pi 4 minicomputer, and did not report dataset size, species count, statistical significance, DBSCAN clustering, or biodiversity metrics, so it provides limited direct evidence for the proposed optical backscatter BWR extraction or field richness estimation.

### Suggested Action

Most important, explicitly define and validate the body-to-wing ratio before using it for DBSCAN: adopt the body/wing separation approach from [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b) as a baseline, estimate the slowly varying body backscatter by interpolating local minima or using a robust low-pass fit, isolate the wing modulation, and compute BWR as a reproducible ratio such as body power to wing modulation amplitude, variance, or peak-to-peak power. Then test whether BWR improves separation over WBF-only on labeled single-insect data. Second, because Genoud et al. showed species overlap and [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949) showed wingbeat-only modest accuracy, evaluate WBF-only, BWR-only, and WBF-plus-BWR baselines using cluster purity, adjusted Rand index, F1 score, and confusion matrices against taxonomic labels, rather than relying only on Spearman correlation with conventional richness. Third, address field and hardware gaps by specifying 810 nm and 970 nm LED power, beam geometry, photodiode bandwidth, sampling rate, and signal-to-noise ratio, and benchmark the system against the higher-end controlled setup in Genoud et al., which used a 3.6 W 1320 nm laser, 67 kHz detector bandwidth, 250 MS/s digitization, and 125 MHz bandwidth. Include multi-insect, overlapping, low-SNR, and noisy recordings because Genoud et al. had no simultaneous transits and the proposed plan discards observations without clearly identified wingbeats or body-to-wing ratios. Fourth, use the multisensor lesson from Tschaikner et al. to consider adding morphological or image-based features, or at least using camera-validated subsets, and report dataset size, species count, and statistical significance. Finally, improve statistical validation by using nested cross-validation or site-level splits, permutation tests for Spearman correlations, confidence intervals, and ANOVA with TukeyHSD post hoc tests only after checking assumptions, while tuning DBSCAN epsilon and min_samples on the 30% optimization set without leaking test-site information.

## Context: To derive an automated proxy for species richness from optical insect traits. Method: We will apply the DBSCAN (Density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise) algorithm to cluster the optical data, using the number of clusters as a proxy for species richness.

### Support

As a later study, the strongest support for the proposed DBSCAN richness-proxy plan is that [(Rydhmer, and Selvan, 2021-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18bfc290cbfd1e6837a5dbe4a72140b3ff96bae3) demonstrates that near-infrared backscatter insect signals contain species-discriminative structure that can be clustered, using 808 and 975 nm wavelengths close to the proposed 810 and 970 nm LEDs, and that a learned dynamic beta-VAE latent space plus K-means achieved higher ARI and AMI than PCA, sigmoid Kernel-PCA, complete-linkage HCA, and a conventional VAE on 40,000 unlabelled field recordings and 6,000 labelled recordings from 12 species groups. This supports the core assumption that optical insect signals can be grouped into species-like clusters, although it does not directly validate DBSCAN or cluster-count richness. A second, weaker support is that the study used a CNN to remove non-insect recordings before clustering, analogous to the proposed proprietary cloud-based neural network, and used 193-bin log-transformed max-normalized Welch power spectra from 0 to 2 kHz, showing that frequency-domain WBF-related features are informative; the model was trained with Adam at learning rate 1e-3, batch size 256, 5000 epochs, and an Nvidia GTX 1050, indicating that such clustering is computationally feasible at field-deployment scale. A third, weaker support is that K was selected from 5 to 50 by maximum average silhouette score and semi-supervised clustering with 8 labelled species improved over unsupervised clustering, suggesting that cluster-count or cluster-structure choices can be optimized and that labels, if available, can improve reliability. The weakest support is qualitative because the excerpt does not report numeric ARI/AMI values, exact selected K, or statistical significance, so the magnitude of improvement over baselines cannot be quantified.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the related work does not test the proposed central claim that the number of DBSCAN clusters can serve as a proxy for species richness; it uses K-means in a 2D learned latent space, constrains K to 5-50, and evaluates cluster quality with ARI/AMI against labelled species rather than correlating cluster count with Malaise trap or sweep-net richness. This means the proposed DBSCAN cluster-count proxy remains unvalidated by this evidence. A second contradiction is that the related work clusters 193-bin spectral features rather than explicit WBF and body-to-wing ratio, so it does not support the proposed assumption that WBF and BWR alone are sufficient for species-like clustering; it instead suggests richer spectral representations may be needed. A third contradiction is that the related work shows representation and optimization sensitivity: a nonadaptive VAE had large run-to-run variation and frequent collapse, while the dynamic beta-VAE kept beta below 1 for most of training and used dynamic beta updates every 5 epochs with tuned parameters a=0.2, b=0.05, w1=w2=1.2, w3=0.9, and w4=1.1, implying that simple or unstable feature pipelines may produce inconsistent clusters, and the proposed DBSCAN tuning on a 30 percent optimization set may be vulnerable to similar instability if WBF/BWR features are noisy or nonstationary. A fourth contradiction is that the sensor and sampling context differ: the related work used a fixed 20 L measurement volume at 1 m, 20 kHz sampling, 0-5 kHz bandwidth, and 193 bins between 0 and 2 kHz, whereas the proposed plan uses variable 5 to 70 L measurement volumes depending on insect size and extracts WBF/BWR, so cluster comparability across sites and months may be affected by volume and signal normalization differences. The weakest contradiction is that the related work reports a reproducibility discrepancy in the semi-supervised gamma schedule, cycling between 0.01 and 0.2 in the parameter section but 0.01 and 2 in the discussion, and it omits numeric ARI/AMI, exact K, and significance, limiting confidence in the reported improvements.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to add a validation layer that directly tests whether cluster count, not just cluster quality, correlates with conventional richness: use a labelled subset or morphospecies identifications to compute ARI/AMI, silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz, and cluster-count correlation, and evaluate with site-level or temporal cross-validation to avoid leakage from the 20 sites and two sampling months. The next most important action is to improve the feature representation before DBSCAN by including the 193-bin log-transformed max-normalized Welch power spectra or another learned representation, such as a dynamic beta-VAE or stable autoencoder, alongside WBF and BWR, because the related work shows learned spectral features outperform PCA, Kernel-PCA, HCA, and conventional VAE baselines and that conventional VAEs can collapse. Then tune DBSCAN more robustly than relying only on Spearman correlation on a 30 percent optimization set: use k-distance, reachability plots, silhouette, stability across random seeds and subsamples, and nested optimization/testing splits, with epsilon and min_samples selected to maximize both ecological correlation and cluster stability. Also normalize for the proposed variable 5 to 70 L measurement volume by including volume, signal duration, amplitude, or distance as covariates or by standardizing features per site and month, and compare DBSCAN against K-means, Gaussian mixture models, HCA, and PCA/Kernel-PCA baselines to show that DBSCAN is not an arbitrary choice. Finally, report full reproducibility details, including hardware, wavelengths, sampling rate, bandwidth, number of recordings per site, random seeds, hyperparameters, and statistical significance, and if labels are available use a semi-supervised or supervised validation set analogous to the 8 labelled species and 4 held-out test species used in the related work.

## Context: To establish reference biodiversity estimates and verify conventional identifications. Method: For conventional methods, we will identify all collected specimens to the lowest possible taxonomic unit and calculate standard biodiversity metrics including species richness, Shannon index, and Simpson index. All specimens from conventional methods will be identified to species or morphospecies level, with voucher specimens maintained for verification.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed conventional reference method is methodological rather than empirical: [(Montgomery et al., 2021-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a326b1afaab1f003e6aefdc9b98d40858995a71b) explicitly frames standardized invertebrate monitoring around measurement goals, consistent sampling design, replication, location, taxonomic resolution, scalability, specimen and metadata curation, and benchmarking, which directly matches the plan's 20 Kansas agricultural sites, two sampling occasions in June and July 2020, Malaise traps, sweep-net transects, species or morphospecies identification, voucher specimens, and calculation of species richness, Shannon index, and Simpson index. This is the most relevant evidence because it endorses the exact design elements the plan uses to create a verifiable conventional baseline, although it reports no dataset, population size, sensor hardware, field site, sampling duration, hyperparameters, statistical model, quantitative outcomes, or baseline comparisons. The next strongest support is empirical but less directly aligned: [(Armando et al., 2022-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1412fadfe026f74fe7b94bd07c23d78bbad09625) demonstrates that conventional physical collection and laboratory taxonomic identification can generate biodiversity estimates in an agricultural system, using pitfall traps, swing nets, and direct observation in three 100 m by 100 m oil palm fields with five 100 m aisles spaced 9.4 m apart and 20 selected plants per field. It recovered 81 arthropod families overall, with pitfall traps yielding 37 families and 2010 individuals, swing nets yielding 57 families and 315 individuals, and direct observation yielding 30 families and 210 individuals, and it reported that swing net sampling had the highest diversity index. This supports the plausibility of using sweep-net-like sampling as a conventional reference in crops, but it is weaker support because it was conducted in 2015 in West Java, Indonesia, not Kansas, USA; it used pitfall traps and direct observation rather than Malaise traps; it identified mainly to orders and families rather than species or morphospecies; it did not report voucher specimens; and it did not calculate the proposed Shannon and Simpson indices or compare to optical near-infrared sensor data.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction, or at least the strongest evidence limiting the proposed method, is that the most recent empirical conventional study does not validate the proposed taxonomic resolution, trap combination, or metric set: [(Armando et al., 2022-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1412fadfe026f74fe7b94bd07c23d78bbad09625) reports family-level richness and a diversity index from pitfall traps, swing nets, and direct observation, with no species or morphospecies identification, no voucher specimen reporting, no Malaise trapping, and no Shannon or Simpson index. This matters because the plan's central comparison depends on a species or morphospecies-level conventional reference from Malaise traps and sweep nets; if existing agricultural arthropod baselines are often coarser and trap-specific, the proposed reference may be harder to reproduce, less comparable to prior work, and more sensitive to taxonomic expertise. A weaker contradiction is that neither related work provides quantitative evidence for the proposed optical-sensor pipeline or its statistical comparison: [(Montgomery et al., 2021-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a326b1afaab1f003e6aefdc9b98d40858995a71b) is a methodological framework with no measured biodiversity values, correlations, performance metrics, statistical tests, or baseline comparisons, and [(Armando et al., 2022-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1412fadfe026f74fe7b94bd07c23d78bbad09625) contains no optical near-infrared sensor data, no DBSCAN clustering, no wing-beat frequency or body-to-wing ratio extraction, no Spearman correlation, no ANOVA, and no TukeyHSD post hoc analysis. Therefore, the related work supports the conventional sampling component but does not support the key claim that optical WBF and BWR clusters will correlate with conventional species richness, Shannon, or Simpson indices.

### Suggested Action

Most important, the plan should strengthen the conventional reference protocol using the standards emphasized by [(Montgomery et al., 2021-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a326b1afaab1f003e6aefdc9b98d40858995a71b): predefine measurement goals, document site location and crop type, standardize Malaise trap placement 100 m from the margin and 24-hour deployment, standardize sweep-net transects at 50, 100, and 150 m with 50 sweeps per location, record weather and time, maintain voucher specimens with unique identifiers, and curate metadata for species or morphospecies determinations, observer identity, identification confidence, and date of verification. Next, because [(Armando et al., 2022-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1412fadfe026f74fe7b94bd07c23d78bbad09625) shows that agricultural arthropod surveys often stop at family level and use pitfall traps and direct observation, the plan should add sensitivity analyses at family and order level, report rarefaction or coverage estimates to account for unequal effort between 24-hour Malaise traps and 50-sweep net samples, and either include pitfall traps or direct observation or explicitly justify their exclusion if the target is flying insects. The plan should also define a validation plan for the conventional baseline, including inter-observer agreement for species or morphospecies identification, a minimum number of identifiable specimens per site and month, and a decision rule for when morphospecies are split or merged. Finally, the statistical comparison should be made reproducible by reporting DBSCAN epsilon and min_samples values, the 30 percent optimization and 70 percent testing split, the separate models for richness and diversity indices for each trapping method and the combined model, the Spearman correlation confidence intervals or significance tests, and the ANOVA and TukeyHSD results for month, crop type, and field, so that the conventional reference can be audited against the optical sensor estimates.

## Context: To collect continuous optical data at each agricultural site. Method: At each site, we will place one autonomous near-infrared sensor approximately 50 m from the field margin and monitor continuously for two periods of three days in June and July.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed 50 m, three-day, continuous near-infrared field deployment comes from [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f), which operated a continuous field optical sensor eBoss for 148 consecutive days with downtime below 6.5%, using a 980 nm 5 mW laser, 72 mm beam, 36 m horizontal path, 30,517 Hz 16-bit acquisition, 10-900 Hz band-pass filtering, and wingbeat harmonic confirmation to identify insect events. This directly supports the feasibility of extracting wing-beat-related features from continuous field optical signals and of running a sensor over periods longer than the proposed two 3-day windows, although it used a bistatic 980 nm laser rather than the proposed monostatic 810/970 nm LED backscatter at 50 m. The next strongest support is [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b), which demonstrated WBF extraction from near-infrared backscatter in a controlled 1.25 m Plexiglas tube using a 3.6 W 1320 nm laser, 2 cm FWHM beam, 7 cm off-axis parabolic mirror, 3.14 mm^2 InGaAs detector, 250 MS/s digitizer, and Fourier analysis of 427 mosquito transits; WBFs were 200-900 Hz and Naive Bayes classification using WBF alone achieved 95.8% male, 97.6% female, 96.5% overall gender accuracy, and 62.3% six-class species/gender accuracy. This supports the core signal-processing premise that WBF can be recovered from NIR backscatter and used as a discriminative feature, but it does not support field-scale richness estimation. The weakest but still relevant support is [(Parsons et al., 2019-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fee975fcf2bd48492d8d4b47f5761bc0201928d), which shows that autonomous agricultural optical monitoring with cloud upload, GPS, cellular SIM, solar power, and lithium-ion battery is commercially feasible, although Trapview is a pheromone trap with four cameras and does not measure free-flying WBF or BWR. Taken as an iterative sequence, the 2018 laboratory proof, the 2019 commercial autonomy evidence, and the 2023 field wingbeat detection collectively support the proposed continuous near-infrared field monitoring, but only partially support the proposed 50 m, 810/970 nm, DBSCAN-based richness workflow.

### Contradictions

The most important contradiction is that none of the related works validates the proposed DBSCAN cluster-count proxy for species richness or its correlation with Malaise traps and sweep nets. [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f) reported sustained field detection and mass estimation but no species richness, Shannon or Simpson indices, or Spearman correlations with conventional sampling; its mass model had large relative errors for small insects, 113% for dry mosquitoes with an absolute error of 1 mg, and smaller errors for larger insects, 3.2% for dry bumblebees with an average overestimate of 2.4 mg, indicating that optical signal features may not map cleanly to taxonomic richness. The next strongest contradiction is that the field evidence is not directly transferable to the proposed deployment: [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f) used a 36 m bistatic 980 nm laser, 20 cm above mowed vegetation in a semi-urban field, consumed about 150 W from a building, and was not fully autonomous, whereas the plan requires an autonomous sensor 50 m from Kansas crop field margins for two 3-day periods in June and July. The third major contradiction is that the strongest WBF classification evidence is laboratory and limited to mosquitoes: [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b) used a controlled 1.25 m tube, 427 transits, no simultaneous multi-insect events, and six-class species/gender accuracy only 62.3% with class accuracies 36.2-88.6%, showing substantial overlap among WBF distributions and no evidence that cluster counts will scale to diverse agricultural insect communities. The weakest contradiction is [(Parsons et al., 2019-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fee975fcf2bd48492d8d4b47f5761bc0201928d), which provides no quantitative detection, classification, or biodiversity performance and is trap-based rather than free-flying, so it cannot support the proposed optical richness metric.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to add an explicit validation layer for the DBSCAN richness proxy before treating cluster count as a biodiversity metric: use the 20-site, June and July design to compare DBSCAN cluster counts with Malaise and sweep-net species richness, Shannon, and Simpson indices, tune epsilon and min_samples on the 30% optimization set, evaluate on the 70% test set, report Spearman correlations with confidence intervals and statistical significance, and test cluster stability across the two 3-day periods, crops, and fields. Second, adopt and adapt the event-detection pipeline from [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f), including 10-900 Hz band-pass filtering, sliding-average plus 2.5 standard-deviation extinction threshold, harmonic wingbeat confirmation, and body/wing separation, while recalibrating for 810/970 nm LED backscatter, 50 m monostatic geometry, and 5-70 L measurement volumes; include multi-insect flags because [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b) observed no simultaneous transits and noted multiple Fourier peaks could flag them. Third, ensure true autonomy and operational reliability for two 3-day deployments by using solar/lithium power, LTE/cloud upload, GPS, weather logging, and downtime monitoring, as in [(Parsons et al., 2019-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fee975fcf2bd48492d8d4b47f5761bc0201928d) and [(Genoud et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d99be82ff97b0e19d5678704ae7d6d3a41a4387f), and pre-test the 50 m field-margin placement against the 36 m 980 nm field geometry. Fourth, address the low multi-class WBF classification evidence from [(Genoud et al., 2018-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5fcd98fb07a48b9ae4a670d6f5ae843ecfdbcd8b) by not relying on WBF alone for species-level inference; incorporate BWR, harmonics, amplitude, and temporal features, and discard or flag recordings without clear WBF/BWR as planned. Finally, strengthen statistical inference by reporting ANOVA and TukeyHSD for month, crop, and field, controlling for multiple comparisons, and using voucher-identified conventional samples from Malaise traps and sweep nets to calibrate the optical proxy.

## Context: To collect reference insects from vegetation along field-edge transects. Method: We will also conduct sweep net sampling at 50, 100, and 150 m from the field edge along linear transects, performing 50 sweeps per location perpendicular to the transect.

### Support

The strongest support is that [(Belamkar, and Jadesh, unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/346ce0531172515235b982ce65a67dce7cee97f9) used sweep net sampling in agricultural fields as part of a conventional insect collection protocol, which directly parallels the proposed method’s use of sweep nets at 50, 100, and 150 m from the field edge as a reference for optical near-infrared biodiversity estimates. This is relevant because the research plan depends on conventional sweep-net specimens to generate species richness, Shannon, and Simpson metrics against which DBSCAN cluster counts will be correlated. A second, weaker but still useful support is that the study achieved species-level taxonomic resolution, recording 54 species in 26 families under 6 orders, including Coleoptera with 26 species, Lepidoptera with 13, Hymenoptera with 6, Hemiptera with 5, Orthoptera with 3, and Diptera with 1, which supports the feasibility of identifying sweep-net specimens to species or morphospecies and calculating richness in a way compatible with the plan’s 20-site, two-occasion design. A third, weaker support is that the study calculated alpha-diversity metrics using Shannon-Wiener diversity, Pielou evenness, Simpson reciprocal index, and relative dominance, which aligns with the plan’s stated use of standard biodiversity metrics and provides a precedent for reporting richness and diversity rather than raw counts alone. The weakest support is the seasonal field collection from June to September 2013, which is broadly consistent with the plan’s June and July 2020 sampling window but does not demonstrate that the same seasonal dynamics, crop types, or field-edge distances will produce comparable assemblages.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is not a direct refutation but a major evidentiary gap: the related work does not specify the sweep-net protocol, including transect distance, number of sweeps, sweep path, sweep rate, vegetation height, number of locations, or sampling duration, so it cannot support the proposed standardized protocol of 50 sweeps per location at 50, 100, and 150 m from the field edge perpendicular to linear transects. This matters because the research plan treats sweep-net richness as a reference for tuning DBSCAN epsilon and min_samples and for Spearman correlation with optical sensor clusters; without evidence that a fixed 50-sweep effort is sufficient or comparable across sites, the reference metric may be underpowered or biased. A second contradiction is the absence of quantitative diversity index values, statistical significance tests, baseline comparisons, or uncertainty estimates, which means the study cannot demonstrate that sweep-net-derived richness or diversity is reliable enough to serve as the target variable for the proposed correlation analysis, ANOVA, or TukeyHSD tests across month, crop type, and field. A third contradiction is the internal inconsistency in the Lepidoptera family counts, where Pieridae is reported both as having 6 species and as having only 1 species, which weakens confidence in the taxonomic completeness and accuracy of the reference assemblage and could propagate into richness estimates if similar identification inconsistencies occur in the proposed study. The weakest contradiction is that the related work does not compare sweep-net data to any optical sensor, does not address the sensor’s 5 to 70 L measurement volume, 810 nm and 970 nm backscatter, wing-beat frequency and body-to-wing ratio extraction, or the distinction between flying insects detected optically and vegetation-associated insects collected by sweep net, so it provides no direct evidence that sweep-net richness will correlate with the optical clustering proxy.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to make the sweep-net method fully standardized and reportable before using it as the reference for optical sensor validation: define 50 sweeps per location at 50, 100, and 150 m, specify transect length, sweep path perpendicular to the transect, sweep rate, vegetation height or stratum, number of replicate locations per site, and sampling duration, and include these effort variables in the ANOVA and TukeyHSD design to test whether sweep effort, distance from edge, crop, month, or field affects richness. The next most important action is to add a statistical validation plan that quantifies the reliability of the sweep-net reference, including replicate sweeps or subsampling to estimate detection probability, confidence intervals for richness and diversity indices, and a pre-specified Spearman correlation framework with multiple-testing correction when fitting separate models for Malaise, sweep net, and combined conventional methods. A third action is to strengthen taxonomic quality control by resolving inconsistencies such as the Pieridae count in [(Belamkar, and Jadesh, unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/346ce0531172515235b982ce65a67dce7cee97f9), using voucher specimens, morphospecies where species-level identification is uncertain, and double-identification for key orders that dominate richness, especially Coleoptera and Lepidoptera. A less critical but useful action is to align the sweep-net target assemblage with the optical sensor’s detection domain by documenting which taxa are flying or vegetation-active during the 3-day sensor periods, and, if possible, adding a pilot comparison of sweep-net captures with Malaise or optical detections to ensure that the conventional reference is not measuring a fundamentally different insect guild.

## Context: To remove non-insect or unusable optical recordings before clustering. Method: We will automatically separate insect recordings from noise using proprietary cloud-based neural network software and discard observations without clearly identified wingbeats or body-to-wing ratios.

### Support

The strongest support for using a trained classifier to separate insect recordings from noise comes from [(Kiskin et al., 2018-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2619b9ae34fcc27fa7c84c43cc60aec877fc540f), which trained a wavelet-transform CNN on single-channel 256-scale scalograms of 0.1 s mosquito presence/absence intervals, using one convolutional layer, ReLU activations, dropout, and a two-class fully connected layer, with 10-fold cross-validation maximizing F1 and a window width of 10; it also compared a traditional SVM using STFT slices, MFCCs, entropy, energy entropy, spectral entropy, flux, roll-off, spread, centroid, and zero crossing rate, with recursive feature elimination selecting 27 features and a window width of 1, and reported qualitative transfer to held-out data without hyperparameter re-tuning. This directly supports the proposed step of automatically rejecting non-insect or unusable recordings before WBF/BWR extraction and DBSCAN clustering, because it shows a compact learned detector can operate on short insect signal intervals and generalize across held-out recordings. The newer multisensor work by [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949) provides additional, though less direct, support for machine-learning processing of insect optical and wingbeat recordings: a MobileNet camera model achieved 0.92 accuracy on a small proof-of-concept set, and a linear SVM on optoacoustic wingbeat signals achieved 0.68 accuracy, demonstrating that insect-related optical/wingbeat features can be learned even in a constrained laboratory arena. However, this support is weaker for the proposed cloud-based noise filter because the 2023 pipeline classifies already captured insect recordings rather than separating insects from noise, does not extract BWR, and does not validate discarding observations lacking WBF/BWR against conventional richness metrics.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the most directly relevant newer work, [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949), shows that wingbeat-only deep learning was not feasible on the available small dataset and that a linear SVM reached only 0.68 accuracy, while the camera-only MobileNet reached 0.92; the authors did not report a combined camera-plus-wingbeat accuracy, statistical significance, non-insect rejection, BWR extraction, or biodiversity-metric validation. This challenges the proposed reliance on proprietary cloud-based neural network software to cleanly separate insect optical recordings from noise and to discard observations without clearly identified WBF/BWR, especially because the proposed field deployment will produce heterogeneous, unsupervised near-infrared photodiode time signals across 20 agricultural sites and two months, not the controlled 60 by 45 by 20 mm laboratory arena with standardized diffuse illumination, 500-microsecond flash, 23.5-millisecond exposure, 10-megapixel camera, and local Raspberry Pi 4 processing. The older audio work by [(Kiskin et al., 2018-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2619b9ae34fcc27fa7c84c43cc60aec877fc540f) also contradicts direct transfer to the proposed method because it used 57 smartphone microphone recordings of Culex quinquefasciatus at 8 kHz, 0.1 s presence/absence labels, and qualitative claims that wavelet CNN outperformed STFT-based training and that SVM was best among traditional classifiers, but it did not report numerical accuracy, precision, recall, F1, or significance, did not address optical near-infrared signals, and did not extract WBF or BWR. Consequently, the evidence that a cloud neural network can reliably discard unusable optical observations without biasing richness estimates is limited, and the proposed discard rule could remove true insect observations with weak wingbeats or ambiguous BWR, potentially lowering sensitivity for small or slow-flying insects and distorting DBSCAN cluster counts.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to replace the opaque proprietary cloud filter with a validated, reproducible insect-versus-noise classifier and to report its performance on labeled optical near-infrared recordings from the same sensor, crops, and seasons. Following [(Kiskin et al., 2018-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2619b9ae34fcc27fa7c84c43cc60aec877fc540f), the team should evaluate wavelet/STFT-based features and compact CNNs against SVM, random forest, and naive Bayes baselines using 10-fold cross-validation, F1, precision, recall, AUC, confusion matrices, and held-out transfer tests, while documenting dataset size, label granularity, window width, hyperparameters, and computational resources. Because [(Tschaikner et al., 2023-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1c8e31a876b92218ca85eac3ce1fd5e36578b949) found wingbeat-only CNN training difficult on small data and linear SVM weaker at 0.68, the method should use small-data-appropriate models such as linear SVMs, transfer learning, data augmentation, or hybrid camera/wingbeat features if available, and should not assume a cloud CNN will outperform simpler baselines without evidence. The discard rule should be made probabilistic and auditable: retain low-confidence or missing-WBF/BWR observations in a sensitivity analysis, tune confidence thresholds to balance false insect acceptance against loss of true insects, and quantify how discarding affects DBSCAN epsilon/min_samples tuning, cluster counts, Spearman correlations, ANOVA/TukeyHSD results, and comparisons with Malaise traps and sweep nets. Finally, the plan should include field-specific validation for non-insect noise, class imbalance, seasonal and crop effects, and reproducibility details for the cloud software, including model architecture, training data, versioning, and uncertainty estimates.

## Context: To optimize the optical clustering model against conventional reference metrics. Method: We will randomly divide the data into optimization (30%) and testing (70%) sets. During optimization, we will tune DBSCAN parameters (ε and min_samples) to maximize Spearman correlation between biodiversity metrics from sensors and conventional methods using stochastic gradient descent.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed method is that recent optical near-infrared insect recordings can be converted into WBF-derived spectral features and clustered into species-like groups: [(Rydhmer, and Selvan, 2021-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18bfc290cbfd1e6837a5dbe4a72140b3ff96bae3) used 193-dimensional log-transformed, max-normalized Welch power spectra from FaunaPhotonics 808 nm and 975 nm recordings and reported that a dynamic beta-VAE with K-means achieved higher ARI and AMI than PCA, sigmoid Kernel-PCA, complete-linkage hierarchical clustering, and a conventional VAE against labelled closed-cage species groups, which supports the plan's premise that WBF features can underpin automated richness proxies. A second, weaker support is that the related work used 40,000 unlabelled field recordings, 6,000 labelled recordings with 500 per species group, 8 groups in a semi-supervised clustering loss with gamma cycled between 0.01 and 0.2 every 100 epochs, 4 held-out groups, a 3,000-recording test set, and five random-split runs, showing that large unlabelled optical datasets and repeated random evaluation can stabilize species-like clustering and aligning with the proposed 30 percent optimization and 70 percent testing split; semi-supervised training further improved clustering over the unsupervised dynamic beta-VAE, with clusters reported as more compact and less overlapping, which supports using conventional identifications to calibrate the proposed clustering. A third support is that the related work filtered signals with a CNN before reducing them to 193 frequency bins, supporting the plan's use of neural-network filtering to separate insect recordings from noise. A fourth support is that the method explicitly optimized a clustering hyperparameter by selecting K from 5 to 50 using maximum average silhouette score, which supports the need to tune cluster count, although the objective was silhouette rather than Spearman correlation with conventional biodiversity metrics. The weakest support is computational and implementation feasibility: the model was implemented in PyTorch, trained for 5,000 epochs with Adam at learning rate 1e-3 and batch size 256 on an Nvidia GTX 1050 with 4 GB memory, used a 2-dimensional bottleneck, and used dynamic beta parameters a=0.2, b=0.05, w1=w2=1.2, w3=0.9, and w4=1.1, indicating that learned spectral embeddings and clustering can be run on modest hardware, though the proposed DBSCAN and Spearman optimization have not been directly validated.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the related work does not support tuning DBSCAN epsilon and min_samples with stochastic gradient descent to maximize Spearman correlation with conventional biodiversity metrics: [(Rydhmer, and Selvan, 2021-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18bfc290cbfd1e6837a5dbe4a72140b3ff96bae3) used K-means on a 2-dimensional VAE latent, selected K from 5 to 50 by maximum average silhouette score, and evaluated clusters with ARI and AMI against labelled species groups, not Spearman correlations with species richness, Shannon index, Simpson index, Malaise traps, or sweep nets. The next strongest contradiction is that the related work did not use the number of clusters as a proxy for species richness; it evaluated species-group assignment, so it provides no direct evidence that DBSCAN cluster count will correlate with conventional richness or diversity indices. A further contradiction is the sensor, feature, and population mismatch: the related work used 808 nm and 975 nm illumination, a 20 L measurement volume within 1 m, 20 kHz sampling, 0 to 5 kHz bandwidth, 193 frequency bins between 0 and 2 kHz, Oresund field recordings, and closed-cage labelled species groups, whereas the proposed plan uses 810 nm and 970 nm LEDs, 5 to 70 L measurement volumes, WBF and BWR features, 20 central Kansas agricultural sites sampled in June and July 2020, and conventional Malaise and sweep-net identifications, limiting transferability. Another contradiction is that the supplied excerpt reports higher ARI and AMI but no numeric ARI or AMI values, exact selected K values, or statistical significance tests, so the claimed superiority over baselines is not quantitatively verifiable. A weaker contradiction is that K-means with a prior K range of 5 to 50 and silhouette selection is methodologically different from DBSCAN, whose epsilon and min_samples can make cluster count highly sensitive to density thresholds and noise, potentially producing unstable richness estimates. The weakest contradiction is that the related work did not test ANOVA or TukeyHSD for month, crop type, or field, nor separate models for richness and diversity indices for each trapping method plus a combined model, so it does not validate the proposed statistical design.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to replace or supplement stochastic gradient descent tuning of DBSCAN with a transparent, reproducible parameter search over epsilon and min_samples, using stratified site, month, and crop cross-validation and repeated random splits, and to report Spearman correlation confidence intervals, paired significance tests, and multiple-comparison correction; if SGD is retained, use a differentiable surrogate or continuous relaxation for min_samples and validate that it does not overfit the 30 percent optimization set. The next most important action is to add baselines and alternative clustering methods from the related work, including PCA, sigmoid Kernel-PCA, complete-linkage hierarchical clustering, conventional VAE, K-means, and HDBSCAN, and compare both cluster-count correlations with conventional metrics and, where labels exist, ARI and AMI. A further action is to consider a learned embedding, such as a dynamic beta-VAE with beta adapted every 5 epochs and parameters a=0.2, b=0.05, w1=w2=1.2, w3=0.9, and w4=1.1, on log-transformed, max-normalized Welch spectra or WBF and BWR features before clustering, because the related work showed learned latents improved species-group clustering over linear and kernel baselines. Another action is to use partial labels from conventional identifications, morphospecies, or closed-cage references to add a semi-supervised clustering loss or to calibrate cluster count, as the related work found that a clustering loss over 8 labelled species groups with gamma cycled between 0.01 and 0.2 improved compactness and reduced overlap. A less critical but useful action is to aggregate optical cluster counts at the site and month level before correlating with conventional richness and diversity indices, fit separate models for Malaise, sweep net, and combined conventional methods, and include ANOVA and TukeyHSD for month, crop type, and field while controlling for multiple testing. The least important but still useful action is to report implementation details for reproducibility, including PyTorch version, Adam learning rate 1e-3, batch size 256, 5,000 epochs, GPU memory, random seeds, five random-split runs, and a justified prior range for expected cluster count or DBSCAN min_samples based on expected species richness.

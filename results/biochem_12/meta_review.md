## Isotopic symmetry-breaking and cleavage-site assignment in pentaglycine: To break isotopic symmetry and enable unambiguous determination of cleavage sites, we will employ selective ¹⁵N,¹³C-labeling of specific glycine residues in pentaglycine substrates. To determine preferred cleavage sites in pentaglycine, we will use selectively ¹³C-labeled pentaglycine (Gly2 labeled at Cα and CO carbons) and monitor the reaction using glycine-optimized 2D HA(CA)CO NMR experiments. The characteristic chemical shift differences between C-terminal (179.4 ppm) and non-terminal (173.8 ppm) ¹³CO resonances will allow unambiguous identification of cleavage sites.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed isotopic symmetry-breaking NMR method is that [(Maya-Martinez et al., 2019-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af8a8dea1a5f980021848949d211de2bf83f567c) demonstrated that solution-state NMR resonance assignment and titration with soluble S. aureus peptidoglycan fragments and muropeptides can produce interpretable chemical-shift perturbations and support a structural model of a PBP4:muropeptide complex, indicating that S. aureus peptidoglycan-derived substrates are NMR-observable and that NMR can map substrate features in the context of the proposed LSS/LytM pentaglycine studies. A weaker but relevant support is that the same study combined NMR with pull-down experiments, muropeptide/peptidoglycan activity assays, imipenem/native mass spectrometry, and X-ray crystallography of PBP4(S75C), which aligns with the research plan's multi-modal strategy of using 800 MHz NMR, muropeptides from S. aureus USA300 sacculus, and turbidity reduction assays to validate synthetic peptide findings. The weakest support is indirect because the work examined PBP4 rather than LSS or LytM, focused on binding/structural mapping rather than real-time hydrolysis, and did not use selective 15N/13C labeling or glycine-optimized 2D HA(CA)CO experiments, so it does not directly validate the proposed C-terminal 179.4 ppm versus non-terminal 173.8 ppm 13CO cleavage-site assignment.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction or limitation is that [(Maya-Martinez et al., 2019-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af8a8dea1a5f980021848949d211de2bf83f567c) did not use selective 15N/13C labeling of individual pentaglycine glycine residues and did not employ glycine-optimized 2D HA(CA)CO experiments, so it provides no evidence that the proposed isotopic symmetry-breaking strategy or the characteristic C-terminal/non-terminal 13CO chemical-shift difference can unambiguously assign pentaglycine cleavage sites. A less direct contradiction is that the reported NMR outcome was qualitative, with no quantitative binding constants, kinetic rate constants, or unambiguous cleavage-site assignments, suggesting that NMR titration and chemical-shift perturbation alone may be insufficient for the proposed real-time kinetic monitoring at approximately 0.4 mM substrate with 2 or 50 uM enzyme and for determining preferred cleavage sites in LSS or LytM. A further limitation is that the study used S. aureus PBP4 and PBP4(S75C) with soluble fragments, muropeptides, and synthetic branched lactoyl peptides, and found no interaction with high-molecular-weight peptidoglycan or synthetic stem peptides, which may limit direct transferability to the proposed LSS/LytM substrates, especially more complex cross-linked peptides 1-7 and USA300 muropeptides. The weakest contradiction is methodological reproducibility: the provided sections do not specify NMR spectrometer field strength, exact titration concentrations, or detailed pulse sequences, making it difficult to benchmark the proposed 800 MHz quantitative 1H NMR setup, 20-second recycle delays, DSS reference, and 25°C kinetic conditions.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to validate the core isotopic assignment strategy before applying it to LSS and LytM by preparing selectively 13C-labeled pentaglycine with Gly2 Cα and CO labeling, plus additional labeled positions or synthetic cleavage-product standards, and acquiring glycine-optimized 2D HA(CA)CO spectra to confirm that C-terminal 13CO resonances appear near 179.4 ppm and non-terminal resonances near 173.8 ppm under the planned 800 MHz, 25°C conditions. A second priority is to make the NMR kinetic measurements explicitly quantitative and statistically robust by reporting replicate numbers, 20-second recycle delays, DSS integration, time-course linearity over the first 40-60 minutes, linear regression confidence intervals, Michaelis-Menten fits, and product-ratio statistics for each peptide 1-7. A third priority is to add orthogonal validation, inspired by the use of imipenem/native mass spectrometry and activity assays in [(Maya-Martinez et al., 2019-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af8a8dea1a5f980021848949d211de2bf83f567c), by using LC-MS or native MS to identify hydrolysis products and by including inactive-enzyme, unlabeled-substrate, and non-hydrolytic controls to rule out NMR artifacts. The least critical but useful action is to specify all NMR and biochemical parameters, including isotope purity, labeling positions, substrate/enzyme concentrations, pulse sequences, relaxation delays, and USA300 versus SH1000 muropeptide controls, so that the proposed synthetic peptide, muropeptide, and turbidity reduction assays are reproducible and directly comparable to prior S. aureus peptidoglycan NMR work.

## Real-time NMR kinetic monitoring and hydrolysis product identification: Our approach will utilize quantitative ¹H NMR spectroscopy to monitor substrate hydrolysis in real-time, allowing us to measure reaction kinetics while simultaneously identifying hydrolysis products through multi-dimensional NMR experiments including ¹H-¹³C HSQC, HMBC, and glycine-optimized 2D HA(CA)CO correlation experiments. For real-time NMR kinetics, we will acquire quantitative ¹H spectra at regular time intervals using a standard pulse sequence with selective radiofrequency field for residual HDO signal presaturation. We will use 20-second recycle delays to ensure quantitative detection and 0.1 mM DSS as both reference compound and for peak integration.

### Support

Strongest support for the proposed real-time quantitative ¹H NMR kinetic monitoring and hydrolysis-product identification plan comes from [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13), which used solution-state NMR on purified LSS and its CWT domain, complemented by MD simulations and SAXS, to show that LSS catalytic groove embracing loops are highly mobile, that LSS domains do not interact in vitro, that binding of a second zinc by catalytic histidines reduces LSS catalytic activity, and that the CWT domain has higher affinity toward the stem peptide. This directly supports the feasibility of NMR-based study of LSS catalytic-domain conformational states and the need to monitor zinc-dependent activity under controlled in vitro conditions, although it did not use the proposed 20-second recycle quantitative ¹H time course, 0.1 mM DSS referencing, 0.4 mM substrate, 2 or 50 μM enzyme, 25°C, 800 MHz, or ¹H-¹³C HSQC, HMBC, and glycine-optimized HA(CA)CO product assignment. Next, [(Raulinaitis et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e54bf104aab72adefcfe58a8e56b2263c19b147f) supports the chemical premise that a lysostaphin-family enzyme can cleave pentaglycine and that NMR can characterize the catalytic domain and zinc binding; it used pentaglycine hydrolysis, S. aureus lysis, GFP-LytU localization, ITC, and protein NMR to show LytU cleaves pentaglycine and binds a second zinc ion in a pH-dependent inhibitory manner. This is weaker support because it studies LytU rather than LSS or LytM and provides structural rather than real-time quantitative kinetic evidence, with no reported kcat, Km, product ratios, NMR field strength, pulse sequence, concentrations, or statistical significance. Weakest but still relevant support is [(Maya-Martinez et al., 2019-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af8a8dea1a5f980021848949d211de2bf83f567c), which used solution NMR titration and NMR-data-driven docking on S. aureus PBP4 and PBP4(S75C) with soluble PG fragments from S. aureus SH1000 and synthetic branched lactoyl peptides, showing that NMR can resolve S. aureus PG fragment and muropeptide interactions and support structural models. This supports the proposed use of NMR for muropeptide product identification and the value of synthetic PG-like ligands, but it is an equilibrium binding and modeling study of a transpeptidase, not a time-resolved LSS or LytM hydrolysis assay, and it does not validate the proposed 20-second recycle, DSS integration, or glycine-optimized HA(CA)CO cleavage-site assignment.

### Contradictions

Strongest contradiction is that [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) found the LSS CWT domain shows higher affinity toward the stem peptide while LSS domains do not interact in vitro, which challenges the proposed emphasis on LSS and LytM catalytic domains alone for defining substrate specificity across peptides 1-7, especially substrates containing stem peptides. If CWT-mediated stem-peptide recognition contributes to full-length LSS specificity, catalytic-domain-only kinetics at 0.4 mM substrate and 2 or 50 μM enzyme may misassign preferred cleavage sites or underestimate substrate recognition. The same paper also reports that second zinc binding by catalytic histidines reduces LSS catalytic activity, contradicting any assumption that standard buffer conditions will yield a single, stable catalytic state; the proposed 25°C, 800 MHz, 20-second recycle quantitative ¹H time course must therefore explicitly control zinc concentration, pH, and metal chelation, because uncontrolled zinc could shift rates and product ratios. Next, [(Maya-Martinez et al., 2019-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af8a8dea1a5f980021848949d211de2bf83f567c) provides a weaker but important contradiction to the bottom-up synthetic peptide strategy: PBP4 did not interact with high-molecular-weight PG or synthetic stem peptides but did interact with larger soluble PG fragments, suggesting that simple synthetic stem peptides or short pentaglycine-based ligands may not recapitulate native PG recognition. Although this is a PBP4 transpeptidase study rather than an LSS or LytM study, it supports the plan’s inclusion of USA300 sacculus muropeptides and warns that synthetic peptides 1-7 alone may be insufficient to define physiological specificity. Weakest contradiction is that none of the three studies directly validates the proposed real-time quantitative ¹H NMR kinetic protocol: [(Raulinaitis et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e54bf104aab72adefcfe58a8e56b2263c19b147f) used NMR structurally and reported no numerical kinetic constants, [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) used NMR for dynamics and structure without hydrolysis time courses, and [(Maya-Martinez et al., 2019-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af8a8dea1a5f980021848949d211de2bf83f567c) used NMR titration and docking without 20-second recycle, DSS referencing, or ¹H-¹³C HSQC, HMBC, and HA(CA)CO product identification. Thus the proposed method is plausible but not directly supported by prior quantitative kinetic NMR evidence.

### Suggested Action

Most important, add full-length LSS and LytM or CWT-containing constructs alongside catalytic domains, and compare catalytic-domain-only versus full-length kinetics for each peptide 1-7, because [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) shows CWT stem-peptide affinity and domain non-interaction in vitro. This should include 0.4 mM substrate, 2 and 50 μM enzyme, 25°C, 800 MHz, 20-second recycle, 0.1 mM DSS, and replicate biological and technical replicates with statistical significance for kobs, kcat/Km, and product ratios. Second, rigorously control and report zinc, pH, buffer, and metal-chelation conditions for all LSS and LytM reactions, because [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) and [(Raulinaitis et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e54bf104aab72adefcfe58a8e56b2263c19b147f) show second zinc binding can inhibit LSS and LytU in a pH-dependent manner; include zinc-free or zinc-saturated controls, ITC or metal-binding assays if possible, and pH-rate profiles. Third, validate synthetic pentaglycine and stem-peptide findings with native USA300 sacculus muropeptides and, if feasible, larger soluble PG fragments, because [(Maya-Martinez et al., 2019-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af8a8dea1a5f980021848949d211de2bf83f567c) shows larger soluble PG fragments can be recognized when synthetic stem peptides are not; use SDS, DNase, Pronase, HCl, and mutanolysin extraction and compare before and after NMR product patterns. Fourth, prevalidate the quantitative NMR kinetic workflow with a non-hydrolyzing control and a known hydrolysis standard, confirming that 20-second recycle delays, residual HDO presaturation, DSS integration, and linear regression over the first 40-60 minutes yield reproducible rates; include relaxation correction, line-width checks, and Michaelis-Menten fitting only when substrate depletion is appropriate. Finally, use selectively ¹³C-labeled Gly2 pentaglycine and the proposed ¹H-¹³C HSQC, HMBC, and glycine-optimized HA(CA)CO experiments to assign C-terminal 179.4 ppm versus non-terminal 173.8 ppm ¹³CO resonances, but also report peak integration, error estimates, and product ratios for each cleavage site to avoid overinterpreting single-resonance assignments.

## Systematic kinetic comparison across peptidoglycan fragment complexity: We will conduct systematic kinetic studies using seven different peptidoglycan fragments (peptides 1-7) of varying complexity, from simple pentaglycine to more complex cross-linked structures containing stem peptides. Each experiment will monitor substrate hydrolysis at approximately 0.4 mM substrate concentration with 2 μM or 50 μM enzyme concentration.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed method is that [(Mitkowski et al., 2019-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/469b18dd3e5e7eb317e4872a3be7d7e62d7c5c49) used pentaglycine as the ligand and S. aureus cells for mature lysostaphin activity, which directly supports the proposed inclusion of pentaglycine as the simplest fragment in the seven-peptide panel and the use of living-cell turbidity reduction as an orthogonal validation. A second, weaker but still relevant point is that the paper examined purified wild-type SH3b and N405A/Y472A variants by microscale thermophoresis with Scatchard analysis and a fluorescent binding/microscopy assay, indicating that pentaglycine recognition by lysostaphin-associated domains is biochemically tractable and functionally linked to activity; this supports adding binding assays or mutant controls to the proposed NMR kinetic workflow. The weakest support is that the available excerpt provides only section headings and no quantitative Kd, fold-change, OD600 reduction, or statistical significance, so it cannot quantitatively validate the proposed 25°C, 0.4 mM substrate, 2 or 50 μM enzyme, 800 MHz NMR kinetic conditions.

### Contradictions

The strongest apparent contradiction is that [(Mitkowski et al., 2019-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/469b18dd3e5e7eb317e4872a3be7d7e62d7c5c49) focused on the SH3b domain and mature lysostaphin activity, with N405A/Y472A substitutions affecting SH3b binding and lysostaphin activity, whereas the proposed method emphasizes catalytic domains; if SH3b recognition contributes to substrate positioning or apparent activity, catalytic-domain-only NMR kinetics may miss recognition-domain effects on cleavage specificity. Next, the related work tested only a single pentaglycine ligand rather than seven peptidoglycan fragments of increasing complexity, so it does not provide evidence that fragment complexity can be ranked by the proposed real-time NMR kinetic comparison. A further limitation is that the study used MST/Scatchard binding, fluorescent binding/microscopy, and turbidity reduction rather than quantitative 1H NMR, HSQC/HMBC, or glycine-optimized 2D HA(CA)CO, and the excerpt lacks buffer, temperature, concentration, instrument, and statistical details, so it does not directly validate the proposed 25°C, 0.4 mM substrate, 2 or 50 μM enzyme, 800 MHz NMR implementation. Finally, because no numerical outcomes are available, any inference that pentaglycine binding predicts cleavage site or rate remains unsupported.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to include full-length lysostaphin or SH3b-containing constructs, and ideally N405A/Y472A controls, in the NMR and turbidity experiments to test whether non-catalytic pentaglycine recognition alters apparent cleavage site or rate, as suggested by [(Mitkowski et al., 2019-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/469b18dd3e5e7eb317e4872a3be7d7e62d7c5c49). Next, the seven-fragment panel should be measured under identical, fully specified conditions—25°C, 0.4 mM substrate, 2 and 50 μM enzyme, 800 MHz, 20-s recycle delays, 0.1 mM DSS, and linear regression over the first 40–60 min—with replicate experiments and statistical significance reported for each fragment and enzyme concentration. Then, complement NMR with MST or fluorescent binding assays for pentaglycine and selected fragments to separate binding from hydrolysis and to benchmark against the Scatchard/fluorescent approach used in [(Mitkowski et al., 2019-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/469b18dd3e5e7eb317e4872a3be7d7e62d7c5c49). Finally, report turbidity reduction assays with defined S. aureus USA300 OD600 6–8, 50 μg/mL enzyme, 16-h OD600 time courses, and statistical comparisons to NMR-derived product identification, so that the living-cell validation is directly comparable to the biochemical and NMR evidence.

## Standardized experimental conditions and kinetic data analysis: All experiments will be conducted at 25°C using 800 MHz NMR spectroscopy, and reaction rates will be calculated using linear regression of the first 40-60 minutes of each reaction, with kinetic data fitted to Michaelis-Menten equations where appropriate.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed standardized kinetic NMR method is that NMR-based characterization of S. aureus lysostaphin-family hydrolases is feasible and biologically relevant. [(Raulinaitis et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e54bf104aab72adefcfe58a8e56b2263c19b147f) used purified recombinant LytU, GFP-LytU fusion construction, enzyme activity assays, cell lysis assays, isothermal titration calorimetry, and protein NMR to show that LytU lyses S. aureus and cleaves pentaglycine, directly supporting the plan's focus on pentaglycine hydrolysis and NMR monitoring of S. aureus peptidoglycan hydrolase activity. This is the most relevant support because it connects the proposed substrate, pentaglycine, to a functional S. aureus target, although it does not provide 800 MHz, 25°C, 40-60 minute, or Michaelis-Menten data. A secondary but still relevant support is the newer, iterative extension by [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13), which used NMR, MD simulations, and SAXS on purified lysostaphin and its CWT domain to show catalytic groove mobility, lack of in vitro domain interaction, zinc-dependent activity reduction, and CWT stem-peptide affinity. This supports the plan's use of NMR to resolve domain-specific and substrate-binding features of LSS, and it reinforces that NMR can distinguish catalytic-domain behavior from full-length or CWT-associated behavior. However, neither paper supplies the exact kinetic implementation proposed: no 800 MHz field, no 25°C reaction temperature, no 0.4 mM substrate, no 2 or 50 μM enzyme, no 20-second recycle delay, no DSS integration, no first 40-60 minute linear regression, no Michaelis-Menten fitting, no computational resources, and no statistical fitting diagnostics.

### Contradictions

The strongest potential contradiction is not to the use of NMR, but to the assumption that a single standardized 25°C, 800 MHz, 40-60 minute initial-rate window will yield reproducible Michaelis-Menten parameters without controlling zinc and conformational state. The newer [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) reported that a second zinc bound by catalytic histidines reduces lysostaphin catalytic activity and that catalytic groove embracing loops are highly mobile; if zinc contamination, buffer composition, or conformational sampling varies, the apparent rate in the first 40-60 minutes may reflect metal inhibition or domain dynamics rather than intrinsic substrate turnover. This directly threatens the proposed linear regression and Michaelis-Menten fitting because the rate may be non-linear or condition-dependent. A related but slightly weaker contradiction comes from the earlier [(Raulinaitis et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e54bf104aab72adefcfe58a8e56b2263c19b147f), which found that LytU catalytic domain binds a second zinc ion that inhibits the enzyme in a pH-dependent manner; this implies that fixed 25°C alone is insufficient if pH, metal chelation, or zinc concentration are not tightly controlled, and that rates measured at 2 or 50 μM enzyme may be sensitive to metal availability. A further limitation is that [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) showed lysostaphin domains do not interact in vitro and the CWT domain has higher stem-peptide affinity, suggesting that kinetic rates measured with isolated catalytic domains or simple pentaglycine may not capture full-length substrate specificity for complex stem-peptide-containing fragments. Finally, neither paper provides quantitative kinetic constants, statistical comparisons, regression diagnostics, or hardware specifications that would validate the proposed 40-60 minute window, 800 MHz requirement, or Michaelis-Menten model.

### Suggested Action

Most important, the method should add explicit metal and pH controls before kinetic fitting: use metal-free or defined zinc conditions, include a chelator such as EDTA or iminodiacetate where compatible, report buffer, pH, ionic strength, and zinc concentration, and perform pH and zinc titrations for LSS and LytM catalytic domains because [(Raulinaitis et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e54bf104aab72adefcfe58a8e56b2263c19b147f) and the newer [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) both show zinc-dependent inhibition. Second, validate the 40-60 minute linear regression window empirically for each peptide and enzyme concentration by acquiring full time courses, checking linearity, substrate depletion, product inhibition, and enzyme stability, and reporting R^2, residuals, confidence intervals, and the exact time points used; if rates are non-linear, use a shorter initial-rate window or a mechanistic model. Third, apply Michaelis-Menten fitting only when initial rates are measured across a substrate concentration range under conditions where product inhibition and metal inactivation are absent, using nonlinear regression rather than linearized plots, and report Km, kcat or kcat/Km, standard errors, and statistical comparisons between LSS and LytM. Fourth, strengthen the NMR implementation by specifying 800 MHz, 25°C, 20-second recycle delay, DSS reference, presaturation, number of scans, relaxation delays, and replicate number, and by using quantitative 1H integrals plus 1H-13C HSQC, HMBC, and HA(CA)CO to confirm product identity and cleavage site. Fifth, include both catalytic-domain and full-length or CWT-containing constructs where feasible, because [(Tossavainen et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f1be5c05128f7f58decad25a91cf6789f6065d13) indicates domain mobility and CWT stem-peptide affinity may alter apparent kinetics for complex substrates. Finally, connect kinetic NMR results to turbidity reduction assays and muropeptide analysis to ensure that rates measured at 0.4 mM substrate and 2 or 50 μM enzyme are biologically relevant to S. aureus USA300 sacculus and cell lysis.

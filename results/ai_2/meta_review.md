## Context: To stratify evaluation difficulty and provide known-complete test sets. Method: 'We will define four molecular subsets of varying complexity based on chemical similarity and synthetic accessibility criteria.'

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed method comes from [(Brown et al., 2018-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5bfeb6901db481c08874cfe0ae807d8564513765), which directly operationalizes chemical similarity and synthetic accessibility in a molecular benchmark by filtering ChEMBL 24, removing salts, neutralizing charges, limiting SMILES length to 100 characters, restricting atoms to H, B, C, N, O, F, Si, P, S, Cl, Se, Br, and I, and separating training molecules from a 10 marketed-drug holdout using ECFP4 similarity greater than 0.323. This is the closest precedent to the proposed four subsets because it shows that similarity thresholds and real synthesized molecules can be used to create controlled molecular evaluation sets, and its RDKit-based canonicalization, property, and similarity pipeline is directly reusable for defining Sasp, Ssas, Sd>p, and Sd=p in GDB-13. The next strongest support is [(Liu et al., 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b83e7e9a7bf59347eb9bc53ecaf139e301a8b72d), which evaluates CGVAE on QM9, ZINC, and CEPDB as increasing structural complexity, with QM9 containing about 134k molecules with up to 9 heavy atoms, ZINC containing 250k drug-like molecules averaging about 23 heavy atoms, and CEPDB containing 250k molecules averaging about 28 heavy atoms and six to seven rings; this supports the plan's complexity stratification and shows that validity, novelty, and uniqueness can be measured on molecular generation tasks, although it does not use recall, known-complete sets, SELFIES, or synthetic-accessibility subsets. The weakest but still relevant support is [(Brown et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90abbc2cf38462b954ae1b772fac9532e2ccd8b0), which constructs known-answer word-manipulation tasks with graded difficulty, 10,000 examples per task, and evaluates GPT-3 from 125M to 175B parameters, showing that controlled known-complete test sets can reveal model-size-dependent capability and that harder transformations remain unsolved even by large models; this supports the research plan's goal of using known-complete molecular subsets and model-size comparisons, but it is not molecular and does not address SELFIES, SMILES, recall, or equivalence classes.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction or caution is from [(Brown et al., 2018-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5bfeb6901db481c08874cfe0ae807d8564513765), where on seven trivial goal-directed benchmarks all evaluated models achieved near-perfect or perfect scores, and the best-in-dataset baseline also achieved excellent scores, leading the authors to conclude that such objectives are too easy to distinguish generative models from virtual screening. This contradicts the assumption that merely defining molecular subsets by chemical similarity and synthetic accessibility will produce a discriminative recall benchmark; if the four GDB-13 subsets are too small, too similar to pretraining molecules, or too easy to retrieve, models or best-in-dataset baselines may saturate the metric without demonstrating true recall. The next contradiction is from [(Liu et al., 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b83e7e9a7bf59347eb9bc53ecaf139e301a8b72d), where CGVAE achieved 100% validity on QM9, ZINC, and CEPDB, with 94.35% novelty and 98.57% uniqueness on QM9, 100% novelty and 99.82% uniqueness on ZINC, and 100% novelty and 99.62% uniqueness on CEPDB, while non-CGVAE baselines varied widely, including QM9 validity values of 94.78%, 10.00%, 30.00%, 61.00%, and 98.00%, and uniqueness values of 96.94%, 67.50%, 9.30%, 40.90%, and 99.86%; this shows that standard generation metrics can be high even when recall, complete-set coverage, and equivalence-class handling are not measured, so the proposed method must not rely on validity, novelty, or uniqueness as proxies for recall. The weakest contradiction is from [(Brown et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90abbc2cf38462b954ae1b772fac9532e2ccd8b0), which although it supports known-answer difficulty stratification, does not provide molecular evidence, uses 10,000 word examples of length 5 to 14 characters, a 2048-token context, and non-bijective tasks where 175B GPT-3 still scored only 66.9% on random insertion, 38.6% on cycling letters, 40.2% on A1, 15.1% on A2, and failed reversed words; this limits direct transfer to molecular recall and warns that known-answer tasks can be dominated by representation, search, and prompt effects rather than by the intended chemical complexity.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to adopt the similarity and accessibility protocol from [(Brown et al., 2018-11)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5bfeb6901db481c08874cfe0ae807d8564513765) to define the four subsets explicitly, using RDKit canonicalization, salt and charge handling, atom restrictions, SMILES length limits, ECFP4 or Morgan similarity thresholds, and a synthetic-accessibility proxy such as ChEMBL membership or an SA score; for GDB-13, precompute similarity clusters and SA or complexity scores, then create Sasp, Ssas, Sd>p, and Sd=p with documented thresholds, subset sizes, heavy-atom counts, ring counts, and train-test separation so that no test molecule or near-duplicate is present in pretraining. The second most important action is to prevent benchmark saturation by including best-in-dataset, random, unigram, and theoretical upper-bound baselines, and by reporting precision, recall, coverage, duplicate rate, equivalence-class recall, and statistical significance across seeds and generation counts rather than only top-scored optimization or validity. The third action is to use the complexity stratification from [(Liu et al., 2018-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b83e7e9a7bf59347eb9bc53ecaf139e301a8b72d) to validate that the four subsets differ in heavy-atom count, ring count, molecular weight, and SA, and to include validity, novelty, and uniqueness only as secondary diagnostics. The fourth action is to borrow the known-answer design from [(Brown et al., 2020-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90abbc2cf38462b954ae1b772fac9532e2ccd8b0) by using at least 10,000 known-complete examples per subset, testing model sizes 800K, 125M, and 1.3B, and comparing zero-shot, few-shot, pretrained, and fine-tuned settings to isolate recall from in-context or retrieval effects. The final action is to document implementation details for reproducibility, including SELFIES and SMILES canonicalization, equivalence-class computation, sampling temperatures, beam sizes, 1 to 10 million generation budgets, hardware, hyperparameters, and exact GDB-13 exclusion lists.

## Context: To instantiate the generative model experiments. Method: 'We will use the OPT 1.3B architecture as our base model, with pretraining on a large subset of GDB-13 molecules (excluding our test sets) followed by fine-tuning on the specific molecular subsets.'

### Support

The strongest support for using OPT 1.3B with pretraining on a large molecular subset and fine-tuning on specific subsets comes from [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948), which pre-trained a BART molecular language model on approximately 100 million ZINC-15 SMILES strings with molecular weight <= 500 Da and LogP <= 5, then fine-tuned on sequence-to-sequence and discriminative tasks, and showed that pre-trained weights outperformed randomly initialized weights on all evaluated datasets. This is directly analogous to the proposed pretraining-on-GDB-13-subset-then-fine-tuning design and to the planned ablation comparing pretrained versus randomly initialized models, although Chemformer used an encoder-decoder BART model with 45M and 230M parameters rather than a decoder-only OPT 1.3B model. The Chemformer evidence is especially relevant because it reports concrete training conditions, including 1,000,000 pretraining steps on 4 NVIDIA V100 GPUs with batch size 128 molecules per GPU, about 2.5 days for the 45M-parameter model and 6 days for the 230M-parameter model, 12-hour fine-tuning limits, beam width 10, and top-1, top-5, and top-10 evaluation, all of which provide a useful template for the proposed GDB-13/OPT 1.3B experiments. A second, weaker support is [(Xue et al., 2020-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/879cf44df4b083891ae3d8995a08a5cbd9b2e638), which also used large-scale unlabelled SMILES pretraining followed by task-specific fine-tuning for de novo generation and optimization, but provided only qualitative generation outcomes and did not report GDB-13, SELFIES, recall, model size, or statistical significance, so it supports the general pretrain-finetune paradigm less directly than the Chemformer evidence.

### Contradictions

The most important contradiction or caution is that [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) shows that representation equivalence can distort recall-like top-k evaluation: on-the-fly SMILES augmentation with probability 0.5 increased top-1 accuracy but reduced top-5 and top-10 accuracy because beam outputs contained multiple augmented SMILES forms of the same molecule, and no augmentation gave the lowest top-1 but the highest top-5 and top-10. This directly challenges any assumption that generating more diverse strings automatically improves recall, and it implies that the proposed SELFIES equivalence-class evaluation must canonicalize or deduplicate outputs before computing precision and recall. A second limitation is that neither related work uses the proposed GDB-13 complete-molecule benchmark, SELFIES, or OPT 1.3B: [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) used ZINC-15 SMILES with 99/0.5/0.5 splits, beam width 10, and top-1/top-5/top-10 rather than exhaustive recall over known complete sets, while [(Xue et al., 2020-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/879cf44df4b083891ae3d8995a08a5cbd9b2e638) used unspecified super-large SMILES corpora and non-GDB-13 tasks, so the evidence for the specific GDB-13/SELFIES/OPT 1.3B recall method remains indirect. The weakest contradiction is that both papers lack formal statistical significance and recall-specific metrics, and [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) also found that smaller Chemformer models outperformed the larger model on the percentage of desirable molecules in molecular optimization, which cautions against assuming that the 1.3B OPT model will automatically achieve higher recall than smaller 800K or 125M models.

### Suggested Action

Most importantly, the proposed method should define recall over SELFIES equivalence classes by canonicalizing or deduplicating generated molecules before scoring, and should explicitly report precision, recall, and duplicate/equivalent-class counts, because [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) shows that equivalent SMILES forms can reduce top-k coverage and confound generation evaluation. Next, the pretraining and fine-tuning protocol should borrow the Chemformer ablation and reporting details: compare pretrained versus randomly initialized OPT 1.3B models, use disjoint GDB-13 train/validation/test splits, report pretraining steps, batch size, learning-rate schedule, GPU hours, fine-tuning epochs, beam width, and top-k or recall metrics, and include a SMILES baseline to isolate the SELFIES effect. A third action is to implement the planned recall-oriented beam search with beam size equal to the number of desired generations but add duplicate suppression and equivalence-class merging, and compare it against random sampling and temperature sampling, since [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) demonstrates that beam search and augmentation can trade off top-1 accuracy against broader top-k coverage. Finally, the method should add statistical significance tests and smaller model controls, including the planned 800K and 125M models, to show that recall gains are not driven by model scale or sampling artifacts, while using [(Xue et al., 2020-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/879cf44df4b083891ae3d8995a08a5cbd9b2e638) as a weaker precedent for goal-directed generation and beam-search optimization rather than as direct evidence for recall.

## Context: To validate the recall prediction framework. Method: 'We will test our theoretical prediction framework by computing expected precision and recall using validation set probabilities, then compare predicted values against actual generation results across different sampling methods and temperatures.'

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed method is that [(Holtzman et al., 2019-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cf4aa38ae31b43fd07abe13b4ffdb265babb7be1) directly compares actual generation outcomes across sampling methods and temperatures, which is the same empirical axis the plan will use when comparing predicted precision and recall against actual generation results. The study generated 5,000 passages from GPT-2 Large, a 762M-parameter model trained on 40GB of WebText, conditioned on 1-40 held-out WebText tokens and capped at 200 tokens, and evaluated beam search, full sampling, top-k, nucleus, and temperature variants using likelihood/perplexity, Zipfian vocabulary distribution, repetition, and HUSE. This supports the plan's decision to vary sampling methods and temperatures rather than relying on a single decoding rule, because the paper shows that nucleus sampling achieved the highest HUSE score, top-k was second, beam search produced repetition loops, full sampling was hardest to understand, and top-k with temperature 0.7 devolved into repetition. A second, slightly weaker support is that the paper uses validation-set-like probability diagnostics, including language-model probability/perplexity and HUSE built from a KNN discriminator with k=13 and 200 generations per decoding scheme annotated by 20 annotators, totaling 4,000 annotations per scheme; this supports using validation probabilities as an intermediate signal for generation behavior, although it does not itself predict recall. A third support is that the paper's observation that sampling, top-k, and nucleus can be calibrated to human perplexities suggests that temperature and truncation parameters can be characterized through probability distributions, which is relevant to the plan's theoretical framework for i.i.d. sampling methods. The weakest but still useful support is the implementation detail that truncated sampling distributions were interpolated with 0.1 mass from the original distribution to avoid zero probabilities; this is directly relevant to the plan's computation of expected precision and recall from validation probabilities, because temperature, top-k, and nucleus sampling can create zero or near-zero probabilities that must be handled explicitly.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Holtzman et al., 2019-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cf4aa38ae31b43fd07abe13b4ffdb265babb7be1) does not test a recall prediction framework and does not compute expected precision or recall from validation-set probabilities, so it provides no direct evidence that the plan's core prediction method will work. The study was on open-ended WebText generation, where there is no complete set of correct outputs, no equivalence classes, and no molecular ground truth comparable to the GDB-13 subsets in the plan; therefore its HUSE and perplexity comparisons cannot validate the plan's claim that validation probabilities can predict recall over complete molecular sets. A second contradiction is the lack of numeric HUSE values and statistical significance tests in the excerpt, which weakens the evidence that predicted or measured generation quality differences are robust enough to support a precision/recall prediction benchmark. A third contradiction is that the paper found very high k or temperature values were needed to approximate the reference Zipf distribution while producing unnaturally high perplexity, suggesting that matching distributional or probability-based targets may not align with useful generation quality; this is a caution for the plan because expected precision and recall computed from validation probabilities may diverge from actual recall if the sampling distribution is distorted by temperature or truncation. A fourth contradiction is the domain mismatch: the model was GPT-2 Large on natural language, not OPT 1.3B on SELFIES molecules, and the evaluation did not involve molecular validity, canonical equivalence, or duplicate avoidance, so its findings cannot be transferred directly to the plan's molecular recall setting. The weakest contradiction is that beam search produced repetition loops, which contradicts the use of ordinary beam search for diverse generation, but the plan already proposes a novel recall-oriented beam search that avoids duplicates, so this is a caution rather than a direct refutation.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to make the recall prediction framework explicit and testable before scaling to 1-10 million molecules: derive closed-form or Monte Carlo estimates of expected precision and recall for random sampling, temperature sampling, top-k, nucleus, and the proposed duplicate-avoiding beam search, using validation-set probabilities and explicitly modeling truncation, temperature scaling, and zero-probability smoothing, including an interpolation term analogous to the 0.1 mass used in [(Holtzman et al., 2019-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cf4aa38ae31b43fd07abe13b4ffdb265babb7be1). The next most important action is to validate the prediction on a small pilot with complete ground truth, such as one GDB-13 subset, by comparing predicted precision and recall against actual generated molecules and reporting confidence intervals, bootstrap or permutation tests, and calibration curves rather than only point estimates, because the related work lacked numeric HUSE values and significance tests. A third action is to include the same sampling axes as the related work, namely temperature variants, top-k, nucleus, full sampling, and beam search, but replace human typicality with molecular precision, recall, uniqueness, validity, and equivalence-class coverage, so that the predicted values are tied to the plan's recall-oriented metrics. A fourth action is to address the beam-search failure mode observed in the related work by implementing duplicate-avoiding beam search and measuring repetition, unique valid molecules, and recall per beam size, since the related work showed ordinary beam search can loop. The least important but still useful action is to report computational resources and generation scale transparently, noting that the related work used 5,000 passages and 4,000 annotations per scheme, whereas the plan uses 1-10 million molecules and models of 800K, 125M, and 1.3B parameters, so the plan should include a smaller pilot to ensure the prediction framework is stable before large-scale generation.

## Context: To improve recall through training objectives. Method: 'We will design experiments with modified loss functions during fine-tuning, using batches containing multiple SELFIES representations of the same molecules and testing three aggregation functions (mean, minimum, maximum) to determine if forcing models to focus on single representations improves recall.'

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed fine-tuning loss experiments comes from [(Yüksel et al., 2023-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8489b55d992e6a3ac54aec7094a42ec8e333012f), which fine-tuned an 86M-parameter SELFIES-based RoBERTa transformer, SELFormer, on MoleculeNet using byte-level BPE SELFIES tokens, cross-entropy for classification and MSE for regression, with 80/10/10 splits and both random and scaffold splits. Its quantitative results—10% ROC improvement over MolCLR on scaffold SIDER, 12% average improvement over SMILES-based ChemBERTa on HIV, 18% improvement over MolBERT on BBBP, and >15% RMSE improvement over GEM on scaffold ESOL—show that SELFIES tokenization and transformer fine-tuning can be effective for molecular tasks, and its detailed hyperparameter search (100,000-molecule subset, attention heads 4/8/12, layers 4/8/12, learning rates 1e-1/1e-3/1e-5/5e-5, batch sizes 8/16/32, epochs 5/10/25, two NVIDIA A5000 GPUs, ~12 days) provides a reproducible template for the proposed OPT fine-tuning experiments. As an iterative line of work, this newer SELFIES transformer result builds on the earlier representation-level evidence in [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57), which showed that SELFIES improves generative validity and diversity on QM9: a 241-dimensional VAE decoded 100% valid molecules on random and displaced latent planes, and the best SELFIES GAN produced 78.9% unique valid molecules from 10,000 samples versus 18.6% for SMILES after 200 hyperparameter settings. This weaker but still relevant support justifies using SELFIES as the representation for recall-oriented generation because validity and unique-molecule diversity are prerequisites for recall, although the paper does not test loss aggregation or complete-set recall.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction or risk is that neither paper provides direct evidence that batching multiple SELFIES representations of the same molecule with mean, minimum, or maximum aggregation improves recall. [(Yüksel et al., 2023-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8489b55d992e6a3ac54aec7094a42ec8e333012f) achieved strong property-prediction performance using a single SELFIES string per molecule and standard CE/MSE losses, without equivalence-class aggregation, and did not report statistical significance; this suggests the proposed aggregation may add complexity, variance, or label noise without a demonstrated recall benefit. A second, more indirect contradiction is that [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57) attributes its gains to the SELFIES representation itself—100% validity and denser latent space—rather than to any loss that forces focus on a single representation, and its GAN metric of 78.9% unique valid molecules from 10,000 samples on QM9 is a diversity/validity measure, not a complete-set recall measure over GDB-13. Weaker still, both studies are mismatched to the proposed scale and evaluation: Krenn used QM9, VAE/GAN, 241-dimensional latent spaces, and 10,000 samples with no hardware or recall metrics, while Yuksek used MoleculeNet property prediction, 86M parameters, two A5000 GPUs, and no generation, no 1-10M sampling, no OPT 1.3B, and no precision/recall over complete molecular sets.

### Suggested Action

Most important, reframe the method around a direct recall benchmark before testing aggregation: use GDB-13 subsets as complete ground-truth sets, canonicalize or compute SELFIES equivalence classes, and evaluate precision/recall after 1-10M generations with duplicate removal, rather than relying on validity or unique-molecule diversity as in [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57). Second, establish a single-SELFIES fine-tuning baseline using standard cross-entropy or autoregressive loss, following the SELFIES tokenization and fine-tuning protocol of [(Yüksel et al., 2023-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8489b55d992e6a3ac54aec7094a42ec8e333012f) but adapted to OPT 1.3B, and only then compare mean, minimum, and maximum aggregation over multiple SELFIES strings for the same molecule; report paired bootstrap confidence intervals or significance tests because neither related work reports significance. Third, make the aggregation loss explicit and stable: define whether losses are sequence-level or token-level, how multiple strings are weighted, and how min/max aggregation handles variable-length sequences, and pilot on 800K/125M models before 1.3B to control compute. Finally, use Krenn's validity/density checks and Yuksek's hyperparameter search details (learning rates, batch sizes, epochs, validation split, hardware) as secondary diagnostics and reproducibility controls, while keeping the primary metric as recall against complete GDB-13 subsets.

## Context: To assess generation scale and evaluation coverage. Method: 'All experiments will generate between 1-10 million molecules using random sampling as the baseline, with systematic evaluation of precision and recall metrics.'

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed 1-10M random-sampling baseline is [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57), which used random latent-space sampling from a VAE trained on QM9 and 10,000 samples from each of 200 SMILES and SELFIES GAN hyperparameter settings to diagnose validity and diversity; SELFIES decoding produced valid molecules across sampled latent planes, the SELFIES VAE latent space was more than two orders of magnitude denser than SMILES, and the best SELFIES GAN produced 78.9% diverse molecules versus 18.6% for SMILES, directly supporting random sampling as a coverage diagnostic and SELFIES as a validity-preserving representation for the plan's GDB-13, OPT, and 800K/125M/1.3B experiments. As an iterative progression, this 2019 SELFIES-validity and random-sampling result is extended by later large-scale molecular evaluation and sequence-model work. Next, [(Campos, and Ji, 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c449207a8a94412f34f18c05c8a02cec43635776) supports explicit random baselines and large-scale molecular evaluation by constructing MOLCAP from 81,230,291 unique molecules, using a 1,000,000-molecule training subset and a 5,000-molecule validation set, and comparing IMG2SMI, OSRA, and DECIMER against a random 5,000-molecule baseline with validity, exact match, BLEU, ROUGE, Levenshtein, and Tanimoto metrics; it also found SELFIES produced the most valid captions and beam search decreased performance, which is relevant to the plan's random-sampling baseline and SELFIES choice. Then [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) supports the need to evaluate generation coverage beyond top-1 accuracy by showing Chemformer and Chemformer-Large, with about 45M and 230M parameters and pre-trained on 100M SMILES strings on 4 NVIDIA V100 GPUs for 1,000,000 steps, generated up to 198,560 candidates with beam width 10 and that SMILES augmentation increased duplicate equivalent forms, causing top-5 and top-10 to converge toward top-1; this motivates the plan's precision and recall over equivalence classes and its model-size comparisons at 125M and 1.3B. Weaker support comes from [(Eckmann et al., 2022-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eb0edd6467ac747e36311d27fda1b76e3a623b4d), whose LIMO framework uses SELFIES and a ZINC-based MOSES random-generation corpus of about 2 million molecules but provides no quantitative random-generation outcomes in the excerpt, and from [(Vakili et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0dc299ad951690ebaa0bee0c49d1bb0ed86c4cba), whose 850K-molecule KRAS generation and virtual-screening workflow, 15 synthesized compounds, and SPR Kd values such as 1.4 µM for ISM061-018-2 show that large generated pools can yield useful candidates, but it does not use random sampling or complete-set precision and recall.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction to the proposed method is that naive random sampling and top-k generation can be confounded by equivalent representations, as shown by [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948), where SMILES augmentation with p_aug values of 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 produced comparable top-1 but lower top-5 and top-10 because beam outputs contained more augmented forms of the same molecule, reducing molecular diversity and causing top-5 and top-10 to converge toward top-1; this newer 2021 result contradicts a naive reading of [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57)'s diversity metric and contradicts the assumption that generating 1-10M random samples will automatically improve effective recall unless SELFIES strings are canonicalized and precision/recall are computed over unique equivalence classes. Next, [(Campos, and Ji, 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c449207a8a94412f34f18c05c8a02cec43635776) contradicts the expectation that random sampling will be a weak baseline that is easily outperformed, because existing systems performed only slightly better than random molecule selection on complex MOLCAP molecules, exact match remained under 10%, BLEU improved only about 17%, Levenshtein distance was 21, and beam search decreased performance, suggesting that random sampling may be necessary but not sufficient for recall evaluation and that beam search may reduce coverage. Then [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57) contradicts extrapolating random-sampling validity and diversity results to the proposed scale, because it sampled only 10,000 molecules per GAN setting, used QM9 rather than GDB-13, reported no precision or recall against a complete ground-truth set, and showed SMILES latent sampling produced only a small fraction of valid molecules, which is a risk for any SMILES ablation in the plan. Weaker contradictions come from [(Eckmann et al., 2022-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eb0edd6467ac747e36311d27fda1b76e3a623b4d), which mentions a random-generation task on a ZINC-based MOSES dataset of about 2 million molecules but provides no quantitative random-generation outcomes, sampling temperature, seed, hardware, or metric definitions, and from [(Vakili et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0dc299ad951690ebaa0bee0c49d1bb0ed86c4cba), which used a KRAS-specific 650-point training set, a 16-qubit Guadalupe quantum processor, Chemistry42 filters, and biological assays rather than a general random-sampling precision/recall benchmark, limiting its direct applicability to the proposed GDB-13 recall method.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to define precision and recall over canonical unique molecules rather than raw SELFIES strings, using RDKit or an equivalent canonicalizer to collapse SELFIES equivalence classes before scoring, and to report both raw and unique precision/recall, validity, uniqueness, and diversity, because [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) shows duplicate equivalent forms can reduce effective coverage and [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57) shows SELFIES validity is materially better than SMILES. The next most important action is to make the 1-10M random-sampling protocol statistically and computationally explicit by fixing random seeds, sampling temperatures, model checkpoints, and generation budgets, reporting confidence intervals or bootstrap significance for precision/recall, and including coverage curves versus number of generated molecules, because none of the related works provide statistical significance for million-scale random sampling and [(Campos, and Ji, 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c449207a8a94412f34f18c05c8a02cec43635776) shows random baselines can be close to model performance on difficult molecules; the plan should also report GPU hours, memory, and whether 10M generations are feasible for the 1.3B OPT model, using stratified subsampling with extrapolation if necessary. Then the plan should add baselines and ablations that directly test recall-oriented generation, including unigram models, random molecule sampling, random SELFIES sampling, temperature sampling, and a duplicate-avoiding beam search, while comparing pretrained versus randomly initialized OPT 800K, 125M, and 1.3B models and SELFIES versus SMILES, with training details such as steps, batch size, GPUs, epochs, and hyperparameters reported, following the scale and representation lessons from [(Irwin et al., 2021-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f7f690e003176316d0ee56fbcbfed4b6b0948) and [(Krenn et al., 2019-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8338a903d8078481ff8af777475f7394d00e9d57); the theoretical recall-prediction framework should also be compared against empirical random-sampling recall. A less critical but useful action is to include auxiliary metrics from [(Campos, and Ji, 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c449207a8a94412f34f18c05c8a02cec43635776), such as validity, exact match, BLEU, ROUGE, Levenshtein, and Tanimoto similarity, to diagnose why precision/recall changes, and to treat downstream property or biological filters, as in [(Vakili et al., 2024-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0dc299ad951690ebaa0bee0c49d1bb0ed86c4cba), only as secondary application analyses rather than the primary recall benchmark.

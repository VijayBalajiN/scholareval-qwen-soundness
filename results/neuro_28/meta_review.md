## Central analytic strategy: Our approach involves creating "proxy measures" of cognitive abilities using predictive modeling techniques across different units of analysis, followed by commonality analyses to understand shared variance | Synthesis: This is the core pipeline that converts behavioral, neural, genetic, and environmental information into comparable cognitive-ability proxies and tests their shared variance with mental health.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed central analytic strategy comes from [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf), which directly endorses the plan's nested leave-one-site-out cross-validation and internal 10-fold tuning for polygenic score construction. Choi et al. show that P-value threshold selection for PRS is a model-selection step that can overfit the target sample if the most predictive threshold is chosen in the same data later used for association or downstream variance partitioning; they recommend optimizing thresholds in a training set and evaluating in an independent validation set, or using repeated cross-validation when independent data are unavailable. This aligns closely with the plan's use of 21 ABCD sites, where one site is held out as test and the remaining sites are used for 10-fold tuning of Elastic Net and PGS thresholds before predicted cognitive-ability values are extracted for commonality analyses. A second, more domain-specific support comes from [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9), which supports deriving a genetic proxy from large-scale cognitive GWAS summary statistics rather than from the ABCD sample alone. Lee et al. used educational attainment and related cognitive phenotypes, including cognitive performance, math ability, and highest math, with conditional and joint multiple-SNP analysis, Bayesian winner's-curse-adjusted posterior effect sizes, replication, cross-cohort genetic correlation, multi-trait GWAS, and EduYears polygenic scores, while applying standard SNP QC such as removing strand issues, invalid P values, minor allele count below 25, poor imputation, indels or non-autosomal variants, invalid or duplicated coordinates, allele mismatches, and UK Biobank variants absent from the Haplotype Reference Consortium panel. This supports the plan's decision to use three large-scale cognitive GWAS PGS definitions as a genetic proxy, although Lee et al. do not evaluate ABCD-specific predictive modeling, opportunistic stacking, or commonality with mental health. The weakest but still relevant support is again from Choi et al., whose power and accuracy design for PRS using traits with estimated heritabilities of 0.49, 0.23, and 0.11, 100,000 base individuals, 100,000 target individuals, validation samples of 50 to 3,000 repeated five times, and emphasis on empirical P-values supports the plan's need to report out-of-sample predictive performance and calibrated association evidence before interpreting commonality results.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction or caution comes from [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf), which warns that selecting the most predictive PRS threshold can overfit the target sample and that association P-values for an optimized PRS should be interpreted as empirical P-values estimated from the target data rather than nominal regression P-values. The proposed plan selects optimal PGS thresholds within training sets and then uses Elastic Net predicted values in commonality analyses, but it does not explicitly state that final PGS associations or commonality variance components will be permutation-calibrated or that predictive performance will be demonstrated on held-out sites before the commonality phase. If threshold selection or Elastic Net tuning leaks information from the test site, or if commonality analyses treat optimized predicted values as fixed observed measures, the shared variance between genetic, neural, environmental, and mental-health variables could be inflated. A second limitation is that [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9) does not provide quantitative outcomes such as genome-wide significant loci, effect sizes, replication statistics, genetic correlations, or polygenic prediction R2 or correlations in the supplied excerpt. Therefore, the related work supports the provenance of large-scale cognitive GWAS PGS but does not substantiate the assumption that these PGS will have sufficient predictive power in ABCD children to support stable commonality analyses. The weakest contradiction is also from Choi et al., who note that the optimal P-value threshold is not a fixed indicator of causal-variant fraction and depends on effect-size distribution, base and target sample power, genetic architecture, and causal-variant fraction, and that underpowered GWAS data can make a P-value threshold of 1, meaning all SNPs, optimal even when only a small fraction of SNPs is causal. This contradicts any implicit assumption that three fixed PGS definitions or a small set of conventional thresholds will capture the best genetic proxy without sensitivity analyses, and it suggests that the plan's Elastic Net plus threshold selection should be evaluated as a joint model-selection problem rather than as a fixed preprocessing step.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to make the PGS proxy fully out-of-sample and calibrated: for each leave-one-site-out fold, select PGS P-value thresholds and Elastic Net hyperparameters only using the 20 training sites and their internal 10-fold cross-validation, generate predictions for the held-out site, and report per-site and aggregate Pearson correlation, R2, MAE, and RMSE before any commonality analysis; if validation power is limited, use permutation-based empirical P-values for the association between the PGS proxy and cognitive or mental-health outcomes, as recommended by [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf). The next priority is to add the QC and provenance checks emphasized by both related works: apply standard base-GWAS and ABCD genotype QC, including genotyping rate, minor allele frequency, imputation information score, individual genotyping rate, strand and allele mismatch checks, removal of indels or non-autosomal variants, invalid or duplicated coordinates, Haplotype Reference Consortium panel filtering where appropriate, file-integrity checks such as md5sum, and population-stratification control using genetic principal components, consistent with [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9) and [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf). A third action is to treat PGS threshold selection as a sensitivity analysis rather than a fixed choice: compute PGS across a range of P-value thresholds, including P<1 if the base GWAS or ABCD target sample is underpowered, report the threshold selection curve, and compare threshold-based PGS with shrinkage-based approaches such as Elastic Net, ridge, LASSO, or Bayesian priors, as discussed by Choi et al. Finally, the plan should gate the commonality analyses on demonstrated predictive performance: if the out-of-sample R2 or correlation of the PGS proxy is low, the commonality results should be interpreted cautiously, with uncertainty intervals or Bayesian shrinkage of predicted values, and the manuscript should explicitly state that commonality analyses use only held-out predicted values, residualized for sex, age, and medication, with family and site random effects, to avoid inflating shared variance due to overfitting.

## Data foundation: We will use data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, examining children aged 9-10 at baseline and 11-12 at two-year follow-up | Synthesis: Provides the longitudinal, multi-site pediatric sample needed to estimate and validate the proposed proxies across development.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed method comes from [(Nimon et al., 2008-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ba8679cfce154f566d3bef504f286f6d8bc3b3cf), which provides an R-based workflow using regression and the commonalityCoefficients function to decompose variance shared among predictors. This directly aligns with the plan's commonality analysis phase, where predicted proxy measures of cognitive abilities from mental health, neuroimaging, polygenic scores, and environmental factors are entered into models to quantify shared variance with mental health outcomes, including the four planned comparisons: mental health with neuroimaging, mental health with PGS, mental health with environmental factors, and all four proxy measures. The next strongest support is [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9), which documents a QC filtering procedure for SNP association data before GWAS-based analyses, including removal of strand-ambiguous variants, invalid or out-of-range P values, minor allele count below 25, poor imputation accuracy, indels or non-autosomal variants, invalid or duplicated chromosomal coordinates, allele mismatches, and UK Biobank variants absent from the Haplotype Reference Consortium panel. This supports the plan's use of large-scale cognitive GWAS summary statistics to construct three PGS definitions, because such QC determines which variants are retained as valid genetic inputs for PGS derivation and reduces the risk of using invalid or poorly imputed variants in the Elastic Net prediction of cognitive abilities. The weakest support is that neither paper provides quantitative outcomes, shared-variance estimates, model comparisons, statistical significance, sample sizes, hardware, hyperparameters, or ABCD-specific validation, so their support is procedural rather than empirical for the proposed longitudinal pediatric design.

### Contradictions

The strongest potential contradiction is that [(Nimon et al., 2008-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ba8679cfce154f566d3bef504f286f6d8bc3b3cf) describes commonality analysis in an ordinary regression context and does not demonstrate that the same shared-variance decomposition is valid for the plan's random-intercept linear mixed models, family nesting within sites, repeated baseline and two-year follow-up measurements, missing neuroimaging data, or predicted proxy measures generated by PLS, Elastic Net, and Random Forest. If the plan assumes that commonality coefficients can be transferred directly from regression to LMMs, the related work does not support that assumption and may contradict the methodological soundness of the four commonality analyses. The next strongest contradiction is that [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9) shows that robust GWAS-based analyses require explicit QC of summary statistics, including minor allele count at least 25, imputation accuracy, autosomal restriction, HRC reference-panel membership for UK Biobank, and allele or coordinate checks, whereas the proposed method only states that PGS will be calculated from three large-scale cognitive GWAS and that population stratification will be controlled with genetic principal components. This omission creates a contradiction between the plan's PGS step and the evidence that unfiltered or poorly specified summary statistics can introduce invalid variants, strand errors, imputation artifacts, or ancestry mismatches. The weakest contradiction is that both related works lack quantitative outcomes, retention rates, predictive accuracy, effect sizes, statistical significance, computational resources, and ABCD-specific sample or site information, so they cannot confirm that the proposed predictive modeling and commonality pipeline will perform well in a 21-site pediatric cohort.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to validate and, if necessary, extend the commonality analysis to the plan's actual model structure before interpreting shared variance: use random-intercept linear mixed models with families nested within sites, repeated baseline and two-year follow-up observations, residualized cognitive abilities and proxy measures controlling for biological sex, age, and medication, and missing-data handling for the 45 neuroimaging feature sets and 44 environmental features; report shared-variance estimates with confidence intervals or bootstrap uncertainty, and compare the LMM commonality results against ordinary regression commonality as a sensitivity analysis, following the conceptual workflow of [(Nimon et al., 2008-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ba8679cfce154f566d3bef504f286f6d8bc3b3cf). The second most important action is to implement an explicit GWAS summary-statistic QC and PGS construction pipeline informed by [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9), including removal of strand-ambiguous variants, invalid or out-of-range P values, minor allele count below 25, poor imputation accuracy, indels or non-autosomal variants, invalid or duplicated coordinates, allele mismatches, and variants absent from the appropriate reference panel such as HRC; additionally specify LD clumping, ancestry matching, genetic principal components, PGS threshold selection within training sites, and report variant retention rates and PGS correlations. The third action is to add reproducibility and evaluation details for the ABCD pipeline: report sample sizes at baseline and follow-up, the 21-site leave-one-site-out cross-validation, 10-fold hyperparameter tuning, the two-layer opportunistic stacking across 45 neuroimaging feature sets using Elastic Net and Random Forest, PLS loadings, Elastic Net coefficients, Random Forest SHAP values, Pearson correlation, R-squared, MAE, RMSE, statistical significance, and comparisons to baselines such as single neuroimaging feature sets, single PGS definitions, or environmental-only models. The least important but still useful action is to document computational resources, software versions, imputation methods, standardization procedures, and the CFA g-factor specification for the six cognitive tasks so that the proxy measures and commonality analyses can be reproduced across the longitudinal pediatric sample.

## Primary shared-variance inference: We will extract predicted values from each predictive model as proxy measures of cognitive abilities and conduct four sets of commonality analyses using random-intercept linear mixed models | Synthesis: Tests the shared variance among mental-health, neuroimaging, polygenic, and environmental proxies, including the four specified pairwise and comprehensive analyses.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed method comes from [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf), which directly addresses the polygenic-score component of the plan. The paper recommends treating PGS P-value thresholds and shrinkage parameters as tuning parameters to be optimized in the target sample, warns against a single arbitrary threshold because it can underfit, and recommends out-of-sample prediction, repeated cross-validation, or permutation-based empirical P-values to control overfitting. This supports the plan's decision to select optimal PGS thresholds within training sets and to use nested leave-one-site-out cross-validation across 21 ABCD sites with 10-fold internal tuning, provided the test site is never used to choose thresholds or Elastic Net coefficients. A second, more indirect support comes from [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953), which provides a computationally efficient penalized least squares implementation for linear mixed models, including sparse Cholesky factorization, compressed sparse column storage, precomputed cross-products, profiled REML/deviance evaluation, and output for covariance matrices, conditional variances, p-values, confidence intervals, fitted values, and predicted values. This supports the plan's use of random-intercept LMMs with families nested within sites and residualized cognitive/proxy variables, because the method can handle sparse random-effects design matrices and repeated objective evaluations needed for stable mixed-model fitting. As an iterative sequence, the 2014 LMM implementation provides the older computational foundation for mixed-model inference, while the 2020 PRS protocol adds newer predictive-validation constraints that the proposed method must satisfy before using predicted values as proxies. Weaker support is that both works are methodological rather than empirical for the proposed ABCD commonality pipeline: Choi's excerpt describes a validation design with base and target samples of 100,000 individuals for traits with estimated heritabilities of 0.49, 0.23, and 0.11, validation samples of 50 to 3,000 individuals, and five repetitions, but does not report the resulting R2, correlations, or P-values; Bates illustrates output methods with a sleepstudy example but does not report dataset size, hardware, hyperparameters, or validation conditions. Thus, they support the statistical and computational building blocks, not the novel shared-variance decomposition itself.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf) cautions that PGS performance depends on base and target sample power, genetic architecture, and the fraction of causal variants, and that optimization in the target sample requires an independent validation sample, repeated cross-validation, or permutation testing to avoid overfitting. The proposed plan uses predicted values from PGS, neuroimaging, PLS, Elastic Net, and Random Forest models as proxy measures in commonality analyses, but if any of those predictions are generated in-sample, if PGS thresholds or Elastic Net hyperparameters are tuned using the held-out site, or if follow-up data influence baseline predictions, the shared-variance estimates will be inflated and the commonality analyses will not be valid. The related work therefore contradicts any implementation in which proxy measures are not strictly out-of-sample. A second contradiction is that [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953) does not implement or evaluate commonality analyses, shared-variance decomposition, or cognitive proxy modeling; it only supports fitting and inference for linear mixed models. Consequently, there is no direct evidence from this work that random-intercept LMMs can validly decompose unique and common variance among multiple predicted proxy measures, especially when those proxies are correlated, derived from the same outcome, and embedded in a nested family-site structure. Weaker contradictions arise from the absence of quantitative validation in both excerpts: Choi's excerpt does not provide R2, correlation coefficients, effect sizes, or association P-values for the PRS methods, and Bates does not provide benchmark comparisons, statistical significance values, or performance metrics for a specific dataset. This leaves the proposed method's key assumption, that predicted values are sufficiently accurate and stable proxies for cognitive abilities, unsupported by the cited evidence.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to enforce strict out-of-sample prediction for every proxy measure before commonality analysis: for each of the 21 ABCD sites, fit PGS threshold selection, Elastic Net, PLS, and Random Forest stacking only on the remaining sites, predict the held-out site, and aggregate predictions; use repeated cross-validation or permutation-based empirical P-values when the goal is association testing rather than individual prediction, as recommended by [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf). Report out-of-sample Pearson correlation, R2, MAE, and RMSE for each proxy, and do not use in-sample predictions or test-site-tuned thresholds in the four commonality analyses. The second most important action is to implement the random-intercept LMMs using a validated sparse mixed-model engine such as the penalized least squares and sparse Cholesky approach described by [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953), with families nested within sites, residualized cognitive abilities and proxy measures, and covariates for biological sex, age, and medication; use REML for variance-component estimation, ML for model comparison, and report convergence diagnostics, covariance matrices, conditional variances, p-values, and confidence intervals. The third action is to add genomic QC and PGS validation details aligned with [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf), including base and target genomic QC, LD handling, MAF/imputation checks, genetic principal components for population stratification, and optimization of PGS thresholds by out-of-sample predictive performance rather than arbitrary P-value cutoffs; consider shrinkage or Bayesian PGS methods if thresholding underfits. The fourth action is to validate the commonality inference itself with simulations or known ground-truth data, including sensitivity analyses for different random-effects structures, missing neuroimaging data, imputation of environmental features, and multiple-testing correction, and to repeat the analyses at baseline and two-year follow-up to demonstrate stability.

## Generalizability validation: We will implement nested leave-one-site-out cross-validation to ensure generalizability across different sites, treating one of 21 sites as a test set while using the remaining sites for training. Within each training set, we will apply 10-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning | Synthesis: Ensures that predictive proxies are not site-specific and that hyperparameters are selected without leakage.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed nested leave-one-site-out validation is that [(Morris et al., 2021-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b6365360938e3ff69bdb19f5809c3f4b578136f6) used a nested participant-level 10-fold outer evaluation with an inner 10-fold grid search to tune Elastic Net lambda1 and lambda2, directly demonstrating that inner tuning and outer held-out evaluation can avoid hyperparameter leakage in regularized predictive models. This is methodologically aligned with the plan's use of inner 10-fold cross-validation inside each 20-site training set for Elastic Net neuroimaging sets and PGS models. A second, more indirect support is that the same study selected a functional degree-count threshold by comparing models at 15%, 20%, and 25% connection thresholds and choosing the threshold with the smallest outer-fold RMSE, which supports the plan's strategy of selecting PGS thresholds within training sets rather than on the final test site. Weaker but still relevant support comes from the reported predictive outcomes: multimodal R2 = 0.22, RMSE = 0.10, MAE = 0.09, p < 0.001; imaging-only R2 = 0.15, RMSE = 0.11, MAE = 0.09, p < 0.001; and non-imaging R2 = 0.06, RMSE = 0.12, MAE = 0.10, p = 0.001, assessed with 1000 nonparametric permutations. These results show that nested regularized regression can extract modest but significant signal from high-dimensional neuroimaging and non-neuroimaging features, which is consistent with the plan's expectation that proxy measures of cognitive abilities may be imperfect but usable for commonality analyses. The study also provides implementation details that support careful preprocessing and feature construction, including FreeSurfer v5.3 cortical thickness and surface area features, CONN v19c/SPM12 functional preprocessing with 2 mm voxels, 6 mm smoothing, 0.01-0.1 Hz filtering, CompCor, and motion regression, although these details are from an older-adult adherence task rather than ABCD children.

### Contradictions

The most important limitation is that [(Morris et al., 2021-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b6365360938e3ff69bdb19f5809c3f4b578136f6) did not use leave-one-site-out validation; it used random participant-level 10-fold cross-validation within a single dataset of N = 131 older adults, mean age 65.79 years, 71.6% female, 89.7% White, predicting exercise adherence. Therefore it does not provide direct evidence that the proposed 21-site ABCD generalizability strategy will succeed, nor does it test whether predictive proxies remain stable when a whole site is held out. The next major limitation is population and outcome mismatch: the related work modeled adherence in older adults, not cognitive abilities or mental health in children aged 9-12, so its feature effects, such as positive functional degree-count nodes in default mode, dorsal attention, and frontoparietal control networks and negative nodes in somatomotor, visual, temporoparietal, and ventral attention regions, cannot be assumed to transfer to ABCD cognitive-mental health modeling. A further concern is sample size and tuning stability: the study had N = 131 with 38 covariates in the multimodal model and reported 96% power to detect an effect size of 0.22, while a sensitivity sample of N = 59 had only 60% power; moreover, nested Elastic Net failed for the non-imaging model and a 10-fold Ridge regression with lambda2 = 100 was used instead, indicating that inner 10-fold tuning can be unstable for smaller or weaker-signal feature blocks. This is relevant to the plan's 45 neuroimaging sets, 44 environmental features, and missing-data stacking, where some sets may have low signal or high missingness. Additional methodological caution is that the related work removed features not correlated with adherence at p < 0.1 before modeling and regressed out age, biological sex, and mean framewise displacement; if such preprocessing or feature selection is applied globally rather than inside each training site, it can introduce leakage, so the plan must ensure all residualization, imputation, standardization, genetic principal components, PGS threshold selection, and feature selection are fit only on the 20 training sites. Finally, the related work did not evaluate opportunistic stacking, Random Forest, PLS, polygenic scores, missing-data handling, family structure, or commonality analysis, and it did not report hardware or cluster resources, limiting direct reproducibility guidance for the larger ABCD pipeline.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to implement the nested validation exactly as a site-level hierarchy: for each of the 21 ABCD sites, hold out that entire site as the test set, train on the remaining 20 sites, and perform all inner 10-fold hyperparameter tuning, feature selection, imputation, standardization, residualization of biological sex/age/medication, genetic principal component estimation, PGS threshold selection, and CFA or proxy construction only within the 20 training sites; this directly addresses the main gap in [(Morris et al., 2021-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b6365360938e3ff69bdb19f5809c3f4b578136f6), which used random participant folds and therefore did not test site generalizability. Second, adopt the related work's threshold-selection logic but make it leakage-safe: for each held-out site, select PGS thresholds and any neuroimaging feature-set thresholds using inner CV or a nested outer CV within the training sites, and report the selected threshold per site rather than a single global threshold. Third, because nested Elastic Net failed in the related work's non-imaging model and required a Ridge fallback, specify fallback models and stability checks for each of the 45 neuroimaging sets and for PLS/Random Forest stacking, such as Ridge/LASSO alternatives, repeated inner CV, bootstrap confidence intervals, and per-site performance distributions. Fourth, compute power and uncertainty for site-level test sets, because the related work's N = 131 sample and 38-covariate model had 96% power for R2 = 0.22 but only 60% power at N = 59; ABCD site test sets may vary in size, so report per-site R2, RMSE, MAE, Pearson correlation, 95% confidence intervals, and permutation-based p-values using at least 1000 permutations as in the related work. Fifth, add a leakage audit and reproducibility report: document software versions, random seeds, hardware/cluster resources, preprocessing parameters, missing-data imputation, family structure handling, and whether family members are kept within the same site fold or additionally split by leave-one-family-out to avoid familial leakage. Finally, compare site-level LOO-SO performance against participant-level nested CV to quantify the site generalizability penalty, since the related work only provides participant-level evidence and cannot benchmark the expected drop in R2 or RMSE when whole sites are held out.

## Mental-health proxy model: We will use Partial Least Squares (PLS) to predict cognitive abilities from mental health features, testing CBCL and temperament measures separately and combined | Synthesis: Creates the mental-health-derived proxy measure used in commonality analyses.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed mental-health proxy model is that [(Rohart et al., 2017-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13653c06f1cecd84cc692f6195c33cc9b849139c) describes a supervised PLS-based multivariate projection framework, including PLS-DA and sparse PLS-DA, that is directly aligned with using PLS to project CBCL and temperament features onto latent components for predicting a cognitive g-factor. The paper emphasizes cross-validated selection of the number of components, evaluation of variable signatures, stability assessment, and prediction on held-out samples, which maps onto the plan's nested leave-one-site-out validation, inner 10-fold tuning, PLS loadings, and stability checks across baseline and two-year follow-up. A weaker but still relevant support is the paper's graphical output, such as prediction-area plots, which can be used to inspect held-out site predictions and component-level fit. The high citation count, 3520, is only indirect support that the PLS framework is methodologically established, but it does not provide outcome evidence for this specific ABCD regression task.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the supplied excerpt from [(Rohart et al., 2017-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13653c06f1cecd84cc692f6195c33cc9b849139c) contains no numerical results, so it does not provide quantitative evidence that PLS will achieve meaningful Pearson correlation, R2, MAE, RMSE, or statistical significance when predicting cognitive abilities from CBCL or temperament features. A second contradiction is task mismatch: the related work centers on PLS-DA classification and multi-omics DIABLO integration, whereas the proposed method is PLS regression on child mental-health scales to create a continuous cognitive proxy, so its validation logic cannot be transferred without modification. A third contradiction is the absence of dataset size, population, hardware, hyperparameters, exact cross-validation details, baseline comparisons, missing-data handling, and controls for sex, age, medication, family structure, or site effects, all of which are central to the ABCD plan and reproducibility. The weakest contradiction is that the related work does not address sparse or multi-block modeling of CBCL and temperament as distinct psychological blocks, leaving the proposed combined model under-specified relative to the paper's sparse PLS-DA and DIABLO options.

### Suggested Action

Most important, implement the mental-health proxy as PLS regression rather than PLS-DA, and select the number of components by inner 10-fold cross-validation inside each leave-one-site-out fold, reporting outer-site Pearson correlation, R2, MAE, RMSE, and permutation-based significance, following the component-selection and held-out prediction emphasis in [(Rohart et al., 2017-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13653c06f1cecd84cc692f6195c33cc9b849139c). Second, use sparse PLS or a multi-block PLS/DIABLO-style formulation for the combined CBCL and temperament model so that the two measurement blocks are not simply concatenated, and report stable loadings or variable signatures with bootstrap or permutation stability checks. Third, add baselines such as null or mean models, ridge or Elastic Net, and Random Forest, and compare them against PLS to justify the choice of PLS for the mental-health proxy. Fourth, include prediction-area plots or equivalent held-out prediction diagnostics, sensitivity analyses for imputation and standardization, and explicit controls for biological sex, age, medication, family structure, and site to align the evaluation with the ABCD commonality-analysis plan.

## Neuroimaging proxy model: We will implement opportunistic stacking with two layers: (1) set-specific layer using Elastic Net to predict cognitive abilities from each of 45 neuroimaging feature sets, and (2) stacking layer using Random Forest to combine predictions across all sets. This approach will handle missing neuroimaging data while maximizing predictive performance | Synthesis: Creates the multimodal neuroimaging proxy from task-fMRI, resting-state fMRI, structural MRI, and diffusion tensor imaging.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed neuroimaging proxy model is that [(Sripada et al., 2019-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ad7a06e50cc77c7c95795eab5c879f45bd3fb52) demonstrates that predictive models for General Ability can show substantial cross-site generalizability, which directly supports the research plan's use of nested leave-one-site-out validation across 21 ABCD sites and the broader goal of deriving a generalizable neuroimaging proxy for cognitive ability. A secondary, but still relevant, support is that the paper derives a cognitive-ability proxy from neuroimaging features, aligning with the plan's operationalization of a g-factor-like target from brain imaging rather than from behavioral scores alone. The weakest support is that the reported per-site predictors emphasized connections within and between task control networks and the default mode network, including hub-like structures, which suggests that some of the 45 proposed feature sets, especially task-fMRI and resting-state connectivity features, may contain informative signal; however, this support is limited because the excerpt does not specify whether those features correspond to the plan's 45 multimodal sets, whether they were selected from task-fMRI, resting-state fMRI, structural MRI, or diffusion tensor imaging, or whether they were combined with other modalities.

### Contradictions

The most important contradiction is not a direct empirical refutation but a lack of evidence for the proposed method's core architecture: [(Sripada et al., 2019-08)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ad7a06e50cc77c7c95795eab5c879f45bd3fb52) does not report use of two-layer opportunistic stacking, set-specific Elastic Net models, a Random Forest meta-learner, or 45 multimodal neuroimaging feature sets, so it cannot support the claim that this specific pipeline will maximize predictive performance while handling missing data. The next strongest concern is that the excerpt provides no quantitative performance values, baseline comparisons, statistical significance, per-site effect sizes, cross-validation scheme, hyperparameters, or evaluation metrics, leaving the proposed method's expected performance and generalizability unvalidated against simpler alternatives such as single-modality Elastic Net, ridge regression, lasso, support vector regression, or linear stacking. A further limitation is that the paper does not address missing neuroimaging modalities, missingness mechanisms, imputation, or how a meta-learner should behave when some of the 45 set-specific predictions are unavailable; therefore the proposed missing-data advantage of Random Forest stacking is unsupported. ## Genetic proxy model: We will use Elastic Net to predict cognitive abilities from three PGS definitions, selecting optimal PGS thresholds within training sets and controlling for population stratification using genetic principal components | Synthesis: Creates the polygenic proxy measure while reducing ancestry-related confounding.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed Elastic Net genetic proxy comes from [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf), which explicitly treats PRS construction as a shrinkage or threshold-tuning problem and recommends out-of-sample prediction, training and validation splits, repeated cross-validation, and empirical P-values when independent validation is limited. This directly aligns with the plan’s nested leave-one-site-out design across 21 ABCD sites and internal 10-fold tuning for Elastic Net and PGS threshold selection, because Elastic Net is a regularized shrinkage method and the proposed threshold selection is analogous to the protocol’s P-value threshold optimization. Read iteratively, this newer protocol builds on the older PGS evidence in [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9), which shows that large-scale cognitive and educational GWAS can produce PGS with meaningful predictive validity: an educational-attainment PGS from 12,887 independent SNPs at P < 5e-8 explained 11.4% of variance in a UK Biobank prediction sample and 13.6% in a 23andMe sample, while MTAG across educational attainment, cognitive performance, math ability, and highest math increased independent SNPs to 14,251 and raised prediction to 13.6% and 15.4% respectively. This supports using three PGS definitions derived from large-scale cognitive GWAS as inputs to a proxy model, especially if one definition is a multi-trait MTAG score. The weakest but still relevant support is the detailed SNP QC and ancestry-aware prediction context in [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9), including removal of strand issues, invalid association values, minor allele count below 25, poor imputation, indels, non-autosomal loci, invalid or duplicated coordinates, allele mismatches, and HRC panel retention for UK Biobank full-release analyses; these QC steps are directly transferable to constructing ABCD PGS inputs and to the plan’s need to reduce ancestry-related confounding with genetic principal components.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is not that PGS proxies are invalid, but that the proposed method is under-specified relative to the validation standards emphasized by [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf). This newer protocol does not invalidate the older PGS evidence in [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9), but it contradicts any assumption that adult GWAS evidence alone justifies a simple training-set threshold selection. The protocol warns that optimal P-value thresholds and shrinkage parameters depend on base and target power, effect-size distribution, genetic architecture, and causal-variant fraction, and that underfitting can be as problematic as overfitting; it also recommends empirical P-values or permutation when association inference is the goal. The proposed method only states that optimal PGS thresholds will be selected within training sets and population stratification controlled with genetic principal components, without specifying repeated validation, empirical P-values, permutation, or how threshold selection will be protected from overfitting in the ABCD site-level test sets. This is a direct methodological gap because the plan later uses predicted values as proxy measures in commonality analyses, where inflated or unstable predictions could bias shared-variance estimates. A second contradiction or limitation is that [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9) provides adult GWAS and prediction evidence, not child-specific validation: the educational-attainment GWAS used 280,865 adults, cognitive traits used UK Biobank samples of about 11,539, and prediction samples were approximately 45,000 UK Biobank and 25,000 23andMe adults. The reported variance explained, 11.4% to 15.4%, is modest and does not establish that the same three PGS definitions will capture cognitive g in 9-10-year-old ABCD children, especially after residualizing age, sex, and medication and controlling family structure. A weaker contradiction is that the supplied excerpt from [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9) does not report Elastic Net hyperparameters, PGS threshold selection, genetic principal component counts, or cognitive-specific PGS outcomes, so it cannot directly validate the proposed Elastic Net implementation or the choice of three PGS definitions. Likewise, [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf) evaluates traits with heritabilities of 0.49, 0.23, and 0.11 using 100,000 base and target samples and validation samples of 50 to 3,000 individuals repeated five times, but it does not provide a quantitative benchmark for ABCD’s site-level test sets, so the plan must not assume that 10-fold internal tuning alone will yield stable out-of-site predictions.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to make the PGS proxy a fully nested, out-of-sample regularized prediction pipeline consistent with [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf): for each leave-one-site-out fold, tune Elastic Net alpha or l1_ratio and each PGS threshold or shrinkage parameter by 10-fold cross-validation on the training sites only, then evaluate the held-out site using Pearson correlation, R2, MAE, and RMSE; repeat the site-level folds or use repeated cross-validation to estimate stability, and if the proxy is used for association or commonality inference, compute empirical P-values or permutation tests on the held-out predictions rather than relying on training-set-optimized thresholds. The second priority is to define the three PGS inputs using the QC and multi-trait framework demonstrated by [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9): use ancestry-matched large-scale cognitive GWAS summary statistics, apply the same SNP filters (autosomal, MAF at least 25, valid strand, coordinates, and alleles, acceptable imputation, no indels or non-autosomal loci, and HRC compatibility where appropriate), and consider one single-trait cognitive PGS, one educational-attainment PGS, and one MTAG multi-trait PGS, reporting the number of independent SNPs and variance explained in an independent adult validation sample before applying them to ABCD. The third priority is to specify population-stratification control: select the number of genetic principal components by validation performance or eigenvalue criteria within the training sites, include site and family random effects in the downstream random-intercept linear mixed models, and report how many PCs were used and whether they improved held-out-site prediction. The least urgent but still important action is to document reproducibility constraints: sample sizes per site, missingness, imputation panels, computational resources, and whether the ABCD PGS definitions are derived from the same ancestry as the ABCD cohort, because the adult evidence in [(Lee et al., 2018-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d49aaa352d4d714f564ea6d8f79b51f558f42ef9) and the power-dependent threshold guidance in [(Choi et al., 2020-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c28110039f725c98fe48a687cca1b853d0588fbf) both imply that prediction performance and threshold choice will depend on base GWAS size, target sample size, heritability, and genetic architecture.

## Confounding and dependency controls: We will control for biological sex, age, and medication effects by residualizing these variables from observed cognitive abilities and proxy measures. ### Support

The strongest support for the confounding and dependency controls method is that [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953) describes a linear mixed-model fitting method that can represent grouping factors and estimate fixed effects under non-independent observations, which matches the plan's need to model families nested within sites after residualizing biological sex, age, and medication from cognitive and proxy outcomes. It specifically supports random-intercept linear mixed models with sparse random-effects matrices, fixed-effects model matrices, case weights, and covariance parameters, so it can accommodate the ABCD design with 21 sites, family-level clustering, and repeated proxy measures derived from mental health, neuroimaging, PGS, and environmental models. A second, more implementation-level support is that the method uses sparse column-compressed storage, separates symbolic and numeric Cholesky phases, and stores weighted cross-products such as X'WX, X'Wy, Z'WX, Z'Wy, and Z'WZ to speed repeated evaluations, which is relevant to the computational burden of fitting multiple commonality models across baseline and two-year follow-up. A weaker but still useful support is that the output module provides fixed-effect coefficient covariance, conditional variances of random effects, p values, confidence intervals, and profile-based diagnostics, which can be used to report uncertainty for proxy-measure coefficients and to assess stability across time points, although the paper itself only illustrates this with a sleep study example rather than a neurodevelopmental or mental-health dataset.

### Contradictions

The strongest limitation that contradicts the proposed method is that [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953) provides no quantitative experimental outcomes, benchmark comparisons, accuracy values, statistical significance, variance-decomposition results, or validation in a neurodevelopmental, cognitive, or mental-health population, so it cannot by itself confirm that residualizing biological sex, age, and medication and then fitting family-nested random-intercept models will adequately control confounding in the ABCD commonality analyses. A second contradiction is that the paper does not address commonality analysis, shared variance partitioning, or proxy measures generated by PLS, opportunistic stacking, Elastic Net, Random Forest, or PGS; it only supplies a mixed-model fitting engine, so the plan's core inferential step of decomposing shared variance among mental health, neuroimaging, genetic, and environmental proxies is not empirically supported by this work. A third, weaker contradiction is that the method assumes a linear mixed-model framework with sparse random-effects matrices and Cholesky-based optimization, and it does not evaluate complex covariance structures, nonlinear age effects, medication interactions, missing-data mechanisms, or convergence behavior in large multi-site developmental cohorts, all of which are relevant to the plan's residualized cognitive g-factor, CBCL, BIS/BAS, UPPS-P, 45 neuroimaging feature sets, three PGS definitions, and 44 environmental features.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to implement the family and site dependency controls using the linear mixed-model framework described by [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953), but to specify the random structure explicitly for the ABCD design, such as a random intercept for site and a random intercept for family nested within site, for example (1|site/family) or (1|site)+(1|family) when family identifiers are unique, and to include biological sex, age, and medication as fixed effects in the model rather than relying solely on residualization; if residualization is retained, it should be performed with the same functional form used in the model and the residuals should be reported as sensitivity analyses.

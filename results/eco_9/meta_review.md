## Experiment 1 developmental timing manipulation to create early-leafing versus late-leafing trees: We will place some trees in climate chambers from April 4 to May 24, cooling them to 2-7°C with simulated day-night cycles, while others remain under ambient conditions.

### Support

The strongest support for Experiment 1 is conceptual: [(Hänninen, and Kramer, unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/239007d37520659dd11b7452a76ac39fd4de54e5) used controlled-condition experiments to isolate specific sub-processes of the tree annual cycle, including the temperature response of rest break and the effect of rest-break state on ontogenetic competence, which is directly analogous to the proposed use of climate chambers from April 4 to May 24 to cool some of the 267 Fagus sylvatica saplings to 2-7°C with simulated day-night cycles in order to create early-leafing versus late-leafing developmental states before testing downstream autumn bud set. A second, weaker but relevant support is the related work’s use of natural-condition observations for statistical fitting of whole-process models, which supports the proposed retention of ambient-control trees and randomized blocks from May 24 to June 21 and after the July and August cooling treatments, because the proposed design also relies on ambient conditions to anchor the controlled manipulations. A still weaker support is the related work’s use of the timing of a final phenological event, bud burst, as the response variable for model fitting; this supports the proposed operational endpoint of bud set defined as the date when each terminal bud reaches 90% of maximum length, because both approaches reduce a multi-month developmental process to a measurable timing variable that can be compared across treatments. However, the support is limited because the related work does not report quantitative outcomes, effect sizes, statistical tests, sample sizes, chamber temperatures, photoperiods, species, or treatment durations, so it cannot confirm that 2-7°C cooling over April 4 to May 24 will reliably shift leaf-out dates or that the resulting developmental timing will alter responses to the proposed July and August cooling treatments in the Solstice-as-Phenology-Switch framework.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is not a direct empirical refutation but a lack of evidence for the specific proposed manipulation: [(Hänninen, and Kramer, unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/239007d37520659dd11b7452a76ac39fd4de54e5) does not provide data showing that 2-7°C chamber cooling from April 4 to May 24, with simulated day-night cycles, will generate the intended early-leafing versus late-leafing contrast in 267 potted European beech saplings, nor does it show that such spring developmental timing will change autumn bud set timing under the planned post-solstice July and August cooling treatments. A weaker contradiction is methodological mismatch: the related work focused on bud-burst timing and parameter estimation for operational phenology models, using numerical optimization and simulated annealing when conventional estimation produced unrealistic parameter estimates, whereas the proposed method uses bud set defined at 90% maximum bud length and linear mixed-effects models with treatment as fixed effects and bud type as random effects; therefore, the related work does not validate the proposed statistical or endpoint framework for testing the solstice switch. A still weaker limitation is that the related work’s controlled experiments were used to isolate sub-processes such as rest break and ontogenetic competence, while the proposed experiment infers developmental constraints from an external temperature treatment and endpoint bud set without measuring the internal physiological states that the related work treats as necessary for whole-process understanding.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to add a pre-registered validation step before the summer treatments: monitor leaf-out dates, shoot elongation, and bud development in the April 4 to May 24 cooling and ambient groups to confirm that the 2-7°C treatment actually creates a measurable early-leafing versus late-leafing contrast in the 267 saplings, and if the contrast is weak, adjust the cooling duration, temperature range, or photoperiod before proceeding to the July and August treatments. The next important action is to strengthen the statistical and modeling plan by treating the spring cooling treatment as a fixed factor in the linear mixed-effects model alongside block, tree, and bud type, and by reporting effect sizes and uncertainty for bud set timing relative to ambient controls; if mechanistic phenology models are also fitted to the 90%-maximum-length bud set endpoint, the related work’s recommendation to use simulated annealing when conventional numerical optimization yields unrealistic parameter estimates should be adopted as a fallback. A less urgent but useful action is to include additional ambient or chamber-control replicates and, if feasible, measure internal developmental proxies such as rest-break status or ontogenetic competence, because [(Hänninen, and Kramer, unknown_d)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/239007d37520659dd11b7452a76ac39fd4de54e5) emphasizes that controlled experiments are most informative when they isolate specific sub-processes rather than relying only on endpoint phenology.

## Experiment 2 day/night temperature manipulation: Pre-solstice treatments (May 22 to June 21) and post-solstice treatments (June 22 to July 21) will include four conditions each: chamber control (continuous 20°C), day cooling (8°C day/20°C night), night cooling (20°C day/8°C night), and full-day cooling (continuous 8°C).

### Support

The strongest support for Experiment 2 is that [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) demonstrates that potted European beech can be subjected to controlled temperature manipulation in climate-controlled enclosures and that the imposed treatment produces measurable microclimatic differences. In that study, 128 three-year-old Fagus sylvatica saplings from the same local provenance were grown in 35 L pots with a 20% peat and 80% white sand mixture, with 8 saplings in each of 12 glasshouses and 32 outside reference saplings, and six glasshouses were warmed by 3°C relative to ambient while six remained at ambient temperature. The warmed treatment had significantly lower daily soil water content and relative humidity than the +0°C treatment, with P < 0.001 and estimated differences of about 0.025 m3/m3 for soil water content and 20% for relative humidity, showing that temperature manipulation can alter the physiological environment of potted beech and therefore justifies the proposed use of precise chamber controls for 20°C and 8°C day/night regimes. A second, somewhat weaker but still relevant point is that the +0°C glasshouses were not significantly different from the outside reference plots for temperature, relative humidity, or vapor pressure deficit, and the study maintained fertilization and irrigation regimes, which supports the proposed inclusion of a chamber control and a 36-tree ambient control, although the proposed chamber control is a fixed 20°C rather than the ambient +0°C condition used in the related work. A third point is that the study used autumn stress and defoliation endpoints, excluding 20 saplings with early or abrupt defoliation before 18 August and 18 saplings with constant chlorophyll content index below 3 from August to November, which supports the general feasibility of monitoring autumn phenological or stress responses in potted beech, but it does not directly validate bud set or CO2 assimilation as endpoints. Finally, the scale of the related experiment, with 128 saplings and 12 enclosures, provides indirect support for the logistical feasibility of a larger 180-tree, eight-treatment chamber experiment, although the proposed design uses 20 L pots and four-year-old trees rather than the 35 L pots and three-year-old trees used in the related work.

### Contradictions

The most important contradiction is that the closest related evidence does not validate the proposed cooling or day/night design and instead shows that manipulated potted beech can experience severe stress and that the treatment may not remain stable. [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) used a +3°C warming treatment combined with reduced irrigation of about 2.5 L once per week and polyethylene shielding from early May until 20 June, and the experiment experienced significant mortality that forced early alleviation of the drought treatment on 20 June, after which irrigation was increased and the glasshouses were opened from July onward; later analyses also excluded 20 saplings with early or abrupt defoliation before 18 August and 18 saplings with constant chlorophyll content index below 3 from August to November. This contradicts the proposed assumption that consistent watering and chamber control will make the 8°C day/night and continuous 8°C treatments low-risk, because even a modest +3°C warming with reduced water availability caused substantial defoliation or death, and the proposed 8°C cooling could impose chilling, altered VPD, or growth stress that is not tested in the related work. A second contradiction is that the related enclosure was not a neutral temperature-only environment: the glasshouses had a polycarbonate roof that reduced incoming light by about 20% and altered ultraviolet spectral quality, with three openable sides and sensors, so enclosure effects on light and UV could confound phenology and photosynthesis. This challenges the proposed assumption that climate chambers will differ only in temperature while simulating ambient day length and light intensity. A third contradiction is that soil water content was measured in only one pot per glasshouse and the soil moisture sensors became available only after the drought treatment was alleviated, so the study could not track the key water-status variable during the critical May-June treatment period; this contradicts the proposed reliance on adequate soil moisture unless the new experiment implements continuous, per-pot or per-chamber soil moisture monitoring. A fourth contradiction is that the related work used 35 L pots, whereas the proposed method uses 20 L pots for 180 four-year-old trees, so the smaller root volume may increase water stress and reduce reproducibility relative to the related setup. A fifth and weaker contradiction is that the related study did not measure bud set, did not separate daytime and nighttime temperatures, did not test pre-solstice versus post-solstice periods, and did not measure leaf-level CO2 assimilation, so it provides no direct evidence for the primary endpoint, the day/night contrast, or the solstice switch hypothesis.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to add a pilot or pre-experiment to test the safety and feasibility of the proposed 8°C day/night and continuous 8°C treatments on potted Fagus sylvatica before the full 180-tree experiment, with continuous logging of air temperature, relative humidity, vapor pressure deficit, soil water content, and light, and with automated irrigation calibrated to maintain adequate soil moisture; this directly addresses the mortality and early defoliation observed in [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922), where significant mortality forced early alleviation of the drought treatment and soil moisture sensors were available only after the treatment ended, and it also tests whether the chambers can maintain the target 20°C and 8°C setpoints without drift or forced opening through the full May 22 to July 21 windows. The second most important action is to ensure that the climate chambers do not introduce light, UV, or spectral confounds by measuring and matching PAR, UV, and day length across chamber control, day cooling, night cooling, full-day cooling, and ambient controls, and by using transparent or supplemental lighting if the enclosure reduces light, because the related glasshouse roof reduced incoming light by about 20% and altered ultraviolet spectral quality. The third action is to increase root volume or otherwise mitigate pot-size stress, for example by using larger pots than 20 L if feasible, using the same 20% peat and 80% white sand mixture or a comparable standardized mixture, and setting soil moisture thresholds based on continuous sensors, because the related work used 35 L pots and still experienced substantial stress. The fourth action is to standardize nutrient supply and phenological monitoring, for example by applying a documented fertilization schedule comparable to the related use of 35 g of slow-release NPK and 1.8 g of microelements per sapling in April and July, and by recording mortality, defoliation, chlorophyll content index, and leaf-level CO2 assimilation in addition to bud set, so that stressed trees can be modeled as covariates or excluded with pre-registered criteria. The fifth action is to strengthen the statistical and replication design by randomizing trees to the eight treatments and ambient control within blocks, pre-registering fixed effects for treatment, period, and their interactions, including tree and bud type as random effects, and reporting effect sizes, confidence intervals, and significance for bud set timing relative to the appropriate chamber or ambient controls, so that the proposed 18-tree treatment groups can be interpreted with the same caution about stress-induced attrition that the related work demonstrates.

## Primary phenological endpoint and measurement: We will use bud set as our primary marker for primary growth cessation and the beginning of autumn phenology, as it represents a key physiological transition point. We will measure terminal buds on primary shoots and lateral stems using digital calipers from July 4 to November 2. Bud set will be defined as the date when each bud reaches 90% of its maximum length, determined through linear interpolation between measurement dates.

### Support

As the only provided related work, the strongest support for the proposed method is the related study's use of a randomized 12-block common garden and linear mixed-effects models with garden block as a random effect, which directly parallels the proposed beech experiments' need to separate treatment effects from block, pot, and individual-tree variation in Experiments 1 and 2 [(Rodríguez et al., 2022-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06adf12aa9442f7005344d40c1e823fd7a1ecdad). That study fitted lme4 models for 14 traits with provenance or bioclimatic principal components as fixed effects and included tree height or cutting diameter as fixed covariates when correlated with the response, providing a concrete precedent for the proposed LME approach in which temperature treatment should be modeled as a fixed effect while accounting for correlated size or developmental covariates. A second, more indirect support is that the study quantified phenological and phenotypic traits under common-garden conditions, showing that phenological endpoints can be measured in a controlled garden setting, although it did not specifically measure bud set or manipulate temperature. The weakest support is methodological rather than biological: its use of marginal and conditional R2, emmeans pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction, and R 4.0.3/lme4/piecewiseSEM suggests that the proposed analysis should report variance partitioning and multiple-comparison control, but it does not validate the proposed digital-caliper protocol, the 90% maximum-length definition, or linear interpolation for bud set.

### Contradictions

No direct contradiction is provided, but the strongest counter-evidence is that the related work does not validate the proposed primary endpoint, because it did not manipulate temperature, did not report bud-set timing, and did not specify whether bud set was scored or measured with calipers, so the proposed 90% maximum-length caliper definition for terminal buds on primary shoots and lateral stems remains unsupported by this source [(Rodríguez et al., 2022-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06adf12aa9442f7005344d40c1e823fd7a1ecdad). A second important limitation is that fixed effects explained less than 10% of total phenotypic variation for most traits, with the largest marginal R2 values being tree height in 2019 and 2020 at 0.102 and 0.113 and flavonol content at 0.199, while conditional R2 was consistently higher, indicating that unmeasured common-garden microenvironmental variation among blocks can dominate over fixed biological effects; this challenges the assumption that the proposed cooling treatments will produce large, easily detectable shifts in bud set with only 18 to 27 trees per treatment group. A third limitation is system mismatch: the study used 549 Populus nigra ramets, 433 of which survived summer 2018, from 12 European provenances in a Marburg garden with 40 to 45 cuttings per block at 1 m spacing, no fertilizers, pesticides, or herbicides, and frequent watering, whereas the proposed method uses potted Fagus sylvatica saplings in 20 L pots, climate chambers, and day/night cooling, so the evidence cannot be directly transferred to the beech bud-set protocol. The weakest contradiction is that the related work's statistical implementation, including emmeans with Bonferroni correction and marginal R2 from piecewiseSEM, is not fully aligned with the proposed unspecified LME random structure, repeated-measurement handling, or effect-size framework, leaving a gap rather than a direct refutation.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to add a pilot or validation protocol for the bud-set endpoint before the main experiments, because the related work does not support the 90% maximum-length caliper definition; the plan should specify repeated non-destructive caliper measurements, inter-rater reliability, the definition of maximum length, how missing or damaged buds are handled, and why 90% rather than another threshold corresponds to growth cessation in Fagus sylvatica [(Rodríguez et al., 2022-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06adf12aa9442f7005344d40c1e823fd7a1ecdad). The next most important action is to strengthen the statistical design and power analysis, using the related study's evidence that fixed effects may explain less than 10% of variation and that block microenvironment can inflate conditional R2; the proposed LME should include treatment as fixed effects, block and chamber or pot as random effects where appropriate, tree as a random intercept, initial height or diameter as covariates, and a power analysis for detecting biologically meaningful bud-set shifts with 18 to 27 trees per treatment, while reconsidering bud type as a random effect because only two bud categories may be better modeled as a fixed factor. A further action is to control and report microenvironmental covariates, because the related common garden showed that unmeasured block variation can dominate; the plan should log chamber temperature, photoperiod, light intensity, humidity, soil moisture, and block position, and include these as fixed or random effects when justified. Finally, the analysis should align with the related work's reporting standards by using lme4 or equivalent, emmeans for treatment contrasts, Bonferroni or FDR correction, marginal and conditional R2, and effect sizes relative to the appropriate chamber or ambient controls, while also monitoring survival and accounting for mortality as the related study did with 433 of 549 ramets surviving.

## Statistical analysis: We will use linear mixed-effects models with treatment as fixed effects and bud type as random effects to analyze bud set timing, with effect sizes calculated relative to appropriate control treatments.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed statistical method is that [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953) describes a penalized least squares algorithm for linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects, random-effects terms, Gaussian likelihood, profiled deviance or REML criterion, and inference outputs including fixed-effect coefficient covariance, conditional random-effect variances, p-values, confidence intervals, profile plots, and fitted or predicted values. This directly matches the plan to use treatment as fixed effects and a random grouping factor to compare bud set timing against controls while accounting for repeated bud measurements and individual tree variation in 267 Experiment 1 saplings across 10 treatment groups and 180 Experiment 2 trees across 9 groups. A second, weaker but still relevant support is computational feasibility: the method uses sparse compressed sparse column model matrices for random-effects terms and avoids repeatedly forming dense random-effects products by storing the random-effects matrix or precomputing weighted cross-products such as XWX, XWy, ZWX, ZWy, and ZWZ, with Cholesky-based updates and a nonlinear optimizer for covariance parameters; no hardware specifications are reported, but this sparse strategy is adequate for the small-to-moderate sample sizes and repeated caliper measurements described in the research plan. The weakest support is general methodological credibility, reflected by the high citation count of 91285, which suggests broad adoption of the LMM fitting approach but does not itself validate the ecological model for bud set timing. Because only this related work is provided, it should be treated as a foundational methodological anchor rather than as an iterative series of studies; no newer work is available to confirm or challenge its applicability to the beech phenology experiments.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953) provides no empirical validation for the specific ecological response: it reports no quantitative bud set timing results, effect sizes, confidence intervals, p-values, sample sizes, or significance tests for treatment effects, and it does not describe climate chamber temperatures, tree numbers, or phenological measurement protocols. Therefore, it does not demonstrate that a Gaussian linear mixed-effects model is appropriate for bud set dates derived from repeated caliper measurements and linear interpolation to 90 percent maximum bud length, especially if some buds are censored because they do not reach bud set by November 2 or November 3. A second contradiction concerns the proposed random-effects specification: the plan says bud type will be a random effect, but the research plan identifies only a small number of bud categories, such as terminal buds on primary shoots and lateral stems, and the related work supports random grouping factors generally without evidence that a random effect with very few levels is stable or preferable to a fixed effect; the more defensible structure would likely include tree ID as a random intercept, possibly bud nested within tree, and bud type as a fixed categorical factor if it has few levels. A third contradiction is that the proposed method does not specify the fixed-effect structure needed for the factorial experiments, including early-leafing versus late-leafing by July and August cooling in Experiment 1 and pre-solstice versus post-solstice by daytime, nighttime, full-day, and chamber control in Experiment 2, nor does it address multiple comparisons, contrast coding, or effect-size estimation relative to the appropriate controls. The weakest contradiction is that the related work is a software and algorithm description rather than an applied phenology study, so it provides no evidence about convergence behavior, optimizer settings, covariance-parameter constraints, residual diagnostics, or reproducibility details for this dataset, and no newer related work is provided to show whether later methods have corrected these limitations.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to specify the exact mixed model before analysis: use a linear mixed-effects model or an appropriate alternative with bud set timing as the response, fixed effects for treatment, treatment interactions, blocks, and relevant covariates such as initial tree size, and random intercepts for tree ID and, if justified, bud nested within tree, while treating bud type as a fixed effect if it has only a few levels; fit by REML or maximum likelihood using an implementation consistent with [(Bates et al., 2014-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b536c7b0f45d4b512f337d0acda09ca3e4cd953), and report convergence diagnostics, variance components, and residual checks. The next most important action is to address the temporal and possibly censored nature of bud set: if bud set is not observed for all buds by the final measurement date, use a mixed-effects survival or time-to-event model, a censored-data model, or a sensitivity analysis comparing Gaussian LMM results with alternative distributions or transformations, and explicitly state how linear interpolation to 90 percent maximum bud length is handled. A third action is to define effect sizes relative to controls using estimated marginal means or contrasts for each treatment versus its appropriate control, with confidence intervals, p-values, and multiple-testing correction, and to report mean differences in days and standardized effects. A fourth action is to document implementation details for reproducibility, including software package and version, sparse matrix format, optimizer and tolerance settings, covariance-parameter constraints, random seed, and computational resources, leveraging the sparse compressed sparse column strategy and precomputed cross-products described in the related work. The least important but still useful action is to perform model selection or sensitivity analyses, such as comparing random intercept versus random slope structures, checking for heteroscedasticity or autocorrelation in repeated caliper measurements, and reporting AICc or likelihood-ratio comparisons where appropriate.

## Experiment 2 phenological monitoring: We will measure buds weekly from August 25 to November 3 using the same methodology as Experiment 1.

### Support

The strongest support for Experiment 2 phenological monitoring is that [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) used potted Fagus sylvatica saplings under climate-controlled glasshouses and repeated autumn phenology monitoring, showing that potted beech can be subjected to manipulative climate conditions and followed through autumn. That study monitored 128 three-year-old saplings in 35 L pots, with 96 glasshouse trees in +0 C and +3 C treatments and 32 outside reference trees, and assessed autumn senescence with chlorophyll content index and defoliation while recording air temperature, relative humidity, vapor pressure deficit, and soil water content. This supports the proposed use of 180 four-year-old potted beech trees and repeated phenological observation after pre- and post-solstice cooling treatments, although it did not measure terminal bud length or weekly bud set. As an iterative progression, the 2020 potted study extends the earlier field drought work of [(Gimbel et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ef187885374e337148d90d0ddf0e34c0e35a3a8) by moving from large field plots to controlled potted climate manipulation, making it more directly relevant to Experiment 2. A weaker but relevant support is that [(Gimbel et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ef187885374e337148d90d0ddf0e34c0e35a3a8) repeatedly monitored 1080 one-year-old Fagus sylvatica saplings across 90 roof and control subplots for growth and stomatal conductance under a field drought-exclusion manipulation, demonstrating that repeated beech sapling monitoring under environmental manipulation is feasible and that physiological responses can be detected even when growth responses are weak. The 2020 potted work is more directly relevant than the 2014 field work because it uses potted beech and climate manipulation, but both studies only provide indirect support for the proposed weekly caliper-based bud-length protocol and the 90 percent maximum-length bud-set definition.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) showed that manipulative potted beech climate treatment can produce severe stress, mortality, and phenological exclusions that may undermine a fixed weekly monitoring window. In that study, the +3 C treatment had significantly lower daily soil water content and relative humidity than +0 C, with P < 0.001 and estimated differences of about 0.025 m3/m3 in soil water content and 20 percent in relative humidity; the drought treatment had to be alleviated on 20 June, and 20 saplings were excluded for early abrupt defoliation before 18 August while 18 were excluded for persistently low chlorophyll content index below 3 from August to November. This contradicts the assumption that 180 trees, with only 18 per treatment and 36 ambient controls, will retain enough healthy, measurable individuals from August 25 to November 3, especially if chamber cooling, light, or vapor pressure deficit conditions cause stress. A second contradiction is that neither related study directly validates the proposed primary marker: [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) measured senescence and defoliation, not terminal bud length or bud set, and [(Gimbel et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ef187885374e337148d90d0ddf0e34c0e35a3a8) measured leaf number, height, leaf length, crown expansion, and stomatal conductance, but not weekly bud length. A third, weaker contradiction is that [(Gimbel et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ef187885374e337148d90d0ddf0e34c0e35a3a8) found no significant first-year drought effects on phytometer growth variables and only a clear significant stomatal conductance reduction in July with a marginal September reduction, suggesting that first-year beech responses to environmental manipulation can be subtle and may require high replication and sensitive metrics. Finally, both studies contain confounds that limit direct transfer to Experiment 2: the 2020 glasshouses reduced light by about 20 percent, modified UV transmission, lacked reference-plot soil water data, and were conducted during the exceptional 2018 drought, while the 2014 roofs intercepted 11 to 15.5 percent of precipitation, targeted relative reductions of about 26 to 33 percent, did not alter air temperature or humidity, and produced variable soil moisture deficits across sites.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to start bud monitoring earlier than August 25 or to add pre-window measurements, because [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) documented early abrupt defoliation before 18 August, indicating that early-autumn phenological transitions can occur before the proposed start date; if bud set begins before August 25, weekly measurements from that date cannot support interpolation to the 90 percent maximum-length bud-set date. A second priority is to increase effective replication and predefine exclusion criteria, because [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) lost 38 saplings to early defoliation or low chlorophyll content index and experienced mortality severe enough to require treatment alleviation, while [(Gimbel et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ef187885374e337148d90d0ddf0e34c0e35a3a8) used 1080 saplings across 90 subplots to detect subtle first-year responses; the proposed 18 trees per treatment and 36 ambient controls may be underpowered if cooling or chamber conditions cause mortality or measurement failure. A third improvement is to expand the statistical model beyond treatment fixed effects and bud type random effects by adding random intercepts for tree, chamber or block, and measurement occasion, reflecting the nested structures used in [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) with 8 saplings per glasshouse and [(Gimbel et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ef187885374e337148d90d0ddf0e34c0e35a3a8) with 12 saplings per subplot across 9 plots. A fourth improvement is to monitor and report soil water content, vapor pressure deficit, relative humidity, light intensity, and UV transmission in all treatments, including ambient controls, because [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) showed that light reduction, UV modification, and missing reference soil water can confound potted climate experiments, and [(Gimbel et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ef187885374e337148d90d0ddf0e34c0e35a3a8) showed that stomatal conductance can respond to water manipulation even when growth does not; this is especially important because the proposed 20 L pots are smaller than the 35 L pots used by [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922). Finally, add secondary phenology and stress markers such as leaf senescence, defoliation, chlorophyll content index, and mortality, because [(Marien et al., 2020-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0adc2794bfbe7c4a423efcde9763b4ade9e34922) demonstrates that autumn phenology in potted beech can be expressed through senescence and defoliation as well as bud development.

## Developmental timing measurement: We will monitor individual leaf-out dates (defined as >50% leaf unfolding).

### Support

The strongest support comes from [(Oddou-Muratorio et al., 2024-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/791731974389be6c97af9ee8f6331ac7c9797a4e), which measured individual-level spring phenology of European beech as time to budburst or leaf-out in both in situ adult trees and ex situ common-garden seedlings, and linked earlier budburst to higher female fecundity and greater seedling growth while also reporting stabilizing selection on time to budburst; this directly supports the proposed plan's use of individual leaf-out dates in potted Fagus sylvatica saplings to define early- versus late-leafing developmental groups and to connect early-season development to later bud-set responses. A second, more operational support comes from [(Jean et al., 2023-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/15f902d1484767fe2c19854700d35d4a6f33d3e5), which recorded 30,933 ground-based phenology observations from 2006 to 2019 on 772 trees, including 2,234 leafing observations (1,530 European beech on the north side, 73 European beech on the south side, and 631 silver fir), using mostly weekly observations, two observers, binoculars, consensus scoring, intercalibration before campaigns, checks against the previous week's score, and validation plots produced with R version 4.2.2; this supports the feasibility of monitoring individual leaf-out timing in a structured, repeatable way, although it was done in natural stands rather than climate-manipulated pots. A weaker but still relevant support is the same Jean et al. evidence that bud development and leafing dates varied by roughly 50 to 100 days across trees, which supports the research plan's need for randomized blocks, individual tree random effects, and sufficient replication when using leaf-out dates to create treatment groups, but it does not directly validate the proposed >50% leaf-unfolding criterion.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that neither related work validates the proposed operational definition of leaf-out as >50% leaf unfolding. [(Jean et al., 2023-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/15f902d1484767fe2c19854700d35d4a6f33d3e5) explicitly assigned leafing as a BBCH-based phenological stage to the whole-tree crown rather than a binary leaf-out date defined by a fixed threshold such as 50% leaf unfolding, and [(Oddou-Muratorio et al., 2024-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/791731974389be6c97af9ee8f6331ac7c9797a4e) does not state whether a >50% threshold, observation interval, or exact scoring protocol was used; therefore the proposed threshold is not evidenced as a standard, reproducible, or biologically calibrated marker for European beech. A second contradiction is that Jean et al. observed 50 to 100 days of variability in bud development and leafing dates across trees in natural stands, with 5,919 tree-year observations, many trees observed for fewer than 5 years, and no treatment effects or statistical significance tests reported because the study was observational; this suggests that a single >50% leaf-out date may be sensitive to observer timing, microclimate, and individual variation, and in the proposed potted-sapling design such variability could blur the early- versus late-leafing groups needed to test whether slower early-season development postpones the solstice-related reversal of temperature effects on autumn bud set. A weaker contradiction is that Oddou-Muratorio et al. reported stabilizing selection on time to budburst and contrasted drought-stress effects on growth selection, while the supplied excerpt lacks numerical effect sizes, selection gradients, sample sizes, and statistical significance values; this indicates that leaf-out timing is not simply a linear developmental clock but a trait shaped by fitness and environmental constraints, so the proposed method should not treat leaf-out date as a clean, treatment-independent proxy for developmental speed without accounting for individual variation, water status, and potential non-linear responses.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to validate and pre-register the >50% leaf-unfolding criterion before using it to assign early- versus late-leafing groups, because neither [(Oddou-Muratorio et al., 2024-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/791731974389be6c97af9ee8f6331ac7c9797a4e) nor [(Jean et al., 2023-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/15f902d1484767fe2c19854700d35d4a6f33d3e5) provides direct evidence for that threshold. The protocol should combine the proposed binary date with a BBCH-style categorical stage, digital crown imagery, and at least two trained observers using consensus scoring, previous-week plausibility checks, and pre-campaign intercalibration, mirroring the operational safeguards used by Jean et al. in their 772-tree, 30,933-observation phenology dataset. The second priority is to strengthen the experimental and statistical handling of leaf-out variability: because Jean et al. documented 50 to 100 days of natural leafing-date variability, the plan should report the distribution of leaf-out dates, define early/late groups with explicit cutoffs or use leaf-out date as a continuous covariate, and include tree, block, pot position, shoot length, and soil moisture as covariates or random effects in the linear mixed-effects models. The third priority is to address the biological complexity highlighted by Oddou-Muratorio et al., who linked budburst timing to fecundity, growth, and stabilizing selection under drought and altitudinal variation; the proposed analysis should therefore test whether leaf-out timing interacts with treatment and bud type, report effect sizes and sample sizes, and include growth or fitness-related measurements if feasible, so that leaf-out date is not treated as a simple linear developmental index. A final, lower-priority action is to increase observation frequency around the expected leaf-out window, since the related work used mostly weekly observations and the proposed potted experiment may require finer temporal resolution to reduce threshold misclassification.

## To test whether bacterial translation inhibitors act through identical mechanisms in mitochondria as in bacteria, we will employ mitoribosome profiling in HEK293 cells to characterize the impact of a diverse panel of bacterial translation inhibitors on mitochondrial translation.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed HEK293 mitoribosome profiling method is that [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6) demonstrated that mitoribosome profiling can resolve codon-specific stalling, queuing, and initiation events, which are exactly the readout classes the research plan uses to distinguish initiation inhibitors from context-dependent elongation inhibitors. Specifically, the related work used MitoIP-Ribo-Seq, MitoIP-Thor-Ribo-Seq, and MitoIP-Thor-Disome-Seq with lysate preparation, mitoribosome isolation, sucrose density gradient fractionation, library preparation, sequencing, and mapping of protected fragments to mitochondrial transcripts, supporting the plan's use of sucrose gradient fractionation and MRPF mapping to identify ribosome accumulation. A second strong support is that the related work detected codon-context effects such as t6A affecting Lys codon elongation, f5C34 facilitating AUA traversal, tau-m5U34 influencing UUG, UGG, and AAG codons, s2U supporting GAG translation and suppressing GAC misreading, and queuosine modulating traversal, which directly supports the plan's analysis of -1, P-site, and A-site amino acid contexts for telithromycin and other context-dependent inhibitors. A third support is that the related work identified widespread cryptic translation initiation sites and investigated mitoribosome collision/disome signals, supporting the plan's goals of annotating initiation sites, validating them by mass spectrometry, and assessing non-coding translation events. Weaker support is that the related work included standard Ribo-Seq and RNA-Seq alongside mitoribosome profiling, suggesting that the plan should include orthogonal transcriptomic and cytoplasmic ribosome profiling controls, although the excerpt does not provide quantitative outcomes, p-values, footprint-length distributions, or statistical comparisons that would directly validate the plan's statistical significance claims.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction to the proposed method is that the related work [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6) does not report antibiotic treatments, so it provides no direct evidence that bacterial translation inhibitors such as retapamulin, tiamulin, josamycin, erythromycin, chloramphenicol, linezolid, or telithromycin produce the same initiation or elongation arrest patterns in mitochondria as in bacteria. The next strongest contradiction is population mismatch: the related work used cell lines, MTU1 knockout cells, a MELAS-associated mitochondrial tRNA mutation background, and mouse cardiac muscle, but does not specify HEK293 cells, antibiotic concentrations, 30-minute treatment durations, or DMSO controls, so the plan's HEK293-specific conclusions cannot be assumed from this work. A further contradiction is that the related work does not specify RNase I versus MNase digestion parameters, footprint-length distributions, phasing thresholds, or statistical cutoffs, so the plan's nuclease optimization and statistical significance framework are not supported by the cited evidence. Another contradiction is that the related work detected widespread cryptic initiation sites, which complicates the plan's assumption that 5' MRPF sizes alone will cleanly correlate with start codon positions and may generate false-positive initiation calls if not constrained by phasing, amino acid composition, and mass spectrometry. The weakest contradiction is that the related work highlights mitoribosome collision/disome signals and MELAS-associated queuing, whereas the proposed method as written focuses on standard MRPF accumulation and does not explicitly include MitoIP-Thor-Disome-Seq or collision-aware analysis, potentially misinterpreting queuing as simple arrest.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to add an explicit antibiotic treatment arm with the planned concentrations and durations, plus DMSO controls and biological replicates, and to benchmark the expected MRPF signatures against the codon-specific stalling and queuing patterns reported by [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6), such as Lys, AUA, UUG, UGG, AAG, GAG, and GAC contexts, to confirm that the HEK293 assay can detect context-dependent arrest. The next most important action is to incorporate MitoIP-Thor-Disome-Seq or an equivalent collision/disome profiling step, because the related work shows that mitoribosome queuing and collision signals are biologically relevant and can confound standard occupancy-based interpretation of antibiotic-induced stalling. A third action is to validate the nuclease optimization by comparing RNase I and MNase using quantitative metrics, including read phasing, footprint length distribution, coverage uniformity across mitochondrial ORFs, and statistical thresholds for MRPF enrichment, rather than relying only on qualitative pattern recognition, since the related work does not provide digestion parameters or statistical thresholds. A fourth action is to include orthogonal controls such as standard Ribo-Seq and RNA-Seq, as done in the related work, to separate mitochondrial-specific effects from global transcriptomic or cytoplasmic ribosome changes. A fifth action is to strengthen initiation-site annotation by requiring concordant 5' MRPF size, codon phasing, amino acid composition, and mass spectrometry detection of N-terminal peptides, because the related work reports widespread cryptic initiation sites that could otherwise be mistaken for antibiotic-induced initiation. The least important but still useful action is to test whether findings generalize beyond HEK293 by including at least one additional cell line or genetic background, such as an MTU1-deficient or MELAS-associated background, given that the related work used such systems and mitochondrial translation defects can be cell-type and genetic-context dependent.

## To evaluate the technical quality of the optimized mitoribosome profiling, the quality will be assessed through read phasing analysis, footprint length distribution, and coverage patterns across mitochondrial open reading frames.

### Support

The strongest support is that [(Li et al., 2022-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/940dd61674973323a0b004866a30c27061b164c0) used low-input RNase footprinting with A-tailing and SMARTer template switching to quantify cytosolic and mitochondrial translation from 1,000 to 50,000 cultured cells and explicitly evaluated mitochondrial ORF footprints by phasing, footprint length, and coverage, which is directly analogous to the proposed quality assessment of mitoribosome profiling in HEK293 cells. This demonstrates that the three proposed metrics are biologically informative and can resolve mitochondrial translation at base resolution, including TUFM-dependent changes and noncanonical ORFs in primary immune cells, supporting their use to distinguish genuine mitoribosome-protected fragments from background. A secondary support is that the method worked across low-input cultured cells, snap-frozen tissues, and primary immune cells, suggesting that the proposed HEK293 experiment can be feasible if cell number and sequencing depth are sufficient, although the paper does not state the exact cell number used for each condition. A weaker but relevant support is that simultaneous cytosolic and mitochondrial mapping provides a built-in comparison for coverage patterns, which can help the proposed plan detect whether mitochondrial ORF coverage changes are specific to mitoribosomes rather than global RNA or library-preparation artifacts.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Li et al., 2022-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/940dd61674973323a0b004866a30c27061b164c0) does not provide numerical phasing scores, footprint length distributions, coverage values, or statistical significance tests, so it does not establish that the proposed metrics alone are sufficient to certify optimized mitoribosome profiling without predefined quantitative thresholds and controls. A second contradiction is that the paper used RNase footprinting but does not compare RNase I with MNase, nor report RNase concentration, digestion time, sequencing platform, read length, mapping parameters, or statistical thresholds, leaving the proposed nuclease optimization and quality criteria under-specified relative to the evidence. A third limitation is that the excerpt does not describe HEK293 cells, antibiotic treatment, sucrose gradient fractionation, or mitoribosome-specific isolation, so the support for the proposed experimental context is indirect. A weaker contradiction is that low-input footprinting can be sensitive to library construction and background, and because the related work did not report replicate-level variance or significance, the proposed plan should not assume that phasing, length, and coverage patterns are automatically reproducible or statistically robust.

### Suggested Action

Because [(Li et al., 2022-02)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/940dd61674973323a0b004866a30c27061b164c0) demonstrates the utility of phasing, footprint length, and coverage but does not report quantitative thresholds, the most important improvement is to convert the proposed quality assessment into explicit quantitative QC gates: report a 3-nucleotide phasing score or phasing ratio for mitochondrial ORFs, footprint length mean, median, and width, and coverage uniformity metrics such as per-codon coverage, 5-prime to 3-prime coverage ratio, start and stop coverage, and RPKM or TPM across mitochondrial ORFs, with thresholds derived from DMSO controls and technical replicates. Next, titrate RNase I and MNase digestion time and concentration, include no-ribosome, nuclease-only, RNase inhibitor, and DMSO controls, and select the nuclease that maximizes phasing and expected mitoribosome footprint length while minimizing non-specific coverage. Then specify the input cell number, sequencing depth, read length, library construction, mapping parameters, and statistical tests such as permutation, bootstrap, or FDR correction for comparing antibiotic treatments to controls. Finally, use the cytosolic ribosome-profiling signal from the same libraries as an internal control for coverage artifacts and validate candidate initiation or stalling sites with mass spectrometry, consistent with the plan's complementary proteomics.

## To obtain mitochondrial ribosome footprints from treated cells, we will isolate mitoribosomes through sucrose gradient fractionation, where cytoplasmic ribosomes and mitoribosomes sediment in distinct 80S and 55S fractions respectively.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed sucrose-gradient isolation of 55S mitoribosomes from 80S cytoplasmic ribosomes is that [(Krüger et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c06e68969775fca74af6f315c7b943bd81d38931) directly used this fractionation logic in a mammalian mitoribosome-profiling workflow: mitochondria were isolated, lysed, loaded onto a sucrose gradient, ultracentrifuged, and fractions were resolved by absorbance, with mitoribosomes recovered from the 55S peak separate from the 80S cytoplasmic ribosome peak. This is the same physical separation assumed in the proposed HEK293 antibiotic experiment, where MRPFs must be obtained from the 55S fraction before sequencing and mapping to the mitochondrial genome. A second, slightly weaker but still relevant line of support is that the same 55S-derived material yielded interpretable mitoribosome footprints after nuclease digestion, reverse transcription, sequencing, and mapping, including stalling at AGA and AGG stop codons in mtRF1-deficient cells and rescue by wild-type but not GGQ-mutant mtRF1; this demonstrates that the 55S fraction can contain translating or stalled mitoribosomes whose protected fragments are detectable and biologically informative. The weakest but still useful support is that the study observed gene-specific changes in COX1 and ND6 transcript and translation outputs and OXPHOS protein or complex activities, indicating that mitoribosome profiling from sucrose-gradient fractions can resolve transcript-specific translation effects, which is relevant to the plan’s goal of mapping antibiotic-induced arrest across mitochondrial open reading frames and annotating novel translation events.

### Contradictions

No related work directly contradicts the proposed assumption that cytoplasmic 80S ribosomes and mitochondrial 55S ribosomes can be separated by sucrose gradient fractionation. The strongest counterevidence is that [(Krüger et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c06e68969775fca74af6f315c7b943bd81d38931) does not provide quantitative validation of the separation under the proposed antibiotic conditions: the excerpt lacks fold changes, p-values, footprint counts, gradient resolution metrics, rotor speed, sucrose composition, nuclease identity, and sequencing depth, so it cannot confirm that the 55S peak will remain cleanly resolved after 30-minute treatment of HEK293 cells with 100 µg/mL tiamulin or josamycin or 10 µg/mL retapamulin, or that 80S contamination will not confound MRPF mapping. A weaker limitation is that the related perturbation was genetic loss of mtRF1 rather than the proposed panel of pleuromutilin, macrolide, context-dependent elongation, or telithromycin inhibitors, so it supports feasibility of mitoribosome isolation and profiling but does not test whether antibiotic-induced stalling, mitochondrial stress, or altered ribosome occupancy changes sedimentation behavior or peak identity. The weakest caveat is that the supplied context specifies mtRF1 knockout and rescue cell lines and bovine liver mitochondria for in vitro assays, without confirming that the exact HEK293 population, mitochondrial isolation and lysis conditions, or antibiotic exposure used in the proposed plan were tested, which limits direct transferability of the gradient protocol.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to adopt the [(Krüger et al., 2023-01)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c06e68969775fca74af6f315c7b943bd81d38931) mitochondrial isolation-then-sucrose-gradient workflow explicitly, rather than applying gradient fractionation directly to whole treated cells, and to document all reproducibility-critical parameters: mitochondrial isolation method, lysis buffer, sucrose gradient composition, rotor, speed, time, temperature, absorbance trace, and the exact 55S fractions pooled. This should be paired with quantitative QC for each antibiotic and DMSO condition, including cytoplasmic 80S contamination estimates from cytoplasmic rRNA or non-mitochondrial reads, mitoribosomal protein markers, mitochondrial and cytoplasmic marker separation, and replicate gradients. The next priority is to pilot the gradient under the proposed antibiotic exposures, 100 µg/mL tiamulin or josamycin and 10 µg/mL retapamulin for 30 minutes, to confirm that the 55S peak remains distinct from 80S and that MRPF length, phasing, and coverage across mitochondrial ORFs remain interpretable after nuclease digestion, especially when comparing RNase I and MNase. A further action is to add statistical and mapping controls: DMSO-treated controls, no-nuclease or RNase-only controls, mitochondrial genome mapping with expected footprint periodicity, and significance testing for enrichment at predicted stall motifs or transcript ends. Finally, because the related work did not quantify antibiotic effects, the plan should include mass spectrometry or western blot validation of mitoribosome recovery and mitochondrial translation output for each antibiotic, so that changes in MRPF abundance are not misinterpreted as changes in ribosome isolation efficiency.

## To determine the sequence contexts that promote ribosome stalling for context-dependent inhibitors, we will calculate the average change in MRPF abundance based on the identity of amino acids at the -1 (penultimate), P-site, and A-site positions.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed method comes from the fact that the prior study directly used ribosome-protected fragment abundance relative to mRNA to infer codon- or amino-acid-specific ribosome occupancy, specifically by calculating ribosome residence time for all 61 sense codons [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608). This is methodologically analogous to the proposed calculation of average change in MRPF abundance based on amino acid identity at the -1, P-site, and A-site positions, because both approaches rely on protected-fragment abundance at codon resolution to infer where ribosomes occupy or stall. A second, weaker form of support is that the prior work used motif investigation and GO enrichment to interpret enriched sequence contexts [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608), which supports the broader goal of identifying sequence contexts associated with altered ribosome occupancy, although it did not test antibiotic-induced stalling. A third, weaker support is that the prior study identified 5' extensions and 3' read-through events to assess translation boundaries [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608), which is relevant to the research plan's analysis of initiation sites and potential novel translation events, but it does not directly support the -1/P/A stalling metric. The weakest support is that the prior work observed strain-specific differences in genes involved in mitochondrial translation [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608), indicating that mitochondrial translation can be perturbed and measured, but this does not establish that bacterial inhibitor-specific contexts will be detectable in HEK293 mitoribosomes.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the prior study did not measure antibiotic-induced -1/P-site/A-site context-dependent stalling and did not report numerical RRT values, fold changes, p-values, or specific stalling motifs [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608). Therefore, it provides no direct evidence that averaging MRPF abundance changes across -1, P, and A amino acid identities will identify inhibitor-specific stalling contexts. The next strongest contradiction is that the experimental system was yeast strains with mitochondrial replacement rather than HEK293 cells, and it was not mitoribosome-specific [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608). This limits direct transferability because the ribosomal machinery, codon usage, mRNA abundance, and antibiotic sensitivity in yeast are not identical to those in human mitochondria. A further contradiction is that the prior method inferred steady-state codon-specific ribosome occupancy from RFP abundance relative to mRNA, whereas the proposed method requires acute drug-induced MRPF accumulation [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608). The prior work did not include inhibitor treatments, DMSO controls, or time-course measurements, so it cannot demonstrate that the proposed average-change metric will distinguish true stalling from global translation changes or mRNA abundance shifts. The weakest contradiction is that the excerpt lacks implementation details such as growth conditions, sequencing depth, statistical thresholds, and software versions [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608), which limits reproducibility and the ability to transfer the RRT calculation to the proposed mitoribosome profiling pipeline.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to add a quantitative statistical framework for the -1/P/A context calculation: normalize MRPF abundance per codon or per amino acid, compute log2 fold change relative to DMSO controls, use biological and technical replicates, and apply permutation or FDR-based significance testing to determine whether enrichment at specific -1/P/A amino acid triads exceeds background expected from codon usage, amino acid composition, and transcript length. The second most important action is to include positive and negative controls for context dependence: use telithromycin's known R/K-X-R/K bacterial motif as a positive control if it arrests mitoribosomes, include non-context-dependent pleuromutilin or macrolide treatments as negative controls, and compare the -1/P/A window to alternative windows such as P/A only or -1/P only to ensure the triad is not an artifact of phasing or local codon bias. The third most important action is to adapt the prior RRT-like analysis [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608) by calculating MRPF-to-mRNA or MRPF-to-total-ribosome-protected-fragment ratios for all 61 sense codons in the mitochondrial genome, reporting numerical fold changes and confidence intervals, and using phasing, footprint length, and coverage across mitochondrial ORFs to confirm codon alignment before interpreting amino acid contexts. The fourth action is to integrate the related work's motif and boundary analyses [(Sun et al., 2016-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/16d6195ee1a1362145673295cba88363cdf3b608) by performing motif enrichment on stalled codon contexts and examining 5' extensions, 3' read-through, and MRPF size at transcript starts, with mass spectrometry validation of N-terminal peptides for alternative initiation sites. The least important but still useful action is to document all implementation details, including HEK293 cell line, antibiotic concentrations, 30-minute treatment, RNase I versus MNase digestion, sequencing depth, mapping software, and statistical thresholds, to make the proposed method reproducible and comparable to the prior codon-resolution RFP/mRNA approach.

## To investigate translation initiation sites in mitochondrial transcripts, we will analyze MRPF sizes at the 5' ends of mitochondrial transcripts, using expected ribosome footprint lengths to correlate with start codon positions.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed 5'-end MRPF size analysis is that [(Rooijers et al., 2013-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1aa6b70f5b0b48f475cc7f253858b4e37aaf4e2f) explicitly analyzed mitochondrial RPF size distributions and periodicity/phasing, showing that MRPF length and phasing are measurable features relevant to the plan's use of expected footprint lengths to infer initiation sites in HEK293 mitoribosome profiling. The next strongest support is [(Gao et al., 2017-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d035d1fca0463a49a51648ae88e633ab70f0e701), which used mitoribosome-protected fragments, codon coverage, and plots of footprints covering the 5' termini of mitochondrial transcripts, directly paralleling the proposed method's strategy of correlating 5' footprint boundaries with start codon positions. The weakest but still relevant support is [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6), which used MitoIP-Ribo-Seq to map 5'-proximal footprints and cryptic initiation sites, and included MTU1 knockout validation, mitochondrial lysate preparation, mitoribosome isolation, nuclease digestion, library construction, and mapping, supporting the broader mitoribosome profiling workflow and the possibility that 5'-proximal MRPFs can reveal initiation events. All three excerpts, however, lack quantitative MRPF length values, read proportions, statistical tests, nuclease specifications, sequencing depth, and start-codon correlation metrics, so the support is methodological rather than quantitative.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Rooijers et al., 2013-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1aa6b70f5b0b48f475cc7f253858b4e37aaf4e2f) reported mitochondrial RPFs are poorly captured by current protocols and that mitochondrial RPF length is bimodal. This directly challenges the assumption that MRPF sizes at the 5' ends of mitochondrial transcripts will form a clean, expected footprint-length class that can be straightforwardly mapped to start codons in the proposed HEK293 antibiotic experiments; if the dominant protected fragments are heterogeneous or protocol-dependent, start-codon inference may require nuclease optimization, size cutoffs, and phasing filters before interpretation. A second, weaker contradiction is that [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6) found cryptic translation initiation sites widespread in the mitochondrial transcriptome and emphasized tRNA modification effects, which complicates the plan's simpler expectation that 5'-proximal MRPFs primarily mark canonical start codons; 5' footprints may reflect noncanonical or cryptic initiation, requiring additional validation by mass spectrometry N-terminal peptides and phasing/amino-acid composition. The weakest contradiction is that [(Gao et al., 2017-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d035d1fca0463a49a51648ae88e633ab70f0e701) does not provide quantitative codon-coverage values, MRPF size distributions, statistical thresholds, or control comparisons, so it does not establish that 5' footprint plots and codon coverage can robustly distinguish initiation from elongation-related accumulation under the antibiotic treatments planned here.

### Suggested Action

Most important, the plan should first quantify and optimize MRPF size and phasing before using 5' footprint lengths to infer start codons: compare RNase I and MNase digestion in HEK293 cells, report the full MRPF length distribution, periodicity, and 5' footprint size classes for DMSO and each antibiotic, and define empirical cutoffs that separate expected mitoribosome footprints from the bimodal/poorly captured signal described by [(Rooijers et al., 2013-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1aa6b70f5b0b48f475cc7f253858b4e37aaf4e2f). Next, adopt the 5' footprint and codon-coverage framework of [(Gao et al., 2017-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d035d1fca0463a49a51648ae88e633ab70f0e701) by aligning 5' MRPF ends to candidate start codons, requiring in-frame codon coverage and phasing, and comparing antibiotic versus DMSO with statistical significance and replicate-level variance. Finally, incorporate controls and validation informed by [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6): verify mitoribosome fraction purity and mitochondrial specificity, consider MTU1 knockout or mitoribosome-depletion controls if feasible, and use mass spectrometry N-terminal peptides plus phasing/amino-acid composition to distinguish canonical start codons from cryptic or noncanonical initiation events.

## To validate alternative translation initiation sites suggested by mitoribosome profiling, we will complement our mitoribosome profiling data with protein mass spectrometry analysis of isolated mitochondria by detecting N-terminal peptides corresponding to different potential start codons.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed mass-spectrometric validation comes from [(Lualdi et al., 2019-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7077854de1c7ed7bdf34252f68d87dffe775b967), which used a mitochondrial dimethylation-TAILS workflow on isolated mitochondria, including protein digestion, N-terminal dimethylation, N-terminal peptide enrichment, LC-MS/MS, and mapping of N-terminal peptides to mitochondrial proteins. This directly demonstrates that N-terminal peptides from isolated mitochondria can be detected and assigned to mitochondrial proteins, making the proposed detection of N-terminal peptides corresponding to candidate alternative start codons in HEK293 mitochondria methodologically plausible. The next strongest support is from the newer mitoribosome-profiling study [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6), which used MitoIP-Ribo-Seq in cell lines and mouse cardiac muscle, including MTU1 knockout cells, to identify widespread cryptic translation initiation sites at the 5-prime ends of mitochondrial transcripts. This supports the premise that mitoribosome profiling can generate candidate alternative initiation sites that require downstream protein-level validation, but it does not itself provide mass-spectrometric evidence for the proposed N-terminal peptide validation.

### Contradictions

The strongest caution is also from [(Lualdi et al., 2019-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7077854de1c7ed7bdf34252f68d87dffe775b967), because the same mitochondrial N-terminal proteomics context showed that altered dopamine homeostasis changed the mitochondrial N-terminome and that neprilysin was identified and validated as a protease aberrantly activated in that setting. This implies that N-terminal peptides in isolated mitochondria can be generated or remodeled post-translationally by proteases, so detection of an N-terminal peptide matching a predicted alternative start codon would not by itself prove that the peptide arose from translation initiation at that codon. A weaker but important limitation is that [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6) reports cryptic initiation sites from mitoribosome profiling but the supplied excerpt contains no mass-spectrometric validation, peptide counts, read counts, fold changes, p-values, sequencing depth, or statistical thresholds. Therefore, the newer profiling evidence supports candidate generation but does not resolve whether those ribosome-occupied cryptic sites produce stable N-terminal peptides detectable by MS, nor does it provide quantitative benchmarks for the proposed validation.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to explicitly adopt an N-terminal enrichment strategy analogous to the dimethylation-TAILS workflow used by [(Lualdi et al., 2019-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7077854de1c7ed7bdf34252f68d87dffe775b967), specifying mitochondrial isolation from HEK293 cells, protein digestion, N-terminal dimethylation, enrichment, LC-MS/MS acquisition, and database search against a mitochondrial proteome that includes candidate alternative N-terminal variants. The second priority is to control for protease-generated N-termini, which is the main confound highlighted by [(Lualdi et al., 2019-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7077854de1c7ed7bdf34252f68d87dffe775b967), by adding protease inhibitors, comparing canonical and alternative N-terminal peptides, and requiring that the detected peptide exactly matches the predicted start-codon-derived sequence rather than an upstream or processed fragment. The third priority is to integrate the MS validation with the mitoribosome-profiling candidate set from [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6) by prioritizing sites with consistent 5-prime MRPF footprints, correct phasing, and coverage across DMSO and antibiotic conditions, then using biological replicates, FDR-controlled peptide identification, and targeted PRM or MRM validation for the highest-confidence alternative N-termini.

## To ensure reproducibility and statistical significance of the findings, all experiments will include appropriate controls and replicates.

### Support

As a newer methodological extension of mitoribosome profiling, the strongest support is that [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6) used a MitoIP-based mitoribosome profiling workflow, specifically MitoIP-Ribo-Seq, together with generation and validation of MTU1 knockout cells as a genetic perturbation/control, which directly parallels the proposed plan's requirement for controls and replicates when interpreting mitoribosome occupancy in HEK293 cells after antibiotic treatment. By using MTU1 KO to perturb t6A and examine mitoribosome behavior, the study demonstrates that a defined genetic control can anchor interpretation of ribosome profiling signals, supporting the proposed DMSO-treated controls and the broader need for condition-specific controls in the antibiotic panel. A weaker but still relevant support is that the same study included related MitoIP-Thor-Ribo-Seq and MitoIP-Thor-Disome-Seq library preparations, indicating that mitoribosome profiling can be extended to collision/disome analyses; this supports the plan's goal of distinguishing initiation from elongation and context-dependent stalling, although the provided excerpt does not quantify how these modules improve reproducibility or statistical power. The weakest support is the qualitative finding that t6A modification primarily affects elongation at Lys codons, which is mechanistically adjacent to the plan's context-dependent amino acid analysis at the -1, P, and A sites, but it does not provide numeric read counts, fold changes, p-values, replicate statistics, sequencing depth, or statistical thresholds, so it cannot be used to validate the proposed claim that controls and replicates will ensure statistical significance.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the related work does not report the quantitative evidence needed to support the proposed method's reproducibility and statistical-significance claim: no read counts, fold changes, p-values, FDR, replicate-level variance, biological or technical replicate numbers, sequencing depth, or statistical decision thresholds are provided. Therefore, the statement that all experiments will include appropriate controls and replicates is not yet operationalized in the research plan, and the related work cannot confirm that such controls will be sufficient. A second contradiction is that the control used in [(Wakigawa et al., 2024-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f1a70d1dd46868ac8229668227f0e49c6d52fa6) is an MTU1 knockout affecting t6A modification, not a DMSO control, antibiotic exposure control, or control for the specific pleuromutilin, macrolide, context-dependent elongation, or telithromycin arrest mechanisms proposed for HEK293 cells; thus it does not directly validate the proposed antibiotic-specific controls. A third contradiction is that the related workflow is MitoIP-based, whereas the proposed plan relies on sucrose gradient fractionation separating 80S cytoplasmic and 55S mitochondrial ribosomes; the related work provides no evidence that sucrose-gradient isolation, RNase I versus MNase digestion, or the proposed read-phasing, footprint-length, and coverage metrics will yield comparable reproducibility. Finally, the related work does not specify the cell line, MTU1 KO validation assays, nuclease digestion conditions, computational resources, hardware, dataset size, or batch controls, so it cannot be generalized to the HEK293 antibiotic-profiling setup.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to convert the generic controls-and-replicates statement into a concrete statistical design: specify at least three biological replicates and at least two technical replicates for each antibiotic, DMSO, and untreated condition; define randomization, blinding, and batch balancing; and pre-register metrics such as MRPF read counts, phasing score, footprint length distribution, coverage across mitochondrial ORFs, and fold change relative to DMSO with dispersion-based differential abundance testing, FDR correction, and a power analysis. The next priority is to add condition-specific controls borrowed from the related work: include an MTU1 knockout or t6A perturbation control if Lys-codon or t6A-dependent stalling is expected, and consider MitoIP-Thor-Ribo-Seq or MitoIP-Thor-Disome-Seq as orthogonal assays to distinguish true stalling from disome or collision artifacts. A further improvement is to validate the proposed sucrose-gradient and nuclease choices against the MitoIP-based workflow by reporting sequencing depth, library complexity, read mapping rates, phasing, footprint length, and coverage reproducibility across replicates, rather than assuming that controls alone ensure significance. Finally, the plan should report implementation details that affect reproducibility, including HEK293 passage number, antibiotic lot and concentration, DMSO vehicle concentration, 30-minute treatment timing, gradient fractions, nuclease concentrations, sequencing platform and depth, mass-spectrometry replicate-level peptide identification and N-terminal peptide validation, and statistical thresholds, so that the controls and replicates can be audited against the quantitative standards missing from the related work.

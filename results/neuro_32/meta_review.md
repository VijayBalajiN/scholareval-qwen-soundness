## Context: To map how surface chemistry shifts frictional instability boundaries, Method: We will conduct mechanical testing using our PDMS mock finger across six different surface coatings and 16 combinations of finger sliding velocity (5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s) and applied mass (0, 25, 75, and 100 g plus 6 g finger weight).

### Support

The strongest support is that [(Caron, 2023-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6613171e0a6a2bbbd3e3635321380c7bf47437fc) directly demonstrates that Sylgard 184 PDMS friction is governed by adhesion, elastic junction elongation, stick-slip, and velocity-dependent relaxation, which is the physical basis for the proposed instability phases. In that AFM study, a SiOx-coated 27 nm tip on a 1 nm Rq PDMS surface showed a transition from a linear friction-normal-force regime at -50 to -10 nN to a JKR F_n^(2/3) regime at higher compressive loads, with fitted adhesion around 5 nN and shear strength around 10 MPa; this supports the plan to map boundaries in pressure/velocity space using a 100 kPa PDMS mock finger and gram-scale loads. The next strongest support is the observed velocity dependence: the linear-regime friction slope decreased logarithmically from 20 to 160 µm/s, and wear-induced friction increase also decayed logarithmically with velocity, supporting the use of multiple sliding velocities, although the proposed 5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s are two to three orders of magnitude higher. The study also supports the proposed trace-analysis approach because stick-slip events were extracted from lateral force line scans by identifying increasing lateral force sequences longer than five points and fitting them to obtain lateral contact stiffness and sticking strength, a procedure that can be adapted to 550 Hz normal/tangential traces to quantify steady sliding, slow frictional waves, and stiction spikes. Finally, the controlled PDMS preparation (Sylgard 184 mixed 10:1, cured 15 min at 90 °C, ambient 293 K and 50% RH) and SEM/topography checks before and after testing support the plan to use minimal surfaces with roughness below human detection and to monitor surface degradation, but this support is weaker because it concerns a single nanoscale PDMS surface rather than the six proposed silane coatings. As a prior nanoscale baseline, it supports the proposed method as the next iterative scale-up, but it does not by itself establish that scale-up.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that the related work does not test the proposed macroscopic mock-finger method: it used a single 27 nm SiOx AFM tip over 10 by 10 µm areas at -50 to 25 nN, not a PDMS finger with 6 to 106 g total load, six surface coatings, or human-relevant 5 to 45 mm/s sliding. Therefore it cannot validate that nanoscale adhesive stick-slip boundaries translate into the proposed macroscopic phases of steady sliding, slow frictional waves, and stiction spikes. The next strongest contradiction is the velocity and load scale mismatch: AFM velocities of 20 to 160 µm/s are 150 to 2250 times lower than 5 to 45 mm/s, and nN loads are many orders of magnitude below gram-scale loads, so the observed logarithmic velocity dependence and JKR transition may not hold under longer contact times, higher real contact areas, viscoelastic relaxation, thermal effects, or multiasperity contact. A further contradiction is that only one PDMS surface chemistry was tested, so the study provides no evidence that silane surface chemistry shifts instability boundaries, which is the central claim of the research plan. Additional weaker contradictions include the observation of wear and increased friction above about -15 nN, which suggests that nine repeated traces per mass-velocity combination could alter the mock finger or coating and confound phase classification; the use of ambient 50% RH without humidity variation means the method should control or report humidity because adhesion and friction can be humidity-sensitive; and the AFM analysis relied on linear and JKR fits plus qualitative stick-slip extraction rather than the GLMM-based participant accuracy/response-time framework proposed for human testing, nor does it address the 10-participant, 600-trial forced-choice design.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to add a scale-bridging validation before finalizing the mock-finger phase maps: use the same PDMS and at least two of the six silane coatings in an intermediate micro/nano tribometer or AFM to measure adhesion force, shear strength, friction versus load and velocity, and stick-slip frequency, then compare those parameters with the macroscopic mock-finger traces at 5 to 45 mm/s and 6 to 106 g loads to confirm that the instability boundaries shift in the predicted direction. The next most important action is to broaden the preliminary velocity and load range, including lower velocities such as 0.5, 1, and 2 mm/s and possibly higher velocities up to 100 mm/s, so that the final 5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s grid is chosen after observing where steady sliding, slow frictional waves, and stiction spikes appear, rather than assuming the AFM logarithmic velocity dependence persists at human scale. Wear control should be built in: perform SEM or optical profilometry of the mock finger and coatings before and after testing, randomize trace order, limit or replace surfaces after topography changes, and include wear as a covariate in the GLMM because the AFM study [(Caron, 2023-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6613171e0a6a2bbbd3e3635321380c7bf47437fc) showed friction increased after wear above about -15 nN. The trace-analysis pipeline should be made explicit and validated: at 550 Hz, detect stiction spikes and slow waves using thresholded force sequences, fit contact stiffness and sticking strength analogous to the AFM lateral-force method, compute instability frequencies, and use GLMM with participant and surface random effects plus bootstrap confidence intervals to compare accuracy and response time against average friction coefficient, roughness, and contact angle hysteresis. Finally, control and report environmental and material variables, including temperature, relative humidity, PDMS cure, silane deposition parameters, and surface roughness, because the AFM study [(Caron, 2023-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6613171e0a6a2bbbd3e3635321380c7bf47437fc) was performed at 293 K and 50% RH on a 1 nm Rq Sylgard 184 surface and those conditions may affect adhesion-driven instability boundaries.

## Context: To test whether instability-based predictions outperform conventional surface descriptors, Method: We will compare these correlations against traditional metrics including average friction coefficient, surface roughness, and contact angle hysteresis.

### Support

The strongest support for the proposed comparison comes from [(Zhang et al., 2017-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c2abc15c408b0b8ef82c77555f23af75aa64115e), which is the most directly relevant because it combines blindfolded human perception trials with in vivo fingertip friction on five stainless steel surfaces and shows substantial participant-to-participant variability in friction force and coefficient of friction on the same surface. Its protocol used a 30 mm stroke, 100 Hz force/torque recording, average normal load of 0.25 ± 0.12 N, sliding velocities from 25 to 124 mm/s, skin hydration from 62 to 120 AU, and 3D confocal Sa measurements over 1 mm by 1 mm areas with 63 nm height resolution, which mirrors the research plan concern that human velocity and pressure make raw friction forces less stable than instability-based descriptors. As an iterative step after the earlier conventional friction modelling of [(Tomlinson et al., 2011-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c8bfb3872e88c45c1acc1528be4c1d2df59315d9), Zhang et al. show that the same surface can produce different friction outcomes across people, supporting the plan's motivation to test whether instability frequencies are more discriminative than average friction coefficient or roughness. A second, weaker line of support is [(Tomlinson et al., 2011-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c8bfb3872e88c45c1acc1528be4c1d2df59315d9), whose 75 friction-force measurements across brass, steel, and aluminium ridges, modelled with triangular ridges, a fixed loss fraction of 0.45, and adhesion, interlocking, and hysteresis terms, produced only moderate predicted-versus-measured fits, with R-squared values of 0.78, 0.76, and 0.54 and overprediction attributed to blunt ridge tips and inconsistent steel profiles; this indicates that conventional coefficient-of-friction and geometry descriptors are sensitive to material, ridge shape, and measurement assumptions, and therefore should be treated as baselines rather than sufficient predictors. The weakest but still relevant support is [(Sergachev et al., 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/322f92233f72b50913c369245230611ac0f61cfd), which used reciprocating silicone asperity samples at 2 mm/s over a 20 mm stroke with a 6-axis transducer at 100 Hz and shows that in vivo average dynamic friction coefficients are strongly load-dependent, with non-textured reference surfaces decreasing from about 6.5 at low normal load to about 2.5 at high normal load, and that finger recovery was slow enough to require sequential loading; this supports the plan's phase-map logic that friction state depends on pressure, velocity, adhesion, and elasticity rather than on a single average coefficient. None of these studies measured contact angle hysteresis or classified frictional instabilities, so their support is indirect but consistent with the proposed method's need to compare instability-based predictions against conventional descriptors.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction to the claim that instability-based predictions will outperform conventional descriptors comes from [(Zhang et al., 2017-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c2abc15c408b0b8ef82c77555f23af75aa64115e), because it reports that higher 3D surface roughness Sa, measured with a 3D confocal microscope over 1 mm by 1 mm areas, was associated with lower tactile friction with a coefficient of determination of 0.8313, and that the average coefficient of friction was highest for the crater texture labeled 1HV90. As newer work, this partially contradicts the more deterministic geometry-friction modelling in [(Tomlinson et al., 2011-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c8bfb3872e88c45c1acc1528be4c1d2df59315d9) by showing that the same surface can yield variable friction across participants and that roughness alone can explain much of the friction variation. This suggests that conventional roughness and average friction metrics can already capture meaningful surface-related differences, which would weaken the proposed method if instability frequencies do not explain additional variance in human accuracy or response time. The contradiction is tempered by the small surface set of five stainless steel tiles, the absence of quantitative perception-rating outcomes in the provided excerpt, and the fact that the study did not measure instability phases or contact angle hysteresis. The next strongest contradiction is from [(Tomlinson et al., 2011-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c8bfb3872e88c45c1acc1528be4c1d2df59315d9), whose best conventional model fit for brass, using triangular ridge assumptions and a fixed loss fraction of 0.45, had a slope of 0.96 and R-squared of 0.78, and whose surface-design analysis found that coefficients of friction changed little until ridge width reached 0.28 mm and height reached 108 microns, then increased substantially for the largest 0.3 mm width and 250 micron height texture. This indicates that geometry-based friction coefficients can be predictive of friction force and texture effects, which is a potential baseline that instability-based predictions must exceed. The weakest contradiction is from [(Sergachev et al., 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/322f92233f72b50913c369245230611ac0f61cfd), which tested silicone ellipsoidal asperity samples with 200 um lateral spacing and 30 or 50 um heights and found statistically significant asperity-height effects with paired t-test p-values of 1.2e-5 for E100 and 8.6e-6 for E150, and that a reference surface with Sa around 150 nm had lower friction than references with Sa between 40 and 60 nm. These results show that conventional friction and roughness metrics can detect texture differences, but the evidence is limited by one human subject, a single 2 mm/s sliding velocity, no perceptual accuracy measure, and no instability classification, so it is less directly contradictory to the proposed human discrimination method.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to define the conventional baselines under exactly the same controlled conditions used for the instability metrics and to test them in the same GLMM framework: compute average friction coefficient from the stable sliding region, 3D roughness parameters such as Sa and Sq, contact angle hysteresis, and instability frequencies for each of the six silane coatings across the planned 5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s velocities and 0, 25, 75, and 100 g plus 6 g finger weights, then compare model fit using AIC or BIC, likelihood-ratio tests, effect sizes, and confidence intervals for participant accuracy and response time. This directly addresses the proposed method's comparison goal and avoids the main weakness in the related work, where conventional metrics were measured under different protocols from perception or instability analysis. The second most important action is to validate contact angle hysteresis as a meaningful baseline because none of the related studies measured it; measure forward and receding contact angles on the same vapor-deposited silane coatings, report hysteresis with uncertainty, and include it as a covariate to ensure that any advantage of instability metrics is not simply due to unmeasured surface energy or wetting effects. Third, adopt the human-variability controls highlighted by [(Zhang et al., 2017-06)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c2abc15c408b0b8ef82c77555f23af75aa64115e): record or constrain sliding velocity, normal load, skin hydration, cleaning and air-drying time, stroke length, and trial order, and use random participant and surface-pair effects in the GLMM; given the plan's 10 participants and 600 trials, add a power analysis and consider subjective confidence or 0 to 10 roughness/stickiness/comfort ratings to triangulate forced-choice accuracy. Fourth, incorporate the mechanical-protocol lessons from [(Sergachev et al., 2021-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/322f92233f72b50913c369245230611ac0f61cfd), including preparatory strokes, stable-sliding extraction, removal of drive-mechanism noise, sequential loading when finger recovery is slow, and reporting load-dependent friction coefficients rather than a single average value; the planned 550 Hz sampling should be sufficient to capture stiction spikes and slow frictional waves, but the analysis should specify how steady sliding, slow waves, and stiction spikes are thresholded and validated. Finally, retain the geometry and material descriptors from [(Tomlinson et al., 2011-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c8bfb3872e88c45c1acc1528be4c1d2df59315d9) as secondary baselines, such as ridge or asperity width, height, and material-specific adhesion/interlocking contributions if the minimal surfaces have any detectable microstructure, but because the plan uses roughness below human detection limits, the primary conventional comparison should remain average friction coefficient, areal roughness, and contact angle hysteresis; also document computational resources, code, and reproducibility details for instability classification and GLMM fitting.

## Context: To provide controlled stimuli that differ only in surface chemistry, Method: We will construct "minimal" tactile surfaces using vapor deposition of silanes onto silicon wafers.

### Support

As the only provided related work, it functions as an early proof-of-principle in an iterative sequence, and its strongest support for the proposed method is that [(Chaudhury et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/690276dcd975b048dbab78fc363f584466da7b2a) directly demonstrates that vapor deposition of decyltrichlorosilane onto a 0.29 mm thick 2-inch silicon wafer can create chemically distinct silicon regions without adding a separate bulk material, which is the central fabrication premise of the proposed minimal tactile surfaces. The paper reports hydrophobic regions with an average water contact angle of about 93 degrees and similar angles across four drops, indicating no significant wettability gradient within a hydrophobic stripe, while hydrophilic stripes confined 1 microliter droplets; this shows that silane chemistry can produce measurable, spatially resolved surface-chemistry contrasts on the same silicon substrate. A secondary but important support is that the protocol is sufficiently specified to be adapted: flame cleaning, a 0.12 mm transparency template with 3 mm hydrophilic and 9 mm hydrophobic stripes, a filter paper soaked with decyltrichlorosilane, and a 1 minute vapor exposure in a closed petri dish. These details provide a reproducible starting point for producing the six surface coatings needed in the research plan, especially if the same wafer thickness and cleaning steps are retained to keep bulk and thermal properties as similar as possible. The weakest support is more indirect: the paper shows that chemical patterning changes interfacial behavior, because droplets spread on hydrophilic stripes, remained discrete on hydrophobic stripes, and moved from hydrophobic to hydrophilic zones under steam and mist, suggesting that surface chemistry can modulate interfacial forces. However, this is evidence for wetting and condensation, not for the specific frictional instabilities, PDMS adhesion, or human tactile discrimination targeted by the plan.

### Contradictions

Because no newer work is provided to extend this early condensation protocol to friction, the strongest contradiction is that [(Chaudhury et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/690276dcd975b048dbab78fc363f584466da7b2a) provides no friction force, stick-slip, instability frequency, PDMS mock-finger, or human perception measurements, so it cannot support the plan's core claim that surface chemistry shifts boundaries between steady sliding, slow frictional waves, and stiction spikes. The next major contradiction is that the experimental conditions are thermal condensation conditions rather than controlled dry or humid sliding conditions: the wafer was mounted on a copper cylinder with steam above, 19 C water cooling the opposite face, four thermocouples measuring axial temperature gradients, and a regular camera recording condensation; these conditions introduce water films, temperature gradients, and coalescence-driven drop motion that are not part of the proposed 550 Hz friction traces at 5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s with 0, 25, 75, and 100 g plus 6 g finger weight. A further contradiction is that the related work uses a single silane, decyltrichlorosilane, and a striped 25% hydrophilic/75% hydrophobic pattern, whereas the proposed method requires six different surface coatings and surfaces whose differences are only surface chemistry with roughness below human detection limits; the paper does not report roughness, contact angle hysteresis, chemical uniformity across an unpatterned coating, or aging effects. The weakest contradiction is quantitative incompleteness: the paper reports heat flux consistency from copper temperature gradient and collected condensate mass but no single numerical heat-flux value, and it provides no statistical significance tests for wettability, drop velocity, or surface uniformity, limiting the strength of any inference that the same silane-vapor-deposition approach will yield repeatable tactile surfaces for the planned 600-trial human experiment.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to extend the [(Chaudhury et al., 2014-10)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/690276dcd975b048dbab78fc363f584466da7b2a) silane-vapor-deposition protocol into a direct mechanical validation by preparing the six proposed silane coatings on 0.29 mm 2-inch silicon wafers and testing them with the PDMS mock finger at the specified 16 velocity-mass combinations, 550 Hz sampling, and nine traces per condition, so that the claimed instability maps are generated rather than inferred from wetting behavior. The next priority is to characterize each coating for the variables that the plan will use as controls or baselines: surface roughness below human detection limits, static and dynamic contact angle, contact angle hysteresis, chemical uniformity, and stability over the time scale of mechanical and human testing, using at least the 1 microliter droplet method and, if possible, multiple droplet positions to detect gradients analogous to the four-drop check in the related work. A third action is to use the paper's striped template as a diagnostic rather than the final stimulus: create 3 mm and 9 mm stripe patterns or gradient wafers to verify that exposure time, silane concentration, and template contact produce uniform chemistry, and to map whether chemical transitions correlate with frictional instability transitions before using uniform coatings in the three-alternative forced-choice task. Finally, the experimental environment should be decoupled from the condensation setup by controlling temperature, humidity, and avoiding steam or water films during friction testing, while retaining the flame cleaning, petri-dish vapor exposure, and 1 minute exposure as a baseline only if replicate wafers show consistent contact-angle and friction outcomes.

## Context: To define the instability categories used in mechanical and human comparisons, Method: We will categorize friction traces into three broad phases based on competition between adhesion and finger elasticity: steady sliding (constant friction force with high-frequency oscillations), slow frictional waves (large oscillations at frequencies lower than microscopic stick-slip), and stiction spikes (singular large-magnitude events preceding smoother traces).

### Support

Strongest support for the proposed trace-categorization method comes from [(Skedung et al., 2018-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/475c09ef715548a6b38bdb18d1fd4d6811c6dbe1), which measured human index-finger friction on ten smooth glass coatings at 20 mm/s and 1.2 ± 0.15 N with a ForceBoard at 100 Hz and explicitly stated that 100 Hz was too low to reliably extract oscillation frequencies, while observed stick-slip frequencies appeared high enough to be detectable by Pacinian mechanoreceptors; this directly motivates the plan’s 550 Hz acquisition and the need to classify steady sliding, slow frictional waves, and stiction spikes rather than rely on average friction. The same study also supports the broader research plan because its fixed single-operator, single-speed, single-load design limits phase-map inference, and its perceptual dimension 1 was significantly related to water contact angle while correlation with friction coefficient was not statistically significant overall, suggesting that surface chemistry and instability-related features may be more informative than mean friction; its split analysis, in which contact angle explained hydrophobic samples #6-10 and friction coefficient was more important for hydrophilic samples #1-5, supports using silane coatings to shift instability boundaries and comparing instability metrics against average friction, roughness, and contact angle hysteresis. Next, [(Li et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad53062ed60bef45cadbaea85bd65a643d40cbab) supports the adhesion-versus-elasticity framing by fitting human finger friction with an adhesion plus deformation-resistance model, reporting an adhesion parameter of 3.6, deformation-resistance parameter of 0.04, and elasticity-related parameter of 0.52, with adhesion contributing about 83% at 5 N and deformation resistance becoming equal only near 25 N; because the proposed mock-finger loads are roughly 0.06-1.06 N including 6 g finger weight, this prior work suggests the planned low-load range is adhesion-dominated, consistent with the hypothesis that adhesion-elasticity competition can produce instabilities. Li et al. also used 2 and 50 mm/s, close to the proposed 5-45 mm/s range, and noted that higher-velocity skin friction can involve unsteady phenomena such as stick-slip, although they did not quantify phases, which supports the need for the proposed multi-velocity instability map. Weakest but still relevant support is from [(Peng et al., 2021-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6455a5ff6344a0a28c9e0b656d1d911d653bbfe), which analyzed lubricated microtextured hydrogel and striped PDMS contacts with a 1.27 cm PDMS sphere and Reynolds-based micro-EHL/macro-EHL scaling, showing that friction in soft elastic textured contacts can be decomposed into physical contributions linked to elasticity and geometry; this supports using a PDMS mock finger and physically grounded friction decomposition, but it is less direct because it did not classify time-resolved traces into steady sliding, slow waves, or stiction spikes.

### Contradictions

Strongest contradiction is that none of the related work directly validates the proposed three-phase taxonomy, and [(Skedung et al., 2018-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/475c09ef715548a6b38bdb18d1fd4d6811c6dbe1) provides the clearest warning that instability may not map cleanly onto simple surface parameters: its perceptual dimension 2 showed no systematic relationship with measured physical parameters, samples with the most stick-slip were spread across dimension 2, and only one high-friction, high-water-contact-angle sample was available, so the proposed assumption that surface chemistry will shift well-defined boundaries between steady sliding, slow frictional waves, and stiction spikes is not yet supported. Next, [(Li et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad53062ed60bef45cadbaea85bd65a643d40cbab) contradicts the expectation of strong velocity-driven phase boundaries by reporting only a slight decrease in friction when sliding velocity changed from 2 to 50 mm/s, and it did not identify or quantify distinct instability phases; moreover, its one-participant, sanitized-dried-finger design and two-velocity setup cannot establish the proposed pressure-velocity phase map, and its finding that deformation resistance equals adhesion only near 25 N suggests the proposed maximum load of about 1.04 N may be too low to strongly vary the adhesion-elasticity balance, potentially limiting the number of observable phase transitions. A further contradiction is that [(Peng et al., 2021-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6455a5ff6344a0a28c9e0b656d1d911d653bbfe) shows soft elastic textured contacts can be governed by lubrication film thickness, micro-EHL and macro-EHL forces, and surface geometry under glycerol-water lubrication, which challenges a categorization based only on dry adhesion versus finger elasticity if the mock finger or human finger has moisture, sweat, or surface lubrication; the excerpt also lacks tribometer sampling rate and trace-processing details, so it cannot support the proposed 550 Hz phase classification. Weaker contradictions include the limited generalizability of the prior human data: Skedung et al. used a single female experimenter, fixed 20 mm/s speed, 1.2 ± 0.15 N load, and 100 Hz acquisition, while Li et al. used one 26-year-old male participant and only 2 and 50 mm/s, so neither provides evidence that the proposed mock-finger phase categories will transfer to 10 human participants or to the planned 5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s velocities.

### Suggested Action

Most important, the method should define objective, reproducible signal-processing criteria for the three phases before testing, because [(Skedung et al., 2018-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/475c09ef715548a6b38bdb18d1fd4d6811c6dbe1) showed that 100 Hz was too low to extract oscillation frequencies and [(Li et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad53062ed60bef45cadbaea85bd65a643d40cbab) did not quantify instability phases; the plan should specify bandpass filters, amplitude thresholds, zero-crossing or wavelet frequency bands, event-detection rules for stiction spikes, and validation metrics such as inter-rater agreement, repeatability across the nine traces per condition, and comparison with average friction coefficient, roughness, and contact angle hysteresis. Since the proposed 550 Hz sampling has a 275 Hz Nyquist limit, the team should pilot whether the high-frequency oscillations defining steady sliding fall below 275 Hz or require higher-rate acquisition and anti-alias filtering, especially because Skedung et al. reported Pacinian-range frequencies. Next, the load range should be expanded or explicitly justified: [(Li et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad53062ed60bef45cadbaea85bd65a643d40cbab) found adhesion dominated at 5 N and deformation resistance only equal near 25 N, so the planned 0.06-1.06 N range may be too adhesion-dominated to reveal elasticity-driven transitions; adding higher loads, using mock fingers with different stiffness, or measuring indentation/contact area would make the adhesion-elasticity competition more testable. Third, hydration, lubrication, and contact mechanics should be controlled and measured, because [(Skedung et al., 2018-09)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/475c09ef715548a6b38bdb18d1fd4d6811c6dbe1) controlled skin hydration at 65-70 arbitrary units and 22 °C/45% RH, [(Li et al., 2024-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad53062ed60bef45cadbaea85bd65a643d40cbab) used sanitized dried fingers and 500 fps contact-area imaging, and [(Peng et al., 2021-04)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6455a5ff6344a0a28c9e0b656d1d911d653bbfe) showed lubrication can dominate soft-contact friction; the mock finger should therefore have standardized surface moisture, and the experiment should record contact area or film thickness where feasible. Finally, the surface set and statistical power should be strengthened: Skedung et al. used ten coatings, 150 samples per coating, six randomized series, and water contact angles from 0° to about 120°, whereas the proposed six coatings and nine traces per mass-velocity combination may be insufficient to map phase boundaries; the plan should increase trace count, randomize surface order, include repeated coatings, and use GLMM with surface, participant, velocity, load, and instability-difference random/fixed effects while reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals.

## Context: To capture repeated instability events for each surface and condition, Method: We will collect friction force traces at 550 Hz sampling rate, gathering nine traces per mass-velocity combination for each surface to observe multiple instability occurrences.

### Support

The strongest support for collecting repeated force traces across mass-velocity conditions comes from [(Viswanathan, and Sundaram, 2017-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb1e347ec77f475a86c8b034b73f577ea814ccaf), which used a Sylgard 184 PDMS slab, a plano-convex lens, a Kistler 9254 piezoelectric dynamometer, and simultaneous 100-5000 fps imaging to show that stick-slip was dominant from 0.05 to 10 mm/s and that force-trace oscillations could be assigned to Schallamach waves, slip pulses, and separation pulses, with unit slip times wave frequency matching remote velocity within 2-6%. This 2017 work extends the earlier controlled elastomer slider concept of [(Ronsin, and Coeyrehourcq, 2000-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad7a3b8b0adb57ae9e8c61bd349a9a43c1e78ce2) by adding in situ imaging and force-trace mode classification, and it directly supports the plan's core assumption that force traces from a soft mock finger can reveal distinct instability phases across velocity and normal load, although it used contact width 2a rather than applied mass and did not state a 550 Hz force sampling rate or nine replicates. The next strongest support is [(Juvekar, and Singh, 2024-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d7d7c26b9b7c7929fb09e2ca7c96e59fe4dd8ddd), which builds on the 2017 force-trace approach by showing that cycle-by-cycle extraction from repeated stress-time traces can quantify critical stress (259 ± 5 Pa), stick-phase slope, slip stress, stick interval (7.51-18.3 s), and slip velocity (702-1026 µm/s), and that a two-step model fit to 140-2000 µm/s steady-sliding data can predict critical velocity and stress; this supports using multiple traces per condition to build instability maps and later correlate instability frequencies with human perception, but it analyzed published gel-glass data rather than a 550 Hz PDMS mock-finger matrix. A weaker but still relevant support is [(Gao, and Wang, 2022-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2c49d57dd06c0fbe068552e9a37e9602566e9107), which used five replicates per load, 25 Hz force sampling, 70 fps DIC, and a 100 mm/min silicone-on-metal test to identify preliminary adjustment, deformation, stick-slip, and steady-state phases, with stick-slip becoming more pronounced as normal load increased from 0 to 0.03 N; this supports repeated trace collection and load-dependent instability, but its lower sampling, single velocity, and small load range provide less direct support for the proposed 550 Hz, nine-trace, 16 mass-velocity design. The weakest support is [(Ronsin, and Coeyrehourcq, 2000-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad7a3b8b0adb57ae9e8c61bd349a9a43c1e78ce2), which established a controlled elastomer slider-track apparatus with 1.3 µm rms roughness, a glass track to limit interdiffusion, and 0.1-750 Hz dynamic mechanical measurements, but reported no friction force traces, instability counts, or statistical comparisons, so it supports the mechanical context and viscoelastic characterization rather than the specific trace-acquisition protocol.

### Contradictions

The strongest tension is that the same soft adhesive sliding work that best supports force-trace instability classification, [(Viswanathan, and Sundaram, 2017-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb1e347ec77f475a86c8b034b73f577ea814ccaf), relied on simultaneous high-speed imaging at 100-5000 fps to resolve interface waves and showed that force oscillation frequency equaled waves per second; this implies that microscopic stick-slip, slip pulses, or high-frequency steady-sliding oscillations may occur faster than the proposed 550 Hz force-only sampling, especially at the plan's upper velocity of 45 mm/s, which exceeds the 10 mm/s upper bound tested in that study. A second tension comes from [(Gao, and Wang, 2022-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2c49d57dd06c0fbe068552e9a37e9602566e9107), which, although newer than the 2017 imaging study, observed multi-phase sliding and load-dependent stick-slip using only 25 Hz force sampling, five replicates per load, and a single 100 mm/min velocity, suggesting that 550 Hz and nine traces are not necessary for detecting macroscopic stick-slip, while also showing that jump amplitude decreased over time, so nine traces may not be sufficient to capture rare or time-dependent stiction spikes unless trace spacing and contact-age controls are specified. A third tension is that [(Juvekar, and Singh, 2024-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d7d7c26b9b7c7929fb09e2ca7c96e59fe4dd8ddd), the newest of the cited works, validated cycle-level metrics and model predictions using published gel-glass traces at 50-350 µm/s stick-slip velocities and 140-2000 µm/s steady sliding, with stick intervals of 7.51-18.3 s and slip velocities of 702-1026 µm/s, but did not establish that 550 Hz acquisition or nine replicates per mass-velocity condition are adequate for a 100 kPa PDMS mock finger at 5-45 mm/s, where cycle durations and slip speeds may be substantially different. The weakest contradiction is that [(Ronsin, and Coeyrehourcq, 2000-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad7a3b8b0adb57ae9e8c61bd349a9a43c1e78ce2) provides no friction trace outcomes, instability event counts, or statistical comparisons, and its 0.1-750 Hz dynamic mechanical range is not evidence that 550 Hz friction-force sampling will capture the relevant instability dynamics.

### Suggested Action

The most important improvement is to pilot-test the 550 Hz acquisition against a higher-rate force channel, ideally 1-10 kHz with a piezoelectric sensor comparable to the Kistler 9254 used in [(Viswanathan, and Sundaram, 2017-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb1e347ec77f475a86c8b034b73f577ea814ccaf), and optionally 1000-5000 fps imaging, at the highest planned velocity (45 mm/s) and highest mass to verify that 550 Hz does not alias high-frequency steady-sliding oscillations, slip pulses, or deformation waves; if aliasing is found, increase the sampling rate or add anti-alias filtering before final data collection. The second most important action is to make the nine-trace rule event-driven and statistically justified: use nine traces as a minimum, but add a stopping rule based on observed instability counts, report per-trace event frequencies with confidence intervals, randomize trace order, and include controlled rest intervals to account for the time-dependent jump-amplitude decay reported in [(Gao, and Wang, 2022-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2c49d57dd06c0fbe068552e9a37e9602566e9107). Third, calibrate applied mass to actual contact pressure and contact width, following the contact-width control in [(Viswanathan, and Sundaram, 2017-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb1e347ec77f475a86c8b034b73f577ea814ccaf) and the stiffness-normalized cycle analysis in [(Juvekar, and Singh, 2024-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d7d7c26b9b7c7929fb09e2ca7c96e59fe4dd8ddd), by measuring the PDMS mock finger modulus near 100 kPa, contact area, and normal force so that the 0, 25, 75, and 100 g mass levels produce a reproducible pressure range. Fourth, implement the cycle-level extraction used in [(Juvekar, and Singh, 2024-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d7d7c26b9b7c7929fb09e2ca7c96e59fe4dd8ddd) to compute critical stress, stick-phase slope, slip stress, stick interval, slip velocity, and instability frequency for each trace, and use a stick-slip model to predict phase boundaries before correlating them with human GLMM outcomes. Finally, incorporate the controlled surface and viscoelastic characterization practices from [(Ronsin, and Coeyrehourcq, 2000-05)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ad7a3b8b0adb57ae9e8c61bd349a9a43c1e78ce2), including reproducible roughness, track/surface chemistry controls, and DMA/WLF measurements, and use DIC or high-speed imaging as in [(Gao, and Wang, 2022-12)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2c49d57dd06c0fbe068552e9a37e9602566e9107) to validate that force-trace phases correspond to physical deformation waves or slip events.

## Context: To choose human test stimuli that span meaningful instability differences, Method: We will select six pairs of surfaces with varying ranges of instability differences based on mechanical testing results.

### Support

The strongest support for selecting six surface pairs from mechanical testing is that [(Bochereau et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0710910fe3b849426f987ec89e16bc83b030ba73) demonstrates that dynamic finger-glass contact can be quantified with a rigid tribometer, load cells, an electrodynamic voice-coil motor, a Foucault-current damper, and high-speed total internal reflection imaging, yielding interfacial shear stress values from 0 to 1.2 MPa under normal loads near 1 N. This is directly relevant to the plan's mock-finger mechanical testing because it shows that surface state and loading conditions can produce measurable differences in tangential traction and real contact area, which can be used to rank surfaces before human testing. A second, weaker support is that the same study found interfacial shear stress tended to decrease with loading rate while real contact area increment was relatively insensitive to loading rate, and moist fingers showed consistently lower values below 0.4 MPa than dry fingers; this supports the plan's premise that average friction-like metrics are insufficient and that instability-related, condition-dependent variables should drive surface-pair selection. A third, more indirect support is that the study used 2.0 s trials, three trials per condition, vibration frequencies of 10 to 1000 Hz, and amplitudes of a few millimetres, showing that repeated short dynamic trials can capture fluctuating friction and contact variables; this is conceptually aligned with the plan's nine traces per mass-velocity combination and 550 Hz force sampling, although the related work did not report the force sampling rate or classify instabilities.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that [(Bochereau et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0710910fe3b849426f987ec89e16bc83b030ba73) did not test six surface pairs, did not use silane-coated silicon wafers, and did not classify friction traces into steady sliding, slow frictional waves, or stiction spikes; it used a single glass plate and reported averaged interfacial shear stress, so it provides no direct evidence that selecting six pairs based on mechanical instability differences will produce human-discriminable stimuli. A second contradiction is that the experimental protocol was not matched to the plan's sliding parameter space: the plate was vibrated at 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 Hz with a few millimetres amplitude and absence of slip was visually confirmed, whereas the plan uses sliding velocities of 5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s with applied masses of 0, 25, 75, and 100 g plus 6 g finger weight; therefore the instability boundaries measured in the related work may not map onto the plan's velocity-pressure phase maps. A third contradiction is that the related work averaged tangential traction force and real contact area over 2.0 s and used only three trials per condition with one 27-year-old participant, reported no statistical significance or baseline comparisons, and is a single prior study rather than an iterative series of follow-up work, which can obscure transient stiction spikes and slow frictional waves and is insufficient to establish robust pair-selection criteria or generalizable human perception effects. A fourth contradiction is that the study used a real human finger with dry and moist tissue states, normal loads of 0.94 ± 0.60 N dry and 0.75 ± 0.30 N moist, and noted that finger ridges were less compliant than fully hydrated inner forearm stratum corneum at about 5 kPa; this highlights that a PDMS mock finger with 100 kPa stiffness and a rigid bone component may not reproduce the same contact mechanics, moisture sensitivity, or instability boundaries unless explicitly calibrated against human finger data. A weaker contradiction is that the related work's use of 10 to 1000 Hz vibration and high-speed imaging implies that high-frequency contact dynamics may be relevant, which could challenge the plan's 550 Hz sampling if those dynamics are present in the mock-finger traces.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to make the mechanical testing stage directly output the instability metrics used for pair selection: record force traces at 550 Hz or higher, segment each of the nine traces per mass-velocity condition into steady sliding, slow frictional waves, and stiction spikes, and compute event rates, durations, amplitudes, and phase-boundary locations for each of the six silane surfaces before choosing the six human-test pairs. The second priority is to add a contact-mechanics validation inspired by [(Bochereau et al., 2017-07)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0710910fe3b849426f987ec89e16bc83b030ba73), measuring real contact area or an equivalent proxy and interfacial shear stress under controlled normal load, using the final-minus-initial contact-area approach to reduce moisture-related error, and reporting load-cell variability because the related work showed substantial normal-load variability and moisture-dependent shear stress below 0.4 MPa for moist fingers. The third priority is to calibrate the PDMS mock finger against human finger dynamic friction and contact behavior, including stiffness, surface treatment, moisture, and temperature, and to test a subset of conditions at the related work's high-frequency vibration regime (10 to 1000 Hz, few millimetres amplitude) as a sanity check while retaining the plan's 5, 10, 25, and 45 mm/s sliding velocities. The fourth priority is to predefine statistical selection criteria for the six pairs, such as non-overlapping confidence intervals or effect sizes in instability-frequency differences across the 16 mass-velocity combinations, rather than selecting pairs from single averaged friction values; this will make the later GLMM comparison against average friction coefficient, roughness, and contact angle hysteresis more defensible. The fifth priority is to verify that 550 Hz sampling is adequate for the expected high-frequency oscillations associated with steady sliding; if the related work's 10 to 1000 Hz vibration and high-speed imaging suggest relevant dynamics above 275 Hz, increase the sampling rate or apply anti-alias filtering so that instability classification is not compromised.

## Context: To constrain human exploration to the same motion regime as the mock finger, Method: Participants will use only vertical downward motions with their dominant index fingers to mimic mechanical testing conditions.

### Support

The strongest indirect support for the proposed vertical-downward constraint comes from the passive finger-down protocol in [(Zhou et al., 2023-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fc0ee667ac306ac827c67058c1d3a58daa65558), where a participant's index finger was fixed in supports and tape while a PTFE specimen was driven at 10 mm/s against an imposed normal load of 1 ± 0.1 N; the normal force remained near 1 N without significant fluctuation, and friction and vibration signals showed continuous movement without obvious oscillations, yielding steady-region mean friction coefficients of 0.95, 0.88, and 0.64 for PTFE#1, PTFE#2, and PTFE#3. This directly supports the research plan's goal of constraining human exploration to a defined contact configuration, load, and speed so that human trials can be compared with mock-finger mechanical testing, because it demonstrates that a constrained human finger can produce stable, repeatable friction signals across surfaces, with an additional data set showing a similar FFT trend for PTFE#3. A secondary but important contrast is the active protocol, in which participant-controlled sliding averaged 10.06 ± 3.07 mm/s over about 60 mm and produced velocity oscillations, supporting the plan's premise that unconstrained human motion introduces variability that can obscure surface discrimination. The instrumentation details also provide weaker but useful feasibility support: a 2 kHz data acquisition system, a three-axis load cell with 100 N range and 5 mN resolution, and a contact microphone with 40 V/mm sensitivity were sufficient to record human finger friction and vibration under constrained conditions, and the passive FFT for PTFE#3 showed dominant components at 95.19 Hz and 508.08 Hz, indicating that constrained human sliding can contain high-frequency components relevant to the plan's instability classification, although the related work did not test the plan's participant-driven vertical downward motion, PDMS mock finger, or three-alternative forced choice task.

### Contradictions

The strongest contradiction is that neither protocol in [(Zhou et al., 2023-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fc0ee667ac306ac827c67058c1d3a58daa65558) used the proposed participant-driven vertical downward motion; the closest analogue was a device-driven passive finger-down setup in which the table imposed tangential sliding, so the evidence does not directly show that a participant can voluntarily generate vertical downward strokes that match the mock finger's velocity, normal load, and instability regime. A second contradiction is that the active, participant-driven protocol showed substantial velocity variability, averaging 10.06 ± 3.07 mm/s over about 60 mm, which implies that merely instructing participants to use vertical downward motions may not be sufficient to reproduce the mechanical testing conditions unless additional path, speed, or force constraints are imposed. A third contradiction is that the related work explored only a narrow slice of the plan's parameter space: one male participant, unspecified hand dominance, three trials per PTFE specimen, 1 ± 0.1 N normal load, 10 mm/s speed, and roughness-varying PTFE, whereas the plan requires 10 participants, dominant index fingers, six chemistry-only silane surfaces, 5/10/25/45 mm/s, and 0/25/75/100 g plus 6 g finger weight; this limits evidence for the full vertical-downward 3AFC protocol. Finally, the passive FFT for PTFE#3 showed a dominant component at 508.08 Hz, whereas the plan's mechanical and human force traces are specified at 550 Hz; because 508 Hz exceeds the 275 Hz Nyquist limit of a 550 Hz sampling rate, this contradicts the assumption that the same high-frequency instability components can be reliably identified in real-time human data without aliasing or higher sampling.

### Suggested Action

The most important action is to add a pilot validation of the exact proposed motion: have participants perform vertical downward strokes with their dominant index finger through a low-friction vertical guide or kinematic constraint while recording normal force, tangential force, and stroke velocity, and compare the resulting load, velocity, and instability signatures to the mock-finger apparatus at the plan's target conditions, especially 10 mm/s and approximately 1 N normal load as used in [(Zhou et al., 2023-03)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fc0ee667ac306ac827c67058c1d3a58daa65558). If voluntary vertical strokes remain too variable, the method should be revised to include real-time force or velocity feedback, a servo-assisted vertical actuator, or a device-driven passive control condition as a baseline. The second most important action is to increase the sampling rate for human force and vibration measurements to at least 2 kHz, matching the related work's 2 kHz acquisition and safely capturing the 508.08 Hz component observed in passive human finger sliding, rather than relying on the plan's 550 Hz rate for real-time instability identification. Third, expand the human protocol beyond a single-participant analogue by recruiting a larger pilot sample, recording hand dominance and skin condition, and collecting multiple trials per surface to estimate inter-trial and inter-participant variability before the 10-participant, 600-trial 3AFC experiment. Fourth, align the human surfaces with the plan's minimal chemistry-only silane coatings and calibrate the PDMS mock finger to human finger stiffness and stratum corneum treatment, so that instability maps are compared under the same surface and contact assumptions. Finally, in the GLMM analysis, include measured velocity, normal force, and stroke duration as covariates alongside instability frequencies, average friction coefficient, roughness, and contact angle hysteresis, to test whether the vertical downward constraint actually improves correlations relative to unconstrained exploration.
